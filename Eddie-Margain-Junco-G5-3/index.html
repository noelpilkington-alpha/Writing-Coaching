Question 3 Fix — Combining Sentences

Let’s review this together.

You scored 1/2 on this question — partial credit.

The question asked you to combine these two sentences into one:

“In the backyard, Kim unfolded the tent.”

“She sorted poles by size.”

Which sentence truly combines the two ideas into one?
A In the backyard, Kim unfolded the tent, then she sorted poles by size.
B In the backyard, Kim unfolded the tent and sorted the poles by size.
YOU WROTE
“In the backyard, Kim unfolded the tent, then she sorted poles by size.”
HOW TO TRULY COMBINE TWO SENTENCES
When two sentences have the same subject doing different things, drop the second subject and join the verbs:
• “Kim unfolded the tent” + “She sorted the poles” → “Kim unfolded the tent and sorted the poles.”

When the subjects are different, use a conjunction (and, but, so, because) with a comma:
• “Kim set up the tent, and Jaylen gathered firewood.”
Why “then she” does not count
When you write “Kim unfolded the tent, then she sorted poles,” you still have two separate clauses, each with its own subject and verb. That is technically a comma splice. To truly combine, either (1) drop the second subject: “Kim unfolded...and sorted...” or (2) use a conjunction: “Kim unfolded..., and then she sorted...”
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Sentence combining is a skill that shows up on every test. The key is recognizing whether the subjects are the same or different — that tells you exactly how to join the sentences.

AVOID WEAK SENTENCE STARTERS
These phrases add words without adding meaning:
• The thing that... → Start with the actual subject instead
• The way that... → Name the action directly
• What happened was... → Just say what happened

Quick test: If you can cross out the first few words and the sentence still makes sense, those words were not needed.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Wordy sentences are harder for the reader to follow, and grading feedback looks for clear, well-structured writing. A tighter sentence shows stronger control of language — which means more points.

Going beyond listing to explaining

A Jaylen might have felt sadness, despair, and many other feelings too.
B Jaylen probably felt disappointed and frustrated because the family had been looking forward to camping all week.
YOU WROTE

💡 Eddie, you chose strong vocabulary (“despair” is a great word). The issue was not what you said but that you stopped too early. Adding “because...” would have turned this into a full-credit answer.
THE “BECAUSE” RULE FOR EXPLAIN QUESTIONS
When a question says “explain,” you need two parts:
1. Name it: State the feeling, action, or idea.
2. Explain it: Add “because” and tell the reader WHY.

Your answer: “Jaylen might have felt sadness and despair...” (names it)
Full answer: “...because the family had been looking forward to their camping trip all week.” (explains it)
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Questions 6–10 are worth 3 points each. The difference between 2/3 and 3/3 is almost always the explanation. Adding “because” is the simplest way to turn a partial-credit answer into full credit.
