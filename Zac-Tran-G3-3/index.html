That is a clean sentence — but it does not have an appositive in it. You rewrote the sentence instead of adding the one piece the question wanted.

Which sentence actually contains an appositive?
A Sally swam out to go with the other seals.
B Sally, a baby seal, swam out to go with the other seals.
WHAT AN APPOSITIVE IS
An appositive is a short phrase that renames the noun right next to it, and it is set off with commas.

✓ Sally, a baby seal, swam out to go with the other seals.
✓ Anna, the girl who found the pup, kept it safe.

The phrase in commas tells you more about who or what the noun is. Take it out and the sentence still works — that is the test for an appositive.
The 3-Step Appositive
FIND Find the noun the question is about (for example, Sally).
RENAME Add a phrase that renames it: a baby seal.
COMMAS Set the phrase off with a comma before and after: Sally, a baby seal, swam...
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
When a question names a skill — "add an appositive" — the points come from doing that exact thing, not from writing a good sentence in general. Rewriting earned you 1 of 2. Dropping a renaming phrase in commas earns the full 2. That is a point you can win every time this type of question shows up.

YOUR IDEA, AS ONE CLEAN SENTENCE

THE RULE
Questions 6–10 ask for one complete sentence that gives your answer and a reason or example. The cleanest way to do that is to join them with because:

[your answer] because [your reason].

If you ever have two complete thoughts and a comma between them, that comma is doing a job it cannot do. Fix it one of two ways:
1. Use because (or so, but, and) to join them into one real sentence.
2. If they truly are two separate thoughts, end the first with a period and start the next with a capital letter.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
