Match the Conjunction to the Relationship

The one multiple-choice question (Q3) — a subtle precision point

Abi, you got 9 of 10 multiple-choice questions right — real mastery. The single one to look at (Q3) is a subtle one, not a weakness. It asked you to combine two ideas with the connector that matches their actual relationship. Here is the question:

Q3 — combine the ideas with a logical conjunction
IDEAS "Animated movies are fun. They also include valuable lessons."
YOU CHOSE "Animated movies are fun because they include valuable lessons."
CORRECT "Animated movies are fun but they include valuable lessons."
WHY "BECAUSE" DOES NOT FIT
"Because" claims a cause-and-effect link: it would mean the movies are fun because of the lessons. But that is not what the two ideas say. They are simply two things that are both true — one is added to the other, with a slight contrast. The connector has to match the real relationship: here the second idea is an additional, slightly contrasting point, so "but" (or "and") fits, not "because".

The rule is small but powerful: only use "because" when one idea truly causes the other. If the two ideas are just both true, or the second one adds to or slightly contrasts the first, reach for "but" or "and" instead.

Two ideas: "The hike was long. The view at the top was worth it." There is no cause-and-effect here. Which combination matches the real relationship?
A "The hike was long, but the view at the top was worth it."
B "The hike was long because the view at the top was worth it."
REMEMBER
Before you pick a connector, ask: does one idea actually cause the other? If yes, "because" works. If they are just both true, or one adds to or contrasts the other, use "and" or "but".

Polish: Keep Your Lists Parallel

When you list three things, make them all the same kind of word

Abi, your thesis does the most important job perfectly — it previews your three points: bravery, teamwork, and honesty. The only polish is keeping the three items in the list the same kind of word. Two are nouns, but the third switched to a verb phrase. Here is your real thesis:

YOUR THESIS
"Animated movies can teach kids valuable life lessons by showing kids bravery, teamwork, and be honest."

See it? "Bravery" and "teamwork" are nouns, but "be honest" is a verb phrase. When the list mixes word types, the sentence reads slightly off. The fix is to make all three nouns:

FIXED — ALL NOUNS
"Animated movies can teach kids valuable life lessons by showing kids bravery, teamwork, and honesty."
THE PARALLEL RULE
When you list items in a sentence, keep them the same form: all nouns (bravery, teamwork, honesty) or all verbs (being brave, working together, telling the truth). Do not mix nouns and verb phrases in the same list. This is a small edit, but it is exactly the kind of precision that lifts the "strong sentences" score.
Which list keeps all three items the same kind of word (parallel)?
A "The movie teaches bravery, teamwork, and honesty."
B "The movie teaches bravery, teamwork, and be honest."
YOUR ACTION STEP
Whenever you write a list of three, read just the listed words back to back: "bravery... teamwork... honesty." If one sounds like a different kind of word, reshape it to match the others. One quick check makes every list parallel.
