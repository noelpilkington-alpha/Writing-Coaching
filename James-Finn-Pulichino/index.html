Fix Run-On Sentences

Q6 — A comma splice costs you points

A comma splice happens when you join two complete sentences with just a comma. You did this on Q6:

YOU WROTE
Once, I got in trouble on the swings for pushing a kid, I admitted to the wrong doing, and I was forgiven.

The first part ("Once, I got in trouble on the swings for pushing a kid") is a complete sentence. The second part ("I admitted to the wrongdoing, and I was forgiven") is also complete. You can't join them with just a comma — that's a comma splice.

Also note: "wrong doing" should be one word: "wrongdoing."

Tap to see the fixed versions
Read the explanation above first
FIX 1 — USE A PERIOD
Once, I got in trouble on the swings for pushing a kid. I admitted to the wrongdoing, and I was forgiven.
FIX 2 — USE A SEMICOLON
Once, I got in trouble on the swings for pushing a kid; I admitted to the wrongdoing, and I was forgiven.
THE COMMA SPLICE FIX
A comma alone can never join two complete sentences. You have three choices:

1. Use a period and start a new sentence.
2. Use a semicolon (;) if the ideas are closely related.
3. Add a conjunction: , and / , but / , so

Quick test: Cover the comma. Can each side stand alone as a sentence? If yes, you need more than a comma.
See more examples
Comma splice: I studied all night, I aced the test.
Fixed (period): I studied all night. I aced the test.
Fixed (conjunction): I studied all night, and I aced the test.
Fixed (semicolon): I studied all night; I aced the test.

SPELLING RULES TO REMEMBER
"admitting" — When a word ends in a single consonant after a single vowel, double the consonant before adding -ing. (admit → admitting, run → running, sit → sitting)

"it's" vs. "its" — It's = "it is" or "it has." Its = belonging to it. Try replacing with "it is" — if it works, use the apostrophe.

"replace it with" — You replace something with something else, not "for."
PROOFREADING TIP
After you finish writing, read your answer backward — start with the last word and work toward the first. This forces your brain to look at each word individually instead of skimming over familiar patterns. It's the fastest way to catch spelling errors.

Build Your Paragraph

Q11 — Strong structure, but match the prompt

Your paragraph scored 16/20. You had a hook, evidence, and a conclusion — that's solid structure. But the topic drifted from what the prompt asked.

YOUR PARAGRAPH
Did you know horses are actually really smart animals? In recent studies, horses have been shown to react to emotions. We knew that they could communicate with other horses, but they can also undersand human emotions. Horses can also be trained to say "yes" or "no" to certain lifestyle changes, such as if they want a blanket when its cold. In conclusion, horses are much smarter animals than we could have imagined. From emotions to personal preferences, they are truly smart animals.

The prompt asks: Explain what new research reveals about horses' ability to communicate.

Your topic sentence says horses are "really smart animals" — but the prompt is about communication, not just being smart. When the topic sentence doesn't match the prompt, the whole paragraph drifts.

BETTER TOPIC SENTENCE
New research shows that horses can communicate in surprising ways.

See how the key word "communicate" matches the prompt? Now every detail in the paragraph supports that idea.

Here's the G5 paragraph plan you should follow:

Paragraph Plan — TS → D1 → W → D2 → W → D3 → W → CS
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail from the passage  •  W = Why it matters  •  CS = Closing Sentence
TS New research shows that horses can communicate in surprising ways.
D1 Horses have been shown to react to human emotions.
W This shows they can read and respond to how people feel, which is a form of communication.
D2 Horses can also be trained to say "yes" or "no" to lifestyle changes, like wanting a blanket.
W This means horses can express personal preferences, not just follow commands.
D3 Researchers found that horses already communicate with other horses and can extend that to humans.
W This shows their communication skills are more advanced than scientists once thought.
CS From reading emotions to expressing preferences, horses communicate far more than we ever imagined.

Notice the closing sentence wraps up the idea in a fresh way — it doesn't just repeat "smart animals." Your conclusion repeated the same phrase twice. A strong closing sentence echoes the main idea with new words.

TWO KEY FIXES FOR YOUR PARAGRAPH
1. Match your topic sentence to the prompt. Circle the key word in the prompt (here, "communicate") and make sure that word appears in your topic sentence.

2. Don't repeat your closing sentence. Your conclusion said "smart animals" twice. Instead, restate your main idea using different words or summarize your strongest evidence.
