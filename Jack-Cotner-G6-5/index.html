Question 11 — Spelling & Grammar

Errors that cost you points

Jack, your ideas are strong, but there are spelling and grammar errors that are easy to fix. Here are the ones to watch for:

SPELLING ERRORS

1. "full of trash and sewege"

2. "working with sciencetists"

3. "was mappingo ut the dirtiest parts"

CORRECTED

1. "full of trash and sewage"

2. "working with scientists"

3. "was mapping out the dirtiest parts" (two words, not one)

GRAMMAR ERROR

"the river began look and smell better"

CORRECTED

"the river began to look and smell better"

The word "began" needs "to" before the verb that follows it: began to look, began to smell.

PROOFREADING TIP
Before you submit, read your essay out loud. When something sounds off (like "began look"), stop and check if a small word is missing. Also watch for words that run together ("mappingo ut") — reading slowly helps you catch these. On this essay, fixing these four errors would have earned you a cleaner score.

Question 11 — Strengthen Your Evidence

How to turn good evidence into great evidence

You used facts from the passage — that is good. But none of your body paragraphs include specific evidence (a direct quote or a close paraphrase with details). Using specific details from the passage and explaining what they show is how you prove your points. There are also two structural issues to fix.

BODY 2 — TOO SHORT (2 SENTENCES)

"Then, Earth5R worked with scientists to plant grasses along the banks. These grasses would help clean the water and stop soil from washing away."

This paragraph has a topic sentence and one fact, but no explanation of why planting grasses matters to the overall restoration. It needs at least one more sentence.

BODY 2 EXPANDED WITH SPECIFIC EVIDENCE

"Next, Earth5R worked with scientists to plant grasses along the banks. According to the passage, 'the grasses filtered dirty water and stopped soil from washing away.' This was an important step because cleaning the water naturally meant the river could stay healthy even after the volunteers left. It also showed that teamwork between scientists and volunteers made the project stronger than either group could have done alone."

CONCLUSION — TOO GENERIC

"In conclusion, Earth5R showed that even if something seems impossible, if you put your mind to it, you will be able to complete the task."

This conclusion could be about any topic. It doesn't mention the Mithi River, teamwork, or the community — the three key ideas from the prompt.

A STRONGER CONCLUSION

"In conclusion, the restoration of the Mithi River shows that teamwork can solve problems that seem impossible. By mapping pollution, organizing cleanup events, and working with scientists, Earth5R and the community transformed a dirty, forgotten river into a place people enjoy visiting again. This experience taught the community that when people work together, even small actions can add up to make a real difference."

THE EVIDENCE SANDWICH
Every piece of evidence should follow this pattern:

1. Set it up: Write your topic sentence that introduces the point.
2. Use specific details from the passage (quoted or paraphrased): Pull a key fact, detail, or example from the passage. You can quote the author's exact words in quotation marks, or you can paraphrase in your own words — either way, be specific. Introduce it with "The passage states," "According to the article," or "The author explains."
3. Explain it: Tell the reader why this evidence matters. Don't just restate your thesis — explain what makes this specific example meaningful.

You need at least two pieces of specific evidence across your essay to earn full credit.
SO WHAT?
Your essay structure is already strong — five paragraphs with clear organization. The difference between 16/20 and 20/20 is: (1) fixing spelling and grammar errors, (2) expanding Body 2 with more detail, (3) adding specific evidence from the passage (quoted or paraphrased) and explaining what it shows, and (4) writing a conclusion that ties back to the specific topic. All four are within reach for you.
