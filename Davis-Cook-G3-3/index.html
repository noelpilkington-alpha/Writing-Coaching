Question 3 Fix — Writing Appositives

Let's review this together.

You scored 1/2 on this question — you lost 1 point.

The question asked you to rewrite a sentence by adding an appositive — a short phrase set off by commas that gives extra information about a noun.

Original: "Sally swam to the other seals."

Which sentence correctly adds an appositive?
A Sally swam all the way to the other seals.
B Sally, a tiny gray seal pup, swam to the other seals.
YOU WROTE
"Sally swam all the way to the other seals."

Your sentence is a fine sentence, but it doesn't include an appositive. You added extra detail ("all the way"), which shows good instincts — but the question specifically asked for an appositive phrase set off by commas.

HOW TO WRITE AN APPOSITIVE
An appositive is a short phrase right after a noun that tells us more about it. It is always set off by commas:
• Step 1: Pick the noun you want to describe (Sally).
• Step 2: Think of a short phrase that identifies or describes that noun (a tiny gray seal pup).
• Step 3: Put it right after the noun with commas on both sides: Sally, a tiny gray seal pup, swam to the other seals.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Appositive questions appear on every writing test and are worth 2 points. The key is to look for the commas-on-both-sides pattern. Once you learn this pattern, it becomes easy points every time.

Questions 2 & 4 Fix — Adding Details and Combining Sentences

Let's review these together.

You scored 1/2 on Question 2 and 1/2 on Question 4 — you lost 2 points total.

Question 2 asked you to add details to a short sentence (called kernel expansion). The original sentence: "Dad freed the pup."

Which sentence correctly adds details to "Dad freed the pup"?
A Do you know where dad freed the pup.
B That morning, Dad gently freed the small pup from the tangled fishing net.
YOU WROTE (QUESTION 2)
"Do you know where dad freed the pup."

You changed the sentence into a question instead of adding details to it. Kernel expansion means keeping the sentence as a statement and making it more interesting by adding words that tell where, when, how, or why.

YOU WROTE (QUESTION 4)
You used "next" to combine two sentences, but "next" is a time word, not a conjunction that joins two sentences together.
KERNEL EXPANSION — HOW TO ADD DETAILS
Start with the original sentence and ask yourself:
• When? (that morning, after lunch, suddenly)
• Where? (on the beach, in the bay, near the rocks)
• How? (gently, carefully, quickly)
• Why? (because the pup was tangled)
Add one or more of these details without changing the sentence into a question.
COMBINING SENTENCES — USE A REAL CONJUNCTION
To combine two sentences, use a joining word like:
• and = adds information
• but = shows a contrast
• so = shows a result
• because = explains why
Words like "next" or "then" tell time order but do not combine sentences.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
These two questions are each worth 2 points. Understanding the difference between adding details (keep it a statement) and combining sentences (use a conjunction) earns you 4 easy points.

MISSING SUBJECT After "because," you left out the word "he" — the sentence needs a subject to be complete. Example: "...because wanted to..." should be "...because he wanted to..."

HOW TO FIX A RUN-ON
The Period Test: Read your paragraph out loud. Every time you pause to take a breath, that is probably where a period should go. Each complete idea gets its own sentence.

Example:
Run-on: "Satoshi liked bugs and then he made a game because wanted to share his love of catching them"
Fixed: "Satoshi liked catching bugs as a child. Later, he created a video game because he wanted to share his love of catching creatures with others."

Question 11 — Stronger Writing

How to push your paragraph score higher
Which paragraph uses evidence better?
A Satoshi made Pokémon. He liked bugs. The game is popular.
B Satoshi's childhood hobby inspired his greatest creation. The passage explains that he "spent hours catching bugs in the fields near his home." This experience gave him the idea to create a game where players could catch and collect creatures of their own.

Once you fix the run-on, the next step is to make your evidence stronger. Here is the pattern:

THE EVIDENCE SANDWICH
1. Set it up: Write a sentence that introduces your evidence.
2. Evidence: Use specific details from the passage (you can quote or paraphrase — either works).
3. Explain: Tell the reader why this evidence matters or what it shows.
EXAMPLE USING YOUR TOPIC

Set up: "Satoshi's childhood hobby inspired his greatest creation."
Evidence: "The passage explains that he spent hours catching bugs in the fields near his home."
Explain: "This experience gave him the idea to create a game where players could catch and collect creatures of their own."

ALSO: CHECK EVERY SENTENCE FOR A SUBJECT
Every sentence needs a subject — the person or thing doing the action. After words like "because," "since," or "when," make sure you include who is doing the action. Read each sentence and ask: "Who is doing this?"
SO WHAT?
Fixing the run-on earns back the most points. Adding the Evidence Sandwich pattern and checking for subjects will push you even higher. These three fixes together could move your paragraph from 13 to 17 or more.

TRICKY PAST TENSE WORDS
Some past tense words do not follow the usual rules:
• choose → chose (not "choosed")
• swim → swam (not "swimmed")
• run → ran (not "runned")
When you write about something that already happened, double-check these tricky words.
HOW TO CATCH SPELLING ERRORS
After you finish writing, read your answer one more time slowly. Point at each word with your finger or pencil. If a word looks wrong, try spelling it a different way on scratch paper.
