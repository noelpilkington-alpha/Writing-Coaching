The "This Shows..." Move

The fastest way to push a G4 paragraph from 14/20 to 18/20

Ian, every one of your body points follows this pattern:

TOPIC + REASON + DONE.
"Recess is important because without it students can't get their energy out. That means students can't focus in class because they are too energetic."

That is two sentences of the same idea. To push to 18+/20, every evidence sentence needs a follow-up that says what that evidence shows about the bigger picture. It is the move that turns your writing from "stating things" into "explaining things."

The 5-Sentence Body Paragraph (G4 Target)
TOPIC State the body paragraph's main point
EVIDENCE "According to the article..." — one specific fact
SHOWS "This shows..." — what the fact means
EVIDENCE A second fact OR an example
SHOWS "This shows..." — what the second fact means
Which body sentence pair would earn more points?
A Recess helps students. Students who go to recess feel better.
B The article says recess gives students a chance to be active and play. This shows that recess is not just for fun — it is part of how kids learn to focus and work with others.
TRY IT: ONE EVIDENCE + ONE "SHOWS"
Before your next test, memorize this move:

Step 1: Write a topic sentence.
Step 2: Write one sentence that starts "The article explains..." or "According to the passage..."
Step 3: Write one sentence that starts "This shows..." or "This means..."
Step 4: Repeat with a second fact.

That gives you 5 sentences per body paragraph, not 2. And it hits every rubric category.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Your G4.7 Q11 is 80 words with 2 body points. A full-credit G4 paragraph is 120–150 words with 3 body points and "this shows" after each one. Adding the "this shows" sentences is worth 2–4 more points per test. You do not need to write more about different things — you need to say more about each thing.

Small Grammar Slips

Verb forms, plurals, and singular/plural agreement

Ian, when you hit the easy questions (Q1–Q5 on G4.7 you got 5 out of 5, huge win) your grammar is strong. The slips happen on your own-words answers — the short-answer questions and the paragraph. Here are the real examples:

SLIP TYPE 1 — "MAKED" (VERB TENSE)
From G4.6 Q8: "Garrett Morgan's traffic signals maked streets safer..."
Should be: made. "Make" is an irregular verb — it does NOT add "-ed."
SLIP TYPE 2 — SINGULAR/PLURAL MATCH
From G4.7 Q1: "Cars stop, go, and wait their turn and these signal allow people to cross streets more safely."
Should be: these signals allow. "These" is plural, so the noun needs an "s."
SLIP TYPE 3 — APOSTROPHE MIX-UP
From G4.7 Q11: "without recess, the student's won't be learning as much..."
Should be: students (more than one). "Student's" with an apostrophe means belonging to one student.
THE THREE QUICK RULES
1. Irregular verbs stay short: made (not maked), built (not builded), ran (not runned), taught (not teached), thought (not thinked).

2. "These" and "those" = plural: If you say "these," the noun right after needs an "s." These signals, these books, these kids.

3. Apostrophe = "belongs to":
• students = more than one student
• student's = belonging to one student
• students' = belonging to more than one student
Which sentence is grammatically correct?
A The teacher maked the rules, and the student's desks were in a row.
B The teacher made the rules, and the students' desks were in a row.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
On Q11 the rubric checks "correct spelling, capitalization, punctuation, and grammar" as a full criterion. Verb form and agreement errors cost points there. They also show up in Q6–Q10 short answers where you were scoring 0.67 partial credit instead of 1.0. Clean grammar = 1–2 points back per test.

MODEL POLISHED PARAGRAPH (18–19/20 level)

Recess is important for students and should be part of every school day. First, recess gives students a break from learning.
