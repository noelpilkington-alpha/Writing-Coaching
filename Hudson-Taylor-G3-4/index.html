Question 1 Fix — Stronger Verbs

Let’s review this together.

You scored 1/2 on this question.

The question asked:

“Rewrite the sentence below by changing the verb to make the action stronger or more descriptive: Layla looked at the flower.”

🔎 Key word: The question says “changing the verb.” That means the verb itself — “looked” — needs to become a different word. Adding extra words around the verb isn’t the same as changing it.
Which sentence actually changes the verb?
A Layla quickly looked at the pretty flower.
B Layla gazed at the flower.
YOU WROTE
“Layla quickly looked at the pretty flower.”

You added the adverb “quickly” and the adjective “pretty,” but the verb is still “looked.” The question wanted you to swap out “looked” for a stronger verb.

STRONGER VERSION
“Layla gazed at the flower.” or “Layla admired the flower.”
HOW TO PICK A STRONGER VERB
Think about how the character is doing the action. “Looked” is general. Ask yourself: did she look quickly? (“glanced”) Carefully? (“examined”) With wonder? (“marveled”) Pick the verb that paints the best picture.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Strong verbs make your writing more vivid. This same skill — choosing precise verbs — is what makes your essay sentences come alive instead of sounding flat.

Question 6 Fix — Answering What the Question Asks

Let’s review this together.

You scored 2/3 on this question.

The question asked:

“What do Layla’s actions tell us about her character? Explain in one sentence.”

🔎 Character means what kind of person someone is — like honest, curious, or responsible. It’s different from listing what they did.
Which answer tells us about Layla's character (what kind of person she is)?
A She likes butterflies, because she takes pictures of them.
B Layla is curious because she stops to examine a butterfly and photographs it instead of just walking past.
YOU WROTE
“She likes butterflies, because she takes a pictures of them.”

Your sentence describes Layla’s actions, but the question asks about her character — what kind of person she is. The fix is to name a trait (like “curious” or “observant”) and then explain it.

STRONGER VERSION
“Layla is curious because she stops to look at the butterfly and take a photo instead of walking past it.”
THE “WHAT KIND OF PERSON?” TEST
When a question asks about someone’s character, ask yourself: “What kind of person does this action show?” Your answer should include a describing word (curious, brave, kind, honest) plus evidence from the story.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?

Question 11 — Your Paragraph

Sports and Society — 13/20 points
Which central idea sentence is stronger for an essay about how sports developed?
A Sports have evolved over the years.
B Over time, sports changed from activities only wealthy people enjoyed to games that everyone could play.
Developing: 13/20 10 sentences Fragment spotted

Hudson, you had good ideas in your paragraph. You knew the key facts from the passage — that sports started with wealthy people and spread to everyone. Let’s look at how each sentence works.

TOPIC (too vague — needs a central idea) sports have evolved over the years.

DETAIL 1 They started with only rich people doing sports.

DETAIL 2 They did it because they had a lot of free time.

DETAIL 3 They mainly played golf and cricket.

DETAIL 4 Then midlle class people started playing sports.

DETAIL 5 They started to have teams repersent towns.

DETAIL 6 After that schools started having sports.

DETAIL 7 Finally, other sports were made.

FRAGMENT Like baseball, basketball, football, hockey, and many more.

CLOSING That is the history of how sports evolved over the years.
WHAT YOU DID WELL
You told the story in order: wealthy people → middle class → schools → more sports. You used transition words like “Then,” “After that,” and “Finally.” That shows you understand how to organize a paragraph.
THREE THINGS TO IMPROVE
1. Your topic sentence needs a central idea. “Sports have evolved” is too vague. Try: “Over time, sports changed from activities only wealthy people enjoyed to games that everyone could play.”

2. Fix the fragment. “Like baseball, basketball...” is not a complete sentence. Attach it to the sentence before: “Finally, other sports were made, like baseball, basketball, football, hockey, and many more.”

3. Use specific evidence from the passage. Instead of just listing facts, use details from the text (quoted or paraphrased) and explain what they show. For example: “According to the passage, wealthy people ‘had the money and free time to play sports.’ This shows that sports started as something only a few people could do.”

Question 11 — Stronger Writing

How to push from 13/20 toward 18–20/20
Which paragraph uses evidence from the passage better?
A They started with only rich people doing sports. They did it because they had a lot of free time. They mainly played golf and cricket.
B The passage explains that wealthy people “had the money and free time to play sports.” This shows that at first, only rich people could enjoy sports because most other people were too busy working.

Your paragraph has good ideas, but it reads more like a list of facts. To score higher, you need to use specific evidence from the passage and explain why it matters.

THE “THEY” PATTERN

Your paragraph uses “They” to start 5 out of 10 sentences. When too many sentences start the same way, the writing feels repetitive. Try starting sentences with the actual subject (wealthy people, middle class families, schools) or with a transition word.

THE EVIDENCE SANDWICH
1. Set it up: Write a sentence that introduces your evidence.
2. Evidence: Use specific details from the passage (quoted or paraphrased).
3. Explain: Tell the reader why this evidence matters or what it shows.
THE 30-SECOND PLANNING TRICK
Before you start writing, jot down 3 things:
• Central idea: What is the main point of my paragraph?
• 2–3 pieces of evidence: What details from the passage support my point?
• Closing: How will I wrap up?
This takes 30 seconds and makes your paragraph much stronger.
