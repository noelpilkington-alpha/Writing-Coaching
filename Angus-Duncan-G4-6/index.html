YOU WROTE (Q3)
"Garrett Morgan idea, an early traffic signal, helped save lives and reduce crashes."
Your appositive is perfect — an early traffic signal — and the combine reads smoothly. The one missing mark is the possessive: Morgan's idea (the idea belonging to Morgan). That apostrophe + s is what a grader is checking for.
THREE QUICK RULES
Possessive apostrophe: when something belongs to a person, add 's. Morgan's idea. Angus's answer. The school's bell.

Hyphen in a compound noun: when two words work as one name for a thing, hyphenate them. problem-solvers, cost-effective, well-organized.

Every day vs. everyday: every day = each day (an adverb). everyday = common, normal (an adjective). “I see it every day” vs. “everyday problems.”
Which version is ready to submit?
A Morgan idea helped problem solvers in every community.
B Morgan's idea helped problem-solvers in every community.
YOUR 3-MINUTE READ-BACK PASS
Check 1 — Possessives. Scan for any name or noun followed by another noun. Ask: does this thing belong to the first one? If yes, it needs 's. Morgan's idea, the community's needs, a family's dinner.

Check 2 — Compound nouns. Look for two-word phrases acting as a single label: problem-solvers, cost-effective, well-organized. These want a hyphen.

Check 3 — Small words. Spell-check each word letter by letter: o-c-c-u-r-s, not o-c-c-u-r-r-e-s. Say the word in your head first; your ear knows the rhythm.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q3 came in at 0.5/1 because of that one missing apostrophe. Q6 lost 0.33 because of the spelling slip. Q11 lost 1–2 points in conventions largely from the missing hyphens and compound-word fixes. Those small marks, added up, are the full 2-point gap to 90%+.

The Comma Splice in Your Paragraph (Q11)

Two full sentences joined with a comma need a stronger connector

Angus, there is one spot in your Q11 paragraph where two complete sentences got joined with just a comma. A grader calls this a comma splice, and it is one of the most common Q11 conventions deductions. Your idea is strong — the fix is about how the two parts connect.

YOU WROTE (Q11)
"Finally, a big way they are important is with safety, they come up with solutions to keep the community safe."
Two complete sentences here. Sentence 1: “Finally, a big way they are important is with safety.” Sentence 2: “They come up with solutions to keep the community safe.” Each one is a full thought — which means a comma alone can't connect them.
THE RULE
A comma is not strong enough to connect two complete sentences by itself. When you have two full thoughts, you have three clean options:

Option 1 — Use a period. Make them two separate sentences.
“...important is with safety. They come up with solutions...”

Option 2 — Keep the comma, add a conjunction. (because, and, so, but)
“...important is with safety, because they come up with solutions...”

Option 3 — Rewrite as one flowing sentence.
“Finally, they keep the community safe by coming up with safety solutions.”
ANGUS'S IDEA, PUNCTUATION FIXED
"Finally, a big way they are important is with safety. They come up with solutions to keep the community safe."

Same meaning, same evidence, same word count — just a period where the comma was. That single change is one rubric point on Q11.
Which sentence is punctuated correctly?
A Inventors help communities, they solve big problems.
B Inventors help communities. They solve big problems.
C Inventors help communities, also they solve big problems.
THE PROOFREAD MOVE
On your read-back pass, when you hit a comma, pause and ask: is what comes before the comma a full sentence? Is what comes after also a full sentence? If the answer is yes to both, the comma alone is not enough. Change it to a period, or add a conjunction after the comma.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q11 came in at 17/20. Comma splices are a convention error the grader calls out specifically on the Q11 rubric — one fix is worth about a full point. Combined with the hyphen and compound-word fixes from Card 3, your paragraph lands at 19–20 instead of 17.
