Q3 Fix — What Is an Appositive?

Can you spot the difference? Then we'll break it down.
📈 You've missed Writing Appositives questions on 3 of 4 tests — but you got it right on G4.3 ("He asked his mom, a nice lady, for some lettuce"), which proves you CAN do this. The pattern each time: adding adjectives instead of a noun phrase. Appositives are the same skill that makes your sentences more detailed in paragraphs.

Q3 asked you to add an appositive to the sentence "Music can help you learn too." You added adjectives instead:

Which version correctly adds an appositive?
A "Calm relaxing music can help you learn too."
B "Music, a powerful tool for the brain, can help you learn too."

Here's what you wrote vs. what was needed:

YOU WROTE
Calm relaxing music can help you lern too.
FIXED WITH AN APPOSITIVE
Music, a powerful tool for the brain, can help you learn too.

"Calm relaxing" are adjectives — they describe the music but they don't rename it. An appositive is a noun phrase that renames or explains the noun next to it, set off by commas.

APPOSITIVE RULE
An appositive is a noun phrase, set off by commas, that renames the noun beside it.

Pattern: Noun , appositive phrase, rest of sentence.
Example: Seattle, the largest city in Washington, gets a lot of rain.

Quick test: Can you replace the noun with the appositive phrase? If yes, it works!
Why does this matter for your paragraphs?
Appositives let you pack extra information into a single sentence without making it a run-on. In your paragraph writing, appositives help you add details about people, places, and things — which makes your evidence more specific and convincing. Instead of "Cheval built a palace," you could write "Cheval, a French mailman with a big dream, built a palace."

Q5 Fix — Adding Details to Sentences

Can you spot the problem? Then we'll show you the fix.
📈 Adding Details to Sentences is your biggest growth area at 50%. When you expand a sentence, the goal is to add real, specific information — not just restate what's already there. This is the exact same skill that makes body paragraphs convincing.

Q5 asked you to expand "Make time for music every day" by adding how you could make time. Which expansion actually adds useful detail?

Tap the one that adds real, specific information:
A "I could take 30 hours before I go to bed to listen to music."
B "I could take 30 minutes before bed to listen to music every day, turning it into a calming routine."

Here's what you wrote:

YOU WROTE
I could take 30 hours before I go to bed to listen to music.

30 hours is more than a full day! You probably meant 30 minutes. This kind of mistake is easy to catch if you re-read your answer one more time before moving on.

FIXED
I could take 30 minutes before bed to listen to music every day, turning it into a calming routine.
THE EXPANSION CHECKLIST
When you need to add detail to a short sentence, run through: WHERE? WHEN? HOW? WHY?
If your longer version doesn't answer at least one of these, you've just said the same thing in different words. Real details add new information the reader didn't already have.
So what? Why does this matter beyond this question?
Adding Details to Sentences is the same skill you use when writing evidence in your paragraphs. When you explain how Cheval showed grit, you need to add specifics like "he spent 33 years collecting rocks on his mail route." Without those details, your evidence is just a vague claim. Specific details = stronger paragraphs.

COMBINING SENTENCES RULE
When you combine two sentences, make sure you keep all the important information from both. Read the combined version and ask: "Did I lose anything from the original sentences?" If yes, add it back in.
So what? Why does combining sentences matter?
In your paragraph writing, strong writers combine related ideas into single, clear sentences instead of writing choppy short ones. When you combine well, your paragraphs flow better and sound more mature. It's the same skill — just applied to a longer piece of writing.

Grammar Fixes

Capitalize, "your" vs "you're," and missing words

Three small grammar patterns came up in your test. Each one is easy to fix once you know the rule:

Which sentence uses "your" and "you're" correctly?
A "They think your crazy."
B "They think you're crazy."

1. Capitalize the start of a sentence

last but not least... → Last but not least...

Every sentence must begin with a capital letter — even when it starts with a common phrase like "last but not least."

2. "Your" vs "You're"

they think your crazy → they think you're crazy

You're = you are. Your = belongs to you. Quick test: replace the word with "you are." If it makes sense ("they think you are crazy"), use you're.

3. Missing "more" in comparisons

people enjoy fast music than slow music → people enjoy fast music more than slow music

When you compare with "than," you usually need "more" (or an -er word) before it.

QUICK REFERENCE
you're = you are  •  your = belongs to you
Capitalize the first word of every sentence
Comparisons: more + adjective + than (or adjective-er + than)

Q11 — 16/20 — Good points, but where's the proof?

Paragraph Plan

Topic Sentence, Detail, Why It Matters, Detail, Why It Matters, Detail, Why It Matters, Concluding Sentence

Here's how a full G4 paragraph about "A Legacy of Stone" would look using the plan:

Paragraph Plan
Topic Sentence → Detail + Why It Matters (repeat 3 times) → Concluding Sentence
Topic Ferdinand Cheval's story teaches readers to never give up on their dreams.
Detail 1 He spent 33 years building a palace entirely from rocks he collected by hand.
Why This shows incredible patience and dedication to a single goal.
Detail 2 He collected rocks during his mail route every single day.
Why Even small daily efforts can lead to something big over time.
Detail 3 Villagers laughed at him, but he kept building anyway.
Why This shows that believing in yourself matters more than what others think.
Ending Cheval's story shows that hard work and determination can turn a simple idea into something extraordinary.
KEY TAKEAWAY
After every detail, write a sentence explaining why it matters. That's what turns a list of facts into a real paragraph. Your paragraph had 5 sentences — aim for 8 sentences using this plan, and always include specific evidence from the passage.
Why is "PLAN FIRST" the single most effective thing you can do?
Before you start writing, jot down your plan in the margin: Topic Sentence, Detail 1, Why, Detail 2, Why, Detail 3, Why, Concluding Sentence. This takes 30 seconds and guarantees you'll include enough details AND explanations. Without a plan, most students write 4-5 sentences. With a plan, they write 7-8 — and those extra sentences are usually the "why it matters" ones that earn the most points.
