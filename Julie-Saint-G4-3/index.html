#2 Fix: One Comma Too Helpful

Q6 — a good sentence interrupted in the middle of its subject

Question 6 asked for one way to keep a small animal safe, and your idea was right on target. Look closely at what happened in the middle:

Q6 — 2 OUT OF 3
“One way to take care of a small animal, so it stays safe is having an encloseure for it.”

Take the comma out and read it: “One way to take care of a small animal so it stays safe … is … having an enclosure for it.” The whole first stretch — all eleven words — is the subject of the sentence. It is one long name for the thing you are about to define. The comma landed in the middle of that name, splitting it from itself.

THE FIXED VERSION
“One way to take care of a small animal so it stays safe is giving it a secure enclosure.”
No comma at all — a long subject still runs straight into its verb. Long is fine. Interrupted is not.
WHY THE COMMA WANTED IN
After ten or eleven words, your voice wants to pause — and the comma is how a pause feels on the page. But a breathing spot is not a comma spot. The check: find what the sentence is about (the whole long “one way to… safe” phrase) and its verb (“is”). If the comma sits between them, lift it out.

AOne way to keep a pet safe at home is closing the gate every time.

Three real strengths first. You quoted the doubter’s exact words from the article — quotation marks and all — which is evidence-handling most fourth graders have not tried yet. You explained the mechanism of the zai holes: they trap water, the land stays moist. And your last sentence circles back to answer the question your first sentence asked. That is a paragraph with a shape.

So where are the three points? Look at your two character claims side by side. “He didn’t give up when someone shouted…” — that one comes with its proof attached: the shout, quoted, and him continuing anyway. But “he was a kind and wise man” stands alone — nothing in the paragraph shows him being kind or wise. One claim is evidence; the other is a label. The question asked how his character helped, and only the proven claim actually answers it.

The opening sentence also tangled its verb — “is helped” wants to be has helped — and this paragraph is where most of the spelling list from card 3 lives.

YOUR PARAGRAPH, DEVELOPED

“Yacouba is a person that has helped the land become green again, but how did his ideas and character help him? His character helped because he was determined. He didn’t give up when someone shouted, ‘Do you really think those seeds will grow without water?’ He kept planting even when people laughed, and that is what determination looks like. His idea was to make zai holes. The zai holes trap the water, so the land stays moist and the seeds can grow where nothing grew before. That is how Yacouba’s character and ideas helped him.”

Notice the one real change: “kind and wise” became “determined” — because determination is the trait your own quoted evidence already proves. The claim and the proof now point at each other, and everything else you built stays.

SO WHAT?
When you name a character trait, the very next sentence should show it happening. You already did this once — the didn’t-give-up line is claim-plus-proof in miniature. Do it for every trait you name and this paragraph is at 20, and the test is past 90.
