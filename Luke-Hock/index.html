Q5: Fragments Need Their Own Sentence

Don't attach a fragment to an unrelated sentence

The question gave you a fragment — a piece of a sentence that can't stand on its own:

Fragment
Nearby, a caterpillar.

To fix a fragment, you need to turn it into a complete sentence by adding a verb — something the subject does.

WHAT WENT WRONG
You tacked the fragment onto an unrelated sentence instead of giving it its own complete thought.

The caterpillar has nothing to do with the other sentence, so attaching it there doesn't work. Instead, give the caterpillar its own action:

BETTER
Nearby, a caterpillar crawled across a leaf.
THE FRAGMENT FIX
When you see a fragment, ask: What is this thing doing?

• "Nearby, a caterpillar." → What is it doing? → crawled across a leaf
• "The old wooden bridge." → What is it doing? → creaked in the wind

Add a verb that makes sense, and the fragment becomes a real sentence. Don't attach it to a sentence about something else — give it its own.

Q6: Add Specific Details from the Passage

Connect your answer back to what you read

The passage was about Noah finding tadpoles. When the question asked about taking care of a small animal, you wrote:

YOU WROTE
One way to take care of a small animal is by feeding it.

This is true, but it's too vague. It could be about any animal — it doesn't connect to the passage at all. The question wanted you to use specific details from what you read.

WITH PASSAGE DETAILS
One way to take care of a small animal is by keeping its habitat clean, like how Noah changed the water in the tadpoles' tank every day.
THE SPECIFICITY RULE
When a question connects to a passage, your answer should too. Ask yourself:

"Could someone tell which passage I read from my answer?"

If the answer is no, you need to add a specific detail. Mention a character's name, an action from the story, or a detail only found in that passage. General statements like "by feeding it" don't show the grader you understood the reading.

Your Proofreading Checklist

Small fixes that make a big difference

You scored 9/10 on editing, so you already have a good eye. Here are the specific things to watch for in your own writing:

Read your paragraph out loud for run-ons.
If you run out of breath or hear two ideas crashing together, put a period and start a new sentence. Your run-on ("very little people believed in him he still pushed through") would sound off when read aloud.
Check: "very little" vs. "very few"
Use "few" for things you can count (people, animals, books). Use "little" for things you can't count (water, time, hope). You wrote "very little people" — it should be "very few people."
Check singular vs. plural nouns.
You wrote "grow plant in dry land" — since Yacouba grew more than one, it should be "grow plants." After writing, scan for nouns and ask: am I talking about one or more than one?
Does every answer connect back to the passage?
Before you move on from a question, ask: "Did I mention something specific from the reading?" If your answer could apply to any passage, add a detail.
