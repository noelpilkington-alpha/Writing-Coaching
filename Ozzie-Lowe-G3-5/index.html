THE EVIDENCE SANDWICH (you already bake these — we're tightening them)
Layer 1 — TOPIC: Your main idea (you nailed this: "there are statues under the sea").
Layer 2 — CLAIM + SPECIFIC FACT from the passage: Your point, plus one concrete thing from the article — quoted OR paraphrased.
Layer 3 — EXPLAIN: One sentence that says what the fact shows or why it matters.

Your G3.5 move: You already have two good sandwiches (Mexico + Grenada as places; coral reefs as the "why"). To hit 18/20, make sure your third supporting detail also has a specific fact — not just a general idea.
What "a specific fact" looks like on a paragraph
GOOD A name, place, date, or number from the passage.
"Jason built his first park in Mexico and his second in Grenada." → two specific places, both from the passage.
GOOD A short exact phrase in "double quotation marks" introduced with "The article says..."
The article says the statues "become homes for coral" over time.
TOO GENERAL "People did things to help." → could be any passage, any topic. No specific fact.
TOO GENERAL "Every year swimmers hurt the reefs." → has the idea but doesn't say what Jason specifically did about it. Add one specific fact from the passage.

Appositives: One Sentence, One Renaming Phrase

Q4 on G3.5 earned 1 of 2 — same skill you nailed on G3.3
🔍
Pattern: G3.1 — missed the appositive (wrote three sentences). G3.3 — nailed it ("Sally, a young harbor seal, swam..."). G3.5 — got partial credit (1 of 2). This is the most common move we see: the skill is there, but one small comma slip pulls the score down. The fix is one tightening move.

Here's what happened on G3.5 Question 4. The prompt asked you to combine two sentences using an appositive. You had the right idea — one sentence with a renaming phrase — but you added an extra piece that broke the structure:

YOUR G3.5 Q4 ANSWER (1 of 2 pts)
"The books are still popular, the best books, they are know for their golden spines."
Two issues: (1) "they are know" should be "they are known" (past participle). (2) The appositive "the best books" renames "the books" fine — but then "they are known for their golden spines" comes after a comma, creating a comma splice (two complete thoughts joined with just a comma).

You had the appositive right ("the best books" renaming "the books"). What dropped the score is the part after the appositive. When you use an appositive, the rest of the sentence should be one clean thought, not a second complete sentence tacked on with a comma.

Prompt: "Combine the ideas by using an appositive: The books are still popular. They are known for their golden spines." Which answer earns full credit?
A The books are still popular, they are known for their golden spines.
B The books, known for their golden spines, are still popular.
The Appositive Shape
SHAPE Noun, renaming phrase, rest of sentence.
Commas on BOTH sides of the renaming phrase. No second complete sentence after the comma — the rest keeps going as one thought.
Sally, a young harbor seal, swam to the other seals. ✓
NOT Noun, renaming phrase, second full sentence. → That's a comma splice.
The books are popular, the best books, they are known for their spines. ✗
NOT Appositive without a comma on one side. → Needs both commas.
My dog Lucy loves the beach. ✗ (no commas) vs. My dog, Lucy, loves the beach. ✓

TWO FULL-CREDIT APPOSITIVE VERSIONS
Option 1: The books, known for their golden spines, are still popular.
Option 2: The books, some of the best books ever made, are known for their golden spines.
Each has one sentence, one renaming phrase, commas on both sides.
YOUR 30-SECOND APPOSITIVE CHECK
Before moving on from an appositive answer, ask yourself:
1. Is my whole answer one sentence (not two or three)?
2. Is there a noun phrase renaming another noun right next to it?
3. Are there commas on BOTH sides of the renaming phrase?
If all three are yes, you're at 2 of 2.
