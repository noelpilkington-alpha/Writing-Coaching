The three body paragraphs then take those three parts, one each. The conclusion runs the same road back: thesis in new words, specific statement, general statement. THE PASSAGE

How Cold Changed the Kitchen

In an American city of the 1850s, food was a race against time. Milk soured within a day in summer and fresh meat spoiled in two, so families shopped every morning and ate what they bought before dark. What could not be eaten quickly was salted, smoked, dried or pickled, and a winter diet meant root vegetables, salt pork and whatever had been preserved in autumn. The only way to keep food cold was ice, cut from frozen ponds in January, packed in sawdust in thick-walled icehouses, and sold block by block through the summer. A family with an icebox needed a fresh block delivered every few days, and in a hot year the ice ran out before September.

The answer came from a machine, not a pond. In the 1870s engineers built refrigeration machines that squeezed a gas such as ammonia into a liquid and then let it expand again. Expanding gas pulls heat out of whatever surrounds it, so a sealed room could be kept cold for as long as the machine ran. Breweries bought the first ones, because beer spoils in warm cellars. Then, in 1878, the Chicago meat packer Gustavus Swift put the idea on wheels. His refrigerated rail car carried ice in bunkers at each end and let cold air flow over the meat hung in the middle. For the first time, beef butchered in Chicago could reach New York still fresh. The price of beef in eastern cities fell, and by 1900 thousands of refrigerated cars were rolling across the country.

The cold took longer to reach the kitchen. The first electric home refrigerators appeared around 1913, but they were loud, cost more than a car, and used gases that were poisonous if they leaked. In 1930 chemists produced a safer refrigerant, factories began building the machines by the hundred thousand, and the price fell from about six hundred dollars to under two hundred. By 1944, more than eight of every ten American homes had a refrigerator. A family could now shop once a week instead of once a day, keep leftovers, and buy frozen vegetables, which Clarence Birdseye had started selling in 1930.

Farms and towns changed shape to fit the cold. A farmer no longer had to grow a little of everything for the neighbours, because a crop could travel. Florida oranges and California lettuce reached northern cities in the middle of winter. Dairy farms grew larger, because milk could now move hundreds of miles before it soured. Towns along the rail lines built ice plants and cold-storage warehouses, and the old icehouses by the ponds closed one by one. The winter plate of root vegetables and salted meat gave way to fresh food in every month of the year.

Today the cold runs in an unbroken chain from the field to the supermarket shelf to the kitchen, and almost nobody notices it. A machine built to keep beer and beef from spoiling is the reason a head of lettuce picked on Monday in one state can be eaten on Thursday a thousand miles away.
