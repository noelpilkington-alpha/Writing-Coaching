AlphaWrite Grade 3 Concept Guide

Seven parts. One part per sitting. Every rule is a check you can run

The Grade 3 writing test and the AlphaWrite Grade 3 activities score the same few things. This guide names each one as a rule you can run before you submit, shows a sentence that passes and a common miss, and gives you three tries with help on a miss. The pass mark on the test is 90%.

How to use it: one part per sitting. Read the rule, try the items, run the quick check at the end of the part, then practice in the AlphaWrite activity each card names. Keep Part 7 open when you write.

Read the Instruction Verb

Part 1 of 7: read the ask, read it back. The first word to read is the job word, not the sentence
THE RULE
Read the instruction verb: name, change, combine, write. Say the verb and the type or slot word before you type. Name means one word. Change means a new sentence. Combine means one sentence from two, in the slot the prompt names.

Three answers with the right grammar and the wrong job:

A COMMON MISS: ASKED TO NAME
What type of sentence is this? The train leaves at eight.
“Does the train leave at eight?” ASKED TO NAME, THE ANSWER CHANGED IT
The verb was what type. The answer is one word.
A COMMON MISS: APPOSITIVE AT THE END
“A gentle giant, the elephant walks for miles each day.” SLOT WORD SKIPPED
The prompt said at the end. The phrase went first.
A COMMON MISS: CHANGE INTO A QUESTION
“The pool opens at nine.” ASKED TO CHANGE, NOTHING CHANGED

The hand runs the move it practiced last. The instruction verb is the first thing to read.

The Read-Back Five

Part 1 of 7: read the ask, read it back. Five checks, a finger on each word, before every submit
THE RULE
The read-back five. Before you submit, read your answer once with a finger on each word and check:
1. Capital I.
2. The end mark matches: a question gets ?, a statement or command gets . (a command may take !), an exclamation gets !
3. A comma after In conclusion and other openers.
4. Names get a capital. A sport or a school subject does not.
5. The pairs: its / it’s, then / than, to / too, where / wear.
The finger catches the rest: a doubled word, a missing word, swapped letters.

These are rules you already know. The hand types what it has seen most, and the grader counts every slip. The read-back is where the rule gets its turn. One common miss for each check:

1. CAPITAL I
“Yes, i would go because i think it would be fun.”
2. END MARK
“How do bees find their way home.”
3. COMMA AFTER THE OPENER, AND 4
“In conclusion Basketball rules are hard to follow.”
No comma after the opener, and basketball is not a name.
5. THE PAIRS
“Tennis balls bounce higher then most balls.”
Then is for time. Comparing takes than.
THE FINGER
“For example, we saw we saw three deer by the creek.”
AFTER THE READ-BACK
Tennis balls bounce higher THANthan most balls.

Three that pass, and one that fails twice:

CHECK 3In conclusion, warming up before a game keeps your muscles loose.
CHECK 5The turtle pulled its head into its shell.
CHECK 1I would feel proud because finishing the race is hard.
CHECKS 3 AND 4In conclusion Chess players have to practice every day.
No comma after the opener. Chess is not a name.

Find the Doer

Part 2 of 7: sentence shapes. Declarative means statement. Imperative means command. Interrogative means question. Exclamatory means exclamation
THE RULE
Find the doer. A doer and a period: statement. No doer, action word first: command. Point at who or what does the action. Then look at the end mark. The topic does not decide. The shape decides.

Two sentences that pass the test:

COMMAND
NO DOER, ACTION WORD FIRSTStretch your legs before practice.
STATEMENT
DOER: THE TEAMThe team trains on Saturday mornings.

Two common misses, both decided by the topic instead of the shape:

TAPPED IMPERATIVE
“Dancers bow to the audience before they leave the stage.” A DOER AND A PERIOD
Dancers is the doer. Period. A statement about what dancers do.
TAPPED DECLARATIVE
“Practice your bow before the show.” NO DOER, ACTION WORD FIRST
Nobody is named. Practice is the action word, and it is first. A command.

Sentences about rules sound like commands. The shape decides, not the topic.

Three statements, labeled by shape, and one command:

STATEMENTA helmet is the hard hat a rider wears.
STATEMENTA rider must keep both hands on the bars.
Must does not make a command. The doer is still there.
STATEMENTSkaters should check the ice for cracks before they play.
COMMANDBow before class.

Rushing is the usual cause of a wrong tap. How to take an identify item without rushing:

  1. Read the sentence once.
  2. Find the doer.
  3. Look at the end mark.
  4. Tap.

Aim to spend at least 20 seconds on each identify item.

A Command Starts with the Action Word

Part 2 of 7: sentence shapes. Turning a statement into a command means the doer has to go
THE RULE
A command starts with the action word. No name, I, you or we in front. Please may come first. Cut everything before the action word, and what is left is the command.

Two commands that pass:

An Exclamation Keeps Its Doer

Part 2 of 7: sentence shapes. A command with an exclamation mark is still a command
THE RULE
An exclamation tells a feeling and keeps its doer. It names who or what, says how they feel or how big, good or bad something is, and ends with !. A command with ! is still a command.

Two exclamations that pass:

EXCLAMATION
DOER: THE PUPPYThe puppy learned to sit in one day!   DOER: YOUYou baked this bread yourself!

Two common misses that came out as commands:

A COMMON MISS, CHANGE Can you clean your room before dinner? INTO AN EXCLAMATION
“Clean your room before dinner!” NO DOER: A COMMAND
AS AN EXCLAMATION
DOER: YOUYou cleaned your whole room before dinner!
A COMMON MISS, CHANGE Did you remember to feed the goldfish? INTO AN EXCLAMATION
“Remember to feed the goldfish!” NO DOER: A COMMAND
AS AN EXCLAMATION
DOER: YOUYou remembered to feed the goldfish!

Both misses started from a question that asked someone to do something, so the hand wrote the doing. An exclamation says how someone feels about it.

Three exclamations, and one command with an exclamation mark:

EXCLAMATIONThat goal was unbelievable!
EXCLAMATIONWe finished the whole trail!
EXCLAMATIONThe cake smells amazing!
A COMMANDLook at that sunset!
No doer, action word first. That sunset is amazing! keeps a doer and tells the feeling.

The Sentence Test

Part 2 of 7: sentence shapes. Three questions for every sentence, and one end mark that is never optional
THE RULE
Find the action word. Find the doer. Is the thought finished? All three: a sentence. One missing: a fragment. In a command the doer is you, left unsaid. And a question ends with a question mark, every time.

Two sentences that pass:

Who plays? Playing alone does not finish the thought. The answer needs its doer: I.

“How do bears find food in the winter.” A QUESTION, A PERIOD

A Naming Phrase

Part 3 of 7: the naming phrase. Finding an appositive is easy. This part is about building one
THE RULE
An appositive is a naming phrase. It gives the thing another name. The check: cover the noun. Can your phrase stand in its place? It is a naming word with its describing words, often with a, an, the or my in front, and it has no verb inside.

Two naming phrases that pass the check:

NAMING PHRASE
Penguins, NAMING PHRASEflightless birds of the far south, huddle against the wind.
NAMING PHRASE
The kettle, NAMING PHRASEa dented copper pot, whistled on the stove.

Two common misses that did not pass:

A COMMON MISS
“The soup, that smelled good, was ready at noon.” THAT + VERB
Cover the soup: that smelled good was ready at noon. It cannot stand in, and smelled is a verb.
AS A NAMING PHRASE
The soup, NAMING PHRASEa thick bean stew, was ready at noon.
A COMMON MISS
“The medal, that hung on the wall, shone in the sun.” THAT + VERB
AS A NAMING PHRASE
The medal, NAMING PHRASEa small tin circle, shone in the sun.

Three naming phrases, and one phrase that only describes:

NAMING PHRASENadia, a girl in my class, won the spelling bee.
NAMING PHRASEThe ticket, a small blue slip, got us into the game.
NAMING PHRASEOur prize, a box of markers, sat on the desk.
THAT + VERBThe broom, that was brown, swept up the confetti.
That was: a verb inside.

Find the Is A

Part 3 of 7: the naming phrase. Which sentence holds the naming phrase, and what happens to the other one
THE RULE
Find the is a. The other sentence stays whole. One of the two sentences says what the thing is: is a, is an, are, was a. The words after it are the naming phrase. Cut It is or They are. The other sentence keeps every word and its verb.

Two common misses, one of them the test’s favorite:

A COMMON MISS: COMBINE WITH AN APPOSITIVE
“My cousin has two noisy parrots, their names are Kiwi and Mango.” TWO SENTENCES, ONE COMMA
The test calls this a comma splice and scores it zero.
A COMMON MISS: APPOSITIVE AT THE BEGINNING
“It brings the whole street together, the block party is a summer tradition.” TWO SENTENCES, ONE COMMA

Every miss keeps a second whole sentence, verb and all. Every clean one cuts the verb and keeps only the naming phrase.

By the rule:

FROM The fox is a clever hunter. It waits by the hedge.
AFTER IS AA clever hunter, THE OTHER SENTENCE, WHOLEthe fox waits by the hedge.
THE BLOCK PARTY, BY THE RULE
Sentence 1 has the is a. Sentence 2 stays whole.
NAMING PHRASEA summer tradition, the block party brings the whole street together.
THE PARROTS, BY THE RULE
My cousin has two noisy parrots, NAMING PHRASEKiwi and Mango.

Three that kept the other sentence whole, and one that kept It is:

FROM: THE WOLF IS A PACK HUNTERA pack hunter, the wolf never travels alone.
FROM: THE SLOTH IS A SLOW CLIMBERA slow climber, the sloth takes all day to cross one tree.
FROM: IT IS A WOODEN FLUTEThe recorder, a wooden flute, is the first instrument many kids learn.
TWO SENTENCESIt is a fast game, soccer needs two teams.
It is kept its verb. Cut it: A fast game, soccer needs two teams.

Three Slots

Part 3 of 7: the naming phrase. Same phrase, same sentence. Only the commas move
THE RULE
The slot moves the commas, not the words. Beginning: phrase, comma, sentence. Middle: noun, comma, phrase, comma, the rest. End: sentence, comma, phrase. Same naming phrase, same whole sentence, every time.

The same phrase in all three slots:

BEGINNING
PHRASEA loyal friend, SENTENCEthe dog waits by the gate every afternoon.
MIDDLE
The dog, PHRASEa loyal friend, waits by the gate every afternoon.
END
SENTENCEThe dog waits by the gate every afternoon, PHRASEa loyal friend.

The end slot is the one most students skip. A common miss on it:

A COMMON MISS, AT THE END: Divers wear a wetsuit. It is a tight rubber suit for cold water.
“It is a tight rubber suit for cold water, a wetsuit.” WRONG NOUN FIRST
“A tight rubber suit that keeps them warm, a wetsuit.” MAIN SENTENCE DROPPED
SENTENCE, COMMA, PHRASE
SENTENCEDivers wear a wetsuit, PHRASEa tight rubber suit for cold water.

One phrase in all three slots, and one in the wrong slot:

BEGINNINGA patient hunter, the heron stands still for hours.
MIDDLEThe heron, a patient hunter, stands still for hours.
ENDThe heron stands still for hours, a patient hunter.
WRONG SLOTPrompt: at the end. A patient hunter, the heron stands still for hours.

Count the Parts

Part 4 of 7: answer the question. The paragraph that answers a question about a passage
THE RULE
Count the parts. Answer every part. And, each, why, what happens: each one adds a part. One sentence per part at least. Then the topic sentence answers, it does not ask. A statement that uses the question’s words.

Three questions with the parts marked:

THE KITE QUESTION
What important steps and conditions are needed to successfully fly a kite? TWO PARTS
A common miss: conditions only.
THE CLEATS QUESTION
How do soccer cleats help players perform better and stay safe on the field? TWO PARTS
A common miss: perform better only.
THE CAMPFIRE QUESTION
What three types of wood are needed to build a campfire, and how does each type help the fire? TWO PARTS
A common miss: a paragraph about campfires in general, with neither part.

A topic sentence that answers both parts:

THE CLEATS QUESTION, ANSWERED
PART 1Soccer cleats help a player perform better PART 2and stay safe by gripping the grass and guarding the feet.
A COMMON MISS, CAMPFIRE, TOPIC SENTENCE
“Campfire wood is important.” NO PART ANSWERED
BY THE RULE
PART 1Campers need three types of wood, PART 2and each one helps the fire in its own way.

Three topic sentences that answer every part, and one that asks:

TWO PARTS, BOTH ANSWEREDWhy do cats purr, and when? → Cats purr to show comfort, and they do it when they are relaxed or petted.
ONE PART, ANSWEREDHow do bats find food in the dark? → Bats find food in the dark by sending out clicks and listening for the echo.
TWO PARTS, BOTH ANSWEREDName two parts of a bike and say what each does. → The pedals turn the chain, and the brakes stop the wheels.
ASKS, DOES NOT ANSWERWhy do cats purr, and when? → Why do cats purr and when do they do it?

A Detail You Can Point To

Part 4 of 7: answer the question. The passage already has the words. Your job is to find them
THE RULE
A detail is a word you can point to in the passage. Find the noun in the passage that answers the part. Put it in your sentence. If you cannot put your finger on it, it is a gist, not a detail.

A passage, and a common miss written from it:

THE CANOE PASSAGE SAID
The person sitting in the front, called the bow paddler, sets the rhythm and watches for rocks or logs in the water. The person in the back, called the stern paddler, is in charge of steering the boat with different paddle strokes.
A COMMON MISS
“You need two people who work well together.” GIST, NO NOUN

By the rule:

CANOE, POINTABLE

Grammar Rules Reference

Part 5 of 7: grammar rules for sentences. The general rules, each one a check you can run. Keep this page open when you write

Nineteen rules, a quick check, and a rules list you can print.

1. A SENTENCE
Find the action word. Find the doer. Is the thought finished? All three there: a sentence. Any one missing: a fragment. In a command the doer is you, left unsaid. Long does not mean finished.
SENTENCEKites fly.
FRAGMENTthe red kite in the tall tree
No action word. The red kite is stuck in the tall tree.
FRAGMENTWhen the wind picked up
When leaves it hanging. When the wind picked up, the kite rose.
2. TWO THOUGHTS
Two thoughts, two sentences, or one joining word between them (and, but, so, because). A comma on its own cannot hold two sentences together.
RIGHTThe rain stopped. We went outside.
RIGHTThe rain stopped, so we went outside.
RUN-ONThe rain stopped we went outside.
COMMA ALONEThe rain stopped, we went outside.
3. FOUR KINDS OF SENTENCE, FOUR END MARKS
The end mark matches. A statement (declarative) tells and ends with a period. A question (interrogative) starts with a question word or a helper such as do, did, can, is, and ends with a question mark. A command (imperative) starts with the action word and ends with a period, or an exclamation mark when it is shouted. An exclamation (exclamatory) tells with strong feeling and ends with an exclamation mark. It never asks.
STATEMENTSwimmers rinse off after the pool.
QUESTIONDo swimmers rinse off after the pool?
COMMANDRinse off after the pool.
EXCLAMATIONThat pool is freezing!
4. GROWING A SENTENCE
Grow a bare sentence: when, where, why, how. Start with the doer and the action word, then add the answers. The dog barked. becomes Last night, the dog barked at the gate because a fox ran past.
GROWNAfter school, the team practiced free throws in the gym because the finals were on Friday.
STILL BAREThe team practiced a lot.
A lot answers nothing. When, where, why, how.

Capitals

Part 5 of 7: grammar rules for sentences. Three places a capital goes, and where it does not
5. CAPITAL LETTERS
A capital goes on: the first word of a sentence; the word I; and names, months and titles: one particular person, place, pet, brand, day, month, holiday, and the main words of a book or film title. The check: is it the name of one particular one? Capital. Is it a kind of thing (a dog, a city, a season)? Small letter.
6. AFTER A COMMA
After a comma, a small letter, unless it is a name or I.
RIGHTOn Friday, Maya and I visited Denver.
RIGHTWe read The Hobbit in March.
RIGHTA patient climber, the mountain goat picks its way up the cliff.
Small letter on the after the comma. A name, like Maya, would keep its capital.
WRONGon friday, maya and i visited denver.
Six capitals missing.
WRONGIn the Summer we swim.
A season is a kind of time, not a name. summer.

Punctuation

Part 5 of 7: grammar rules for sentences. Commas, apostrophes, quotation marks
7. COMMAS
A comma goes: between items in a list (eggs, milk, and bread). Before and, but or so when they join two whole sentences (I ran, but I missed the bus.). After an opening phrase (After lunch, we read.). And on both sides of a naming phrase (Leo, my cousin, plays drums.). A comma hugs the word before it, then one space. A comma never holds two sentences together on its own.
LISTWe packed hats, snacks, and water.
TWO SENTENCES JOINEDThe bus was late, so we walked.
OPENING PHRASEAfter the game, the team ate pizza.
WRONGSam ,my cousin ,plays drums.
Spaces on the wrong side. Sam, my cousin, plays drums.
8. APOSTROPHES
An apostrophe marks a missing letter or an owner. can’t, it’s: letters are missing. Sam’s bike: Sam owns it. A plural noun, more than one of something, gets none: dogs, knives. The owner words its, his, hers never take one.
MISSING LETTERWe didn’t hear the bell.
OWNERThe coach’s whistle was loud.
WRONGThe dog’s ran across the yard.
A plural noun, nothing owned. The dogs ran.
9. QUOTATION MARKS
Quotation marks go around exact words: what someone said, word for word, or a short exact phrase copied from a passage. Your own words about the passage get none.
EXACT WORDSThe article says the reefs “can rest.”
YOUR OWN WORDSThe article explains that the reefs get a break from swimmers.

Words That Agree

Part 5 of 7: grammar rules for sentences. Subject and verb, tense, pronouns, sound-alikes
10. SUBJECT AND VERB
One thing: is. More than one: are. After did: the plain verb. One thing takes is, was, has, does, runs. More than one takes are, were, have, do, run. After did, does or do, the verb goes back to its plain form: did win, not did won. The check: point at the doer and count it.
RIGHTUnder the porch are three puppies.
RIGHTDid the bus leave on time?
WRONGUnder the porch is three puppies.
11. TENSE
Pick a tense and stay in it. If the sentence starts in the past, the other verbs stay in the past.
RIGHTThe pond was frozen, so we skated all afternoon.
WRONGThe pond was frozen, so we skate all afternoon.
12. I AND ME
I does the action. Me receives it. Sam and I went. The coach spoke to Sam and me. The check: take the other person out. I went, not me went. Spoke to me, not spoke to I.
13. SOUND-ALIKE PAIRS
These sound the same and mean different things. its (belongs to it) / it’s (it is). their (belongs to them) / there (a place) / they’re (they are). your (belongs to you) / you’re (you are). to (toward) / too (also, or extremely) / two (2). where (a place) / wear (clothes). then (next) / than (comparing). The check: say the long form back. If it is fits, write it’s.

Joining and Naming

Part 5 of 7: grammar rules for sentences. Joining words, and the naming phrase
14. JOINING WORDS
Each joining word has a job. and adds. but contrasts. so gives the result. because gives the reason. since gives the reason or the time. or gives a choice. When and, but or so join two whole sentences, a comma goes before them. because and since take no comma when they come second in the sentence. Use the joining word the instruction names, not a different one.
RESULTThe gym was closed, so we ran outside.
REASONWe ran outside because the gym was closed.
CONTRASTWe wanted the gym, but it was closed.
WRONG JOBWe ran outside, and the gym was closed.
And only adds. The second half is the reason, so because.
15. JOINING WORDS THAT LEAN
Some joining words start a part that cannot stand alone: because, since, when, if, after, before, while, although, until. Comma when the joining part comes first. When it comes second, no comma.
FIRST, COMMAWhen the bell rang, we lined up.
SECOND, NO COMMAWe lined up when the bell rang.
WRONGWe lined up, when the bell rang.
16. THE NAMING PHRASE (APPOSITIVE)
An appositive is a naming phrase. It gives the thing another name: a naming word with its describing words, and no verb inside. The check: cover the noun. Can your phrase stand in its place? In the middle, commas on both sides. First in the sentence: naming word first, small letter after the comma.
MIDDLEHedgehogs, small spiny mammals, hunt for beetles at night.
FIRSTA fast swimmer, the otter chases fish along the riverbed.
NOT A NAMING PHRASEThe lantern, that was rusty, hung from a hook.
That was has a verb and cannot stand in for the lantern. The lantern, an old brass light, hung from a hook.

Paragraphs and Outlines

Part 5 of 7: grammar rules for sentences. The shape that earns the twenty points
17. THE PARAGRAPH
The topic sentence answers, it does not ask. It is a statement that answers the question in the prompt. Then two or more supporting details, each one a fact from the passage followed by a This shows sentence that says what the fact proves. Then the concluding sentence: one sentence, no new fact. Six sentences at least.
THE SHAPE, ON A MADE-UP PASSAGE ABOUT BEES
TOPIC SENTENCEBees matter to farmers because they pollinate the crops. DETAIL 1 · SHOWThe passage says one hive can visit a million flowers in a day. DETAIL 1 · SO WHATThis shows that a single hive does work no farmer could do by hand. DETAIL 2 · SHOWIt also says apple trees without bees grow fewer than half as many apples. DETAIL 2 · SO WHATThis shows that the harvest depends on the bees showing up. CONCLUDING SENTENCEBees are small, but a farm cannot work without them.
18. TRANSITIONS, AND STAYING ON TOPIC
Transition words show the reader the order: First, Next, Then, Finally. For example brings a detail, However makes a turn, In conclusion ends. A comma follows a transition word that starts a sentence. Every sentence serves the topic sentence. The check: cover the topic sentence, read the detail, ask whether it proves it. If not, cut it.
TRANSITIONFirst, the hive wakes before dawn.
OFF TOPICBees matter to farmers. My cousin is scared of bees.
The second sentence is about the cousin, not the farmers. Cut it.
19. THE OUTLINE
Before the paragraph, the outline: the topic sentence as a full statement, then notes, and notes are key words, not sentences. If a note could stand on its own with a period, it is a sentence, not a note. Then the concluding sentence. On the test, type the outline at the top of the box, write the paragraph under it, then delete the outline before you submit.
NOTEmillion flowers a day, hive
NOTEapple trees, fewer than half
A SENTENCE, NOT A NOTEOne hive can visit a million flowers in a day.

Grammar Rules for Words

1. THE FIVE JOBS
Ask the word’s job. A noun names a person, place, thing or idea: dog, Denver, courage. A pronoun stands in for a noun: he, she, it, they, I, me. A verb is the action or being word: ran, is. An adjective describes a noun, telling which one, what kind or how many: three, muddy. An adverb tells how, when or where about a verb, and often ends in -ly: quietly, yesterday, outside.

2. IDEA NOUNS
Some nouns name things you cannot touch: childhood, courage, kindness, freedom. Idea nouns are still nouns. They can be the doer of a sentence like any other noun.
IDEA NOUN AS DOERCourage kept the firefighters calm.

Nouns: One and Many

Part 6 of 7: grammar rules for words. How a noun becomes plural
3. PLURALS
Add -s, unless the ending says otherwise. Words ending in s, x, ch or sh add -es: boxes, wishes. A consonant plus y changes to -ies: baby, babies. Many f and fe words change to -ves: knife, knives; leaf, leaves. Some change completely: child, children; mouse, mice; tooth, teeth; person, people; foot, feet. A plural noun never takes an apostrophe. The check: say the plural out loud. If you hear an extra beat (box-es), write -es.
RIGHTtwo boxes, three babies, four knives, five children
WRONGtwo box’s
A plural noun, nothing owned, no apostrophe.
WRONGthree babys, five childs
Consonant plus y becomes -ies. Child changes completely.

Verbs and Tenses

Part 6 of 7: grammar rules for words. Past, present, future, and the verbs that change shape
4. THREE TENSES
Yesterday, today, tomorrow. Past tells what already happened: walked. Present tells what happens now or always: walk, walks. Future uses will plus the verb: will walk. The check: put yesterday, today or tomorrow in front and pick the verb that fits. Then stay in that tense.
5. IRREGULAR PAST
Most verbs add -ed for the past. Some verbs change instead of adding -ed, and you learn these by sight: run, ran; go, went; eat, ate; see, saw; write, wrote; take, took; lead, led; sit, sat; bring, brought; think, thought. Never both: ranned and goed are not words.
PASTYesterday we ran to the park.
FIXEDhow the general led his army
Lead is today. In the past, lead becomes led.
WRONGYesterday we runned to the park.
WRONGTomorrow we walked to the park.
Tomorrow wants the future: will walk.

Pronouns Agree

Part 6 of 7: grammar rules for words. It, he, she, they: matched to the noun
6. PRONOUN AND NOUN
A pronoun matches the noun it stands for. One thing: it. One person: he or she. More than one: they. The check: find the noun the pronoun points back to, and count it. For I and me, see Part 5, rule 12.
MORE THAN ONEThe dogs barked because they wanted food.
ONE THINGThe dog barked because it wanted food.
WRONGThe dog barked because they wanted food.
One dog, so it.

Comparing

Part 6 of 7: grammar rules for words. Bigger, biggest, more careful, most careful
7. COMPARING
Two things: -er or more. Three or more: -est or most. Short words take -er and -est: tall, taller, tallest. Longer words take more and most: careful, more careful, most careful. Never both: more taller is wrong. Some change completely: good, better, best; bad, worse, worst. Adverbs compare the same way: more carefully, most carefully.
8. SPELLING -ER AND -EST
Double the consonant, change y to i. Big becomes bigger: one short vowel before one consonant doubles the consonant. Happy becomes happier: y changes to i.
TWO THINGSMaya is taller than Sam.
THREE OR MORELeo is the tallest of the three.
LONGER WORDThis road is more dangerous than that one.
WRONGSam is more taller than me.
Never both. Taller.

Dialogue, Addresses, and Endings

Part 6 of 7: grammar rules for words. Three rules that come up in stories and letters
9. DIALOGUE
Quotation marks go around exact words. The spoken words start with a capital. A comma separates the speaker from the words: Maya said, “Let’s go.” or “Let’s go,” said Maya. The end mark of the spoken words stays inside the closing quotation mark.
10. ADDRESSES
A comma between the parts of an address: between the street and the city, and between the city and the state. 12 Oak Street, Austin, Texas.
11. ADDING ENDINGS
Drop the e, double the consonant, change y to i. A silent e drops before -ing and -ed: hope, hoping, hoped. One short vowel before one consonant doubles the consonant: hop, hopping, hopped. Y changes to i before -es, -ed, -er and -ness: cry, cries, cried; happy, happiness. Y stays before -ing: crying.
DIALOGUE“Pass the ball,” said Leo.
ADDRESSWe drove to Austin, Texas.
ENDINGShoping, hopping, cries, crying, happiness
WRONGLeo said pass the ball.
Spoken words, so a comma, a capital, and quotation marks: Leo said, “Pass the ball.”
WRONGhopeing, crys
Drop the e: hoping. Y to i: cries.
