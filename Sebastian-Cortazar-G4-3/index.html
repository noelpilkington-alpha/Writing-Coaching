#1 Fix: Expand the Fragment

Q5 — 0 out of 2 — the biggest single loss on the test

Sebastian, Question 5 gave you this fragment and asked you to expand it into a complete sentence:

THE FRAGMENT
“Nearby, a caterpillar.”
QUESTION 5 — 0 OUT OF 2
“The bird was nearby, a caterpillar”

Here is what went sideways: instead of adding to the fragment, the answer rebuilt the sentence around a bird — and the caterpillar ended up dangling at the end with no job to do, and no period after it. The result is still not a complete sentence, and it no longer says what the fragment was trying to say.

THE MOVE: KEEP, THEN COMPLETE
“Expand the fragment” means: keep the fragment’s words, then add what is missing. Ask yourself what the fragment lacks. “Nearby, a caterpillar” has a who (a caterpillar) and a where (nearby) — what it is missing is a verb: what did the caterpillar DO?
Which answer correctly expands “Nearby, a caterpillar.”?
ANearby, a caterpillar crawled along a leaf.
BThe bird was nearby, a caterpillar
YOUR TWO-STEP CHECK ON G4.4
Every fragment question: (1) find what the fragment is missing (usually the verb — what happened?), (2) add it while keeping the fragment’s own words in place. Then read your answer alone: does it stand as a complete sentence, with a period? Those 2 points were the difference between 84% and 88% on this test.

#2 Fix: The Appositive Is a Phrase, Not a Word

Q3 — 1 out of 2 — a point it has taken on three tests running

Sebastian, we have worked on appositives before, so let us be precise about what happened this time — because it is a new mistake, and actually a smaller one. Q3 asked you to add an appositive giving more information about Noah’s mom:

QUESTION 3 — 1 OUT OF 2
“He asked his angry mom for some lettuce.”

“Angry” is a single describing word slipped in front of “mom.” An appositive is bigger than that: it is a whole naming phrase, set off between commas, sitting right after the noun. One word inside the sentence cannot do that job — and it earns one point, not two.

WHAT Q3 WAS LOOKING FOR (THE SHAPE)
✓ “He asked his mom, the person he always turned to for help, for some lettuce.”

A whole phrase · between two commas · right after “mom” · and it could never stand alone as a sentence. That is the full 2-point shape.
Which sentence adds a true appositive about the mom?
AHe asked his kind mom for some lettuce.
BHe asked his mom, the kindest person in the house, for some lettuce.
THE THREE-PART SHAPE CHECK
Whenever a question says appositive, check your answer for all three parts: (1) a phrase, not a single word · (2) a comma on each side · (3) placed right after the noun it renames. Miss any one and the point slips away — this question appears on nearly every G4 test, so this is a repeat investment.
ONE MORE FROM THIS TEST: ONE QUESTION, ONE SENTENCE
Q8 asked for one sentence about how a plant or animal changes — and got four, with commas holding some of them together. Your frog knowledge was great; the length cost the point. When Q6–Q10 say “one sentence,” pick your single best idea, attach the reason, and stop. More sentences do not earn more credit — they only add places for errors to hide.

YOUR PARAGRAPH, REBUILT (closer to 20/20)

“Yacouba’s clever idea and his determination helped him bring dry land back to life. Yacouba Sawadogo lived in Burkina Faso, a country in Africa that had gotten almost no good rain for ten years, so the soil was dry, cracked, and slowly turning into desert. Yacouba dug small holes in the ground, filled them with manure, leaves, and other waste, and planted seeds inside them. People didn't believe it was going to work because there was no water, but he said the rain would return soon. The holes he made caught the water instead of letting it run away, so the plants could receive water. This shows his character: even when everyone doubted him, he trusted his idea and kept working. He was right, and people started to use his idea too.”

Two bolded sentences do all the work: the first one answers the question before the story starts, and the second one names the character trait your own evidence was already proving. Everything else is your writing with the spelling repaired.

SO WHAT?
You are a natural storyteller — the details were all there and all accurate. The move that turns 17 into 20: when a prompt asks how or why, open with a sentence that answers it directly, and after your evidence add one sentence starting “This shows…” The story earns 17. The answer earns the last three.
