What Is an Appositive?

Q4 — This is a new skill to learn

Q4 asked you to write an appositive. Here's what you wrote:

YOU WROTE
Bees buzz from flower to flower and is a very good pollinater.

This is not an appositive — it's an extra clause added with "and." It also has two errors: "is" should be "are" (bees is plural), and "pollinater" should be "pollinator."

An appositive is a short description placed right after a noun, surrounded by commas. It renames or describes the noun:

Bees buzz from flower to flower and is a very good pollinater.
Bees, nature's tiny helpers, buzz from flower to flower.

APPOSITIVE EXAMPLES
Bees, nature's tiny helpers, buzz from flower to flower.
Pumpkins, a favorite fall fruit, grow on long vines.
The seeds, flat white ovals, can be roasted for a snack.
HOW TO WRITE AN APPOSITIVE
Step 1: Find the noun you want to describe (e.g., "Bees").
Step 2: Think of a short description that renames it (e.g., "nature's tiny helpers").
Step 3: Place it right after the noun with commas on both sides.

Pattern: [Noun], [description], [rest of sentence].
Example: Bees, nature's tiny helpers, buzz from flower to flower.
See the difference: appositive vs. clause
Appositive (correct): Bees, important pollinators, help pumpkins grow.
Added clause (NOT an appositive): Bees buzz and they are important pollinators.

The appositive goes inside the sentence, right after the noun, with commas.

SPELLING STRATEGIES
1. Passage words: Words like "pumpkin," "pollinator," and "measure" are in the passage. Look back and copy them exactly.
2. Their/there/they're: "Their" = belonging to them. "There" = a place. "They're" = they are. You want "their" when talking about people's stuff.
3. Double letters: "finally" has two l's (final + ly). "pollinating" has two l's too.
4. Sound it out: "pumpkin" = pump-kin (not "pumkin"). "measure" = mea-sure. "reasons" = rea-sons.

Build Your Paragraph

Q11 — Good framework, needs specific evidence

Your paragraph scored 16/20. You had the right structure — topic sentence, three points, concluding sentence — but your ideas need more development.

YOUR PARAGRAPH
Cooking helps children for three resons. Number one is that it can help you problem solve. It can also help you with math when you mesure things and finaly it can help with reading by having to read the instruction. Overall it is very good for kids to learn how to cook.

Issues to fix:

  • No specific evidence from the passage. You listed three reasons but didn't give any examples or details to support them.
  • Ideas are underdeveloped. "It can help you problem solve" — HOW? What kind of problem solving? The passage probably gave examples you can use.
  • Spelling errors: "resons," "mesure," "finaly," "instruction" (needs to be plural).

Here's the G3 paragraph plan you should follow:

Paragraph Plan — TS → D1 → W → D2 → W → D3 → W → CS
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail from the article  •  W = Why it matters  •  CS = Concluding Sentence
TS Cooking helps children learn important skills for three reasons.
D1 First, cooking teaches problem solving because kids have to figure out what to do if they are missing an ingredient.
W This shows that cooking makes kids think on their feet and come up with solutions.
D2 Second, cooking helps with math because kids measure cups, tablespoons, and fractions.
W This shows that real-life math practice in the kitchen makes math easier to understand.
D3 Third, cooking helps with reading because kids have to read recipes and follow the instructions step by step.
W This shows that cooking gives kids a real reason to practice reading carefully.
CS Overall, cooking is a great way for children to build problem-solving, math, and reading skills.
THE KEY: ADD SPECIFIC DETAILS
You had the right three reasons. Now you need to explain each one with a specific example from the passage. Instead of "it can help you with math," say what kind of math — measuring cups, fractions, tablespoons. After each detail, write a "why it matters" sentence: "This shows that..."
