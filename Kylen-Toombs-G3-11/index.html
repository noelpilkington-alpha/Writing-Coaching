G3.11 Q2 — APPOSITIVE COMBINE (SCORED 0/1)
Prompt: "Combine Sentence 13 and Sentence 14 into one sentence by using an appositive. Sentence 13: Hundreds of penguins stand close together. Sentence 14: They form a big group called a huddle."
You wrote: "Hudreds of penguins stand close together, they form a big group called a huddle."
Two issues: (1) this is a comma splice — "they form a big group" has its own subject ("they") and verb ("form"), so it's a full second sentence joined by just a comma. An appositive is a naming phrase, not a second sentence. (2) Spelling: "Hudreds" should be "Hundreds."

A full-credit appositive combine pulls the naming phrase ("a big group called a huddle") out of Sentence 14, drops "they form," and tucks the phrase next to the noun it describes. Two commas, no second subject-verb:

WHAT FULL CREDIT LOOKS LIKE
✓ "Hundreds of penguins stand close together, a big group called a huddle."
✓ "Hundreds of penguins, a big group called a huddle, stand close together."

Notice: "a big group called a huddle" has no subject-verb of its own. It is just the renaming phrase — exactly what an appositive is supposed to be.
G3.11 Q2 — which combine uses a real appositive?
A Hundreds of penguins stand close together, they form a big group called a huddle.
B Hundreds of penguins, a big group called a huddle, stand close together.
THE APPOSITIVE 3-STEP (SAME AS LAST TIME — LOCK IT IN)
Every time you see "combine using an appositive":

1. Pick the sentence with the main action. It stays mostly the same.
2. From the other sentence, grab just the naming phrase — drop "they are / they form / he is / it was."
3. Wrap it in two commas right next to the noun it renames.

G3.11 example:
Main: "Hundreds of penguins stand close together."
Grabbed phrase (from "They form a big group called a huddle"): "a big group called a huddle"
Combined: "Hundreds of penguins, a big group called a huddle, stand close together."
THE APPOSITIVE TEST (DO THIS EVERY TIME)
After you combine, read the part between your two commas. Ask: "Does this part have its own subject + verb?"

• If NO → real appositive. ✓
• If YES → comma splice. Drop the subject + verb.

✓ "penguins, a big group called a huddle, stand..." ← no subject-verb → appositive
✗ "penguins, they form a big group, stand..." ← "they form" = subject + verb → splice
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
This is the one recurring pattern across G3.9, G3.10, and G3.11. If you close this loop, that is a guaranteed point every test. One point on Questions 1–5 is worth 2 percentage points on your overall score. That alone moves you from 79 toward 81.

G3.11 Q3 — COMBINE TWO SENTENCES (SCORED 0.75/1)
Prompt: "Combine Sentence 15 and Sentence 16 into one complete sentence. Sentence 15: The penguins take turns being on the outside. Sentence 16: The penguins take turns being in the warm middle."
You wrote: "The penguins take turns being in the warm middle and the penguins take turns being on the outside."
Three small slips: (1) you repeated "the penguins take turns" twice — the whole point of combining is to say the shared part once. (2) You swapped the order (middle then outside, when the original was outside then middle). (3) No comma before "and," but when the subject is shared you often do not need one anyway.
CLEAN VERSION
✓ "The penguins take turns being on the outside and in the warm middle."
Say "the penguins take turns" once. Then list the two places with "and" between them. Same meaning, way less repetition, full credit.
G3.11 Q3 — which combine is tightest?
A The penguins take turns being in the warm middle and the penguins take turns being on the outside.
B The penguins take turns being on the outside and in the warm middle.
THE SHARED-SUBJECT RULE
When two sentences start the same way, find the shared part and say it once.

Sentence 15: The penguins take turns being on the outside.
Sentence 16: The penguins take turns being in the warm middle.

Shared part: "The penguins take turns being"
Different endings: "on the outside" and "in the warm middle"
Combined: "The penguins take turns being on the outside and in the warm middle."
KEEP THE ORIGINAL ORDER
Sentence 15 came before Sentence 16, so list "outside" before "middle" — match the order of the original prompt. Flipping the order is not wrong grammar, but it reads like you did not stay close to the text.

MODEL POLISHED PARAGRAPH (full-credit level)

A caterpillar's body must change in important ways before it can become a butterfly. First, the caterpillar eats a lot to get the nutrients and energy it needs for the big change. Then it forms a chrysalis and stays inside for about two weeks while its body transforms. Inside the chrysalis, the caterpillar grows wings so that it can fly once it comes out.
