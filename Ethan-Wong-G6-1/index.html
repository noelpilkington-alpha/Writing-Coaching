The 5-Paragraph Shape

What the rubric is looking for on every G6 expository essay

Once you give yourself the time, the next question is: what are you building with that time? Every G6 expository essay is scored against a five-paragraph shape. Here is the blueprint:

The G6 Five-Paragraph Essay
INTRO Hook sentence + 1–2 setup sentences + thesis (the main claim)
BODY 1 Topic sentence + evidence 1 + "this shows..." + evidence 2 + tie-back
BODY 2 Topic sentence + evidence 1 + "this shows..." + evidence 2 + tie-back
BODY 3 Topic sentence + evidence 1 + "this shows..." + evidence 2 + tie-back
CLOSE Restate thesis in new words + "what we learn" closer

Your G6.1 essay was one long paragraph. The rubric has a 5-point row for structure — one paragraph instead of five is how that row costs you points.

Intro: merged Body 1: merged Body 2: merged Body 3: merged Conclusion: merged

Good news: your one paragraph already has the ingredients. You mention her polio, her family helping her, her training, her Olympic medals, and an ending thought about breaking barriers. That is a thesis and three body topics — they are just all crammed together. Pulling them apart into five separate paragraphs is almost entirely a formatting move, not a writing move.

Which structure scores closer to 5/5 on the rubric's "Write 5 clear paragraphs" row?
A One long paragraph that covers the intro, three reasons, and the conclusion all in a row.
B Five separate paragraphs: intro with thesis, one body per reason, a real conclusion at the end.
THE EVIDENCE + ANALYSIS PAIR
Inside each body paragraph, the 5-point "evidence" row is looking for two pairs:

Evidence sentence — "The passage says..." / "According to the article..."
Analysis sentence — "This shows that..." / "This proves that..."

Your G6.1 essay has facts but almost no "this shows" sentences. Adding one analysis sentence after each fact is the single biggest evidence-row upgrade you can make.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Structure = 5 points. Evidence + analysis = 5 points. Together those two rows are half the essay rubric. Splitting into five paragraphs and adding two analysis sentences per body is how 13/20 becomes 17–18/20.

A 17–18/20 VERSION — SAME IDEAS, PROPER SHAPE

[Intro] Wilma Rudolph is an inspiration for many young athletes. Born in 1940 in Tennessee as the 20th of 22 children, she faced challenges that would have stopped most people. Yet through perseverance, Wilma showed that physical setbacks, family support, and years of hard training can turn a struggling child into an Olympic champion.

[Body 1 — Early struggle] First, Wilma showed perseverance by surviving serious health problems. The passage explains that she had polio, and that afterward her left leg turned weak and she had to wear a metal brace for many years. This shows that Wilma was not born an athlete — she was born with an obstacle most children would never overcome. Doctors believed she would never walk again, which proves how unlikely her later success really was.

[Body 2 — Family support] Next, Wilma showed perseverance by accepting help from her family. The article says her family never gave up on her and did exercises with her daily. This shows that perseverance is not always alone — sometimes it means letting other people push you. Because of that daily work, she eventually took off the brace and could walk without any help, something no doctor expected.

[Body 3 — Training to win] Finally, Wilma showed perseverance by training to become one of the fastest women in the world. The passage states that she joined her school's track team, trained hard in college, and in 1960 won three gold medals at the Rome Olympics for the American track and field team. This proves that her earliest steps in a metal brace and her fastest steps on an Olympic track came from the same quality — she did not quit.

[Conclusion] Wilma Rudolph's story teaches us that setbacks do not decide our future — our response to them does. From a child in a brace to an Olympic champion, she proved that with help, effort, and years of patience, even the biggest barriers can be broken.

What changed: Same facts from your original. Split into 5 clear paragraphs. Added a real thesis in the intro. Added "this shows" / "this proves" sentences after each fact. Added a standalone conclusion. Fixed "Doctors". That is roughly the gap between 13/20 and 17–18/20.
