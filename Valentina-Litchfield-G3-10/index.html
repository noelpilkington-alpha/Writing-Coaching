#1 Growth Area: The Appositive on Q1

An appositive is a noun phrase that renames — not a clause with "which"

Q1 on G3.10 asked you to combine two sentences using an appositive. Here is what you wrote:

FROM G3.10 Q1
"The first bikes, which where made of wood, looked very different from the bikes today."
Scored 0.5 out of 1. Two things: "which where made of wood" is a clause, not an appositive (an appositive is a noun phrase that renames). Also "where" should be "were." The idea is right — the shape is not.
WHAT IS AN APPOSITIVE?
An appositive is a noun phrase that sits next to another noun and renames or describes it. It does not have "which," "that," or a verb. It's like giving the noun a nickname.

• Appositive: The first bikes, wooden machines with no pedals, looked very different.
• Not an appositive (clause): The first bikes, which were made of wood, looked very different.

Trick: if you can remove "which/that" and the "was/were" and it still reads as a solid noun phrase, you have an appositive.
Building an Appositive from Sentence 3
STEP 1 Sentence 3 says: "the first bicycle was made of wood." Find the key detail: made of wood.
STEP 2 Turn it into a noun phrase: "a wooden machine" or "a wooden invention".
STEP 3 Drop it into Sentence 2 with commas on both sides.
RESULT "The first bikes, wooden machines, looked very different from bikes today."
Which combined sentence uses a true appositive?
A The first bikes, that were made of wood, looked very different.
B The first bikes, wooden inventions, looked very different.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q1 asked for an appositive specifically. Half credit here (0.5/1) is the difference between 81% and around 82% on this test alone. Appositive shape is one of the sentence-combining moves that shows up on every G3 test. Once you lock in "noun phrase, no verb," Q1 becomes a reliable full-credit question.

#2 Growth Area: The Comma Before "and" / "so"

When you combine two complete sentences with a conjunction, a comma goes before it

Two questions on G3.10 lost points from the same small rule: a missing comma before a conjunction.

FROM G3.10 Q3 — combine with "and"
"It had a giant front wheel, a tiny back wheel, and the rider sat very high up in the air"
Scored 0.75 out of 1. The comma before "and" is actually there — but this combination lost points because it made "the rider sat very high" sound like a third thing the bike had, instead of a second idea. Also no period at the end.
FROM G3.10 Q4 — combine with because/but/so
"It was hard to balance on this wheel so many people fell off and got hurt."
Scored 0.5 out of 1. Two issues: no comma before "so" (two complete sentences joined by a conjunction need a comma), and "this wheel" should be "this bike" to match the original.
THE COMMA-BEFORE-CONJUNCTION RULE
When you join two complete sentences with and, but, or, so, because, put a comma before the conjunction.

• Two complete sentences: "It was hard to balance. Many people fell off."
• Joined: "It was hard to balance, so many people fell off."

The comma is the visual pause that shows the reader "here comes the second idea."
Rewriting Q3 More Cleanly
IDEA 1 "It had a giant front wheel and a tiny back wheel."
IDEA 2 "The rider sat very high up in the air."
JOIN "It had a giant front wheel and a tiny back wheel, and the rider sat very high up in the air."
KEY Keep Idea 1 whole. Add ", and" before the second complete idea. End with a period.
Which version of the Q4 combination earns full credit?
A It was hard to balance on this wheel so many people fell off and got hurt.
B It was hard to balance on this bike, so many people fell off and got hurt.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q3 and Q4 together are worth 2 points. You scored 1.25 on them. Getting the comma-before-conjunction move down (, and / , so / , but / , because) plus matching the original words exactly would put you at 2.0 — and that alone moves you from 81% toward 85%+.

Dogs are fun pets, but some dogs have important jobs. One of these jobs is police work. These dogs use their noses to find missing people or items.
