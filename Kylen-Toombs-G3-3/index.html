Question 1 Fix — Turning a Sentence into a Question

Can you spot the problem? Then we'll fix it together.

The question asked you to change this sentence into a question: "Anna named the seal Sally."

You need to keep all the important words from the original sentence. Which version does that?

Tap the question that keeps all the important words:
A Did Anna name the Sally?
B Did Anna name the seal Sally?
WHAT YOU WROTE

BETTER VERSION

You were really close! You knew to add "Did" at the start and put a question mark at the end. The only problem was accidentally dropping the word "seal."

HOW TO CHANGE A SENTENCE INTO A QUESTION
1. Start with a helper word: Did, Does, Is, Was, Can, etc.
2. Keep every important word from the original sentence
3. End with a question mark

Quick check: read your question and the original sentence side by side. Did you lose any words?
WHY THIS MATTERS FOR YOUR WRITING
When you write paragraphs, sometimes you want to start with a question to grab the reader's attention. If you accidentally drop words, the question won't make sense. Checking that all the key words are there is a small habit that makes a big difference.

Question 3 Fix — Adding an Appositive

First: can you tell the difference?
📈 You've missed appositive questions on 2 out of 2 tests — the pattern is the same each time: adding extra words, but not the right kind of extra words. An appositive is a specific thing, and once you see it, you'll get it every time.

The question asked you to rewrite this sentence and add an appositive: "Sally swam to the other seals."

An appositive is a short description set off by commas that renames or describes a noun. Which version actually adds one?

Tap the sentence that has a real appositive:
A Sally swam to the other seals with her cousins.
B Sally, the baby seal, swam to the other seals.
WHAT YOU WROTE
"Sally swam to the other seals with her cousins."
BETTER VERSION
"Sally, the baby seal, swam to the other seals."

You added words to the sentence, which shows good instinct! But "with her cousins" is just extra detail — it doesn't rename or describe Sally. An appositive always renames or describes the noun right next to it.

THE APPOSITIVE RULE
An appositive is a renaming phrase set off by commas. It answers: "Who or what is this?"

Pattern: [Name], [who or what they are], [rest of sentence]

Examples:
• "Max, my golden retriever, loves to swim."
• "Mrs. Johnson, our neighbor, brought us cookies."

Quick test: Can you replace the name with the appositive and have it still make sense?
"The baby seal swam to the other seals." — Yes! That works.
WHY THIS MATTERS FOR YOUR WRITING
Appositives help your reader understand who or what you're talking about without writing a whole extra sentence. In your paragraph writing, using an appositive makes your sentences sound more grown-up and gives the reader important details in a smooth way.

Questions 4 & 5 Fix — Joining Ideas Together

Two related skills: combining sentences and picking the right connecting word
📈 You've missed "Because, But, So" questions on 2 out of 2 tests — the pattern is the same each time: using the wrong connecting word or leaving it out entirely.

Question 4 asked you to combine two sentences into one:

1. "Anna and Jim ran to the beach."   2. "They found a baby seal in a fishnet."

Tap the version that correctly combines both sentences:
A Anna and Jim ran to the beach they found a baby seal in a fishnet.
B Anna and Jim ran to the beach, where they found a baby seal in a fishnet.
WHAT YOU WROTE (Q4)
"Anna and Jim ran to the beach they found a baby seal in a fishnet."
BETTER VERSION
"Anna and Jim ran to the beach, where they found a baby seal in a fishnet."

You had the right idea — you just forgot to put a connecting word between the two ideas. Without it, the sentence runs together and is hard to read.

Question 5 asked you to pick the best word to fill in the blank:

"___ there was no sign of the seals, the family placed Sally in a fish box and motored into the bay."

WHAT YOU WROTE (Q5)
"Also there was no sign of the seals..."
BETTER ANSWER
"Because there was no sign of the seals, the family placed Sally in a fish box and motored into the bay."

"Also" just adds another idea. But here, the first part explains why they put Sally in the box — there were no seals around. When one part is the reason for the other, use "because."

WHEN TO USE BECAUSE, BUT, AND SO
Because = gives the reason (answers "why?")
• "I wore a coat because it was cold."

But = shows a surprise or opposite idea
• "I was tired, but I kept playing."

So = shows what happened next because of something
• "It rained, so we went inside."

Quick test: ask yourself, "Is this the reason? The opposite? Or the result?"
WHY THIS MATTERS FOR YOUR WRITING
Connecting words are what make your sentences flow together instead of sounding choppy. In your paragraph writing, every time you give a reason, use "because." Every time you show a result, use "so." This makes your thinking clear to the reader.

Questions 7, 8, & 9 Fix — Writing Complete Sentences

Three questions, one shared pattern

Questions 6 through 10 all asked you to write one complete sentence. You got Questions 6 and 10 right, but Questions 7, 8, and 9 had the same kinds of small problems. Let's look at the pattern.

Which of these is a complete sentence?
A When Jim and Anna helped the seal because they helped the seal find her family.
B The most meaningful moment was when Jim and Anna helped the seal find her family.

Here are all three questions and what to fix:

QUESTION 7: WHAT YOU WROTE
"When JIm and Anna helped the seal because they helped the seal find her family."
BETTER VERSION
"The most meaningful moment was when Jim and Anna helped the seal find her family."

Problem: Your answer starts with "When" but never makes a complete statement. It also repeats "helped the seal" twice. Fix: Start with the main idea, then use "when" to add the detail.

QUESTION 8: WHAT YOU WROTE
"Yes because it is a creative name and a cute name for a seal."
BETTER VERSION
"Yes, naming the seal Sally was a good idea because it is a creative and cute name for a seal."

Problem: Starting with just "Yes because" skips the main idea. The sentence should restate what you're agreeing with before explaining why.

QUESTION 9: WHAT YOU WROTE
"No i would not do anything differently because they helped the seal find its paranets."
BETTER VERSION
"No, I would not do anything differently because they helped the seal find its parents."

This one was close! Two small fixes: always capitalize "I," and watch the spelling of "parents."

THE COMPLETE SENTENCE FORMULA FOR "EXPLAIN" QUESTIONS
1. Restate the question in your answer (don't just say "Yes" or "When")
2. Add "because" and give your reason
3. Read it out loud — does it sound finished?

Example: "Would you help a lost puppy?"
Not complete: "Because it might be scared."
Complete: "I would help a lost puppy because it might be scared."
WHY THIS MATTERS FOR YOUR WRITING
Every sentence in your paragraph needs to be a complete thought. If you practice making complete sentences on the short-answer questions, you'll naturally write better sentences in your paragraph too.

THE RUN-ON FIX
A run-on happens when you put two complete thoughts together without a period or connecting word. Your writing has great ideas — they just need to be separated properly.

Run-on: "He thought most games were not very good he wanted to make a better one."
Fixed: "He thought most games were not very good, so he wanted to make a better one."

Quick check: if you can split a sentence into two sentences that both make sense on their own, you need either a period or a connecting word between them.
PLANNING WITH THE BUILDING BLOCKS
Before you write, plan your paragraph:
Topic Sentence: What is my paragraph about? (Answer the prompt)
Detail 1 + Why: What fact will I use? Why does it matter?
Detail 2 + Why: What other fact will I use? Why does it matter?
Closing: How do I wrap up the main idea?

After each detail, ask yourself: "So what? Why does this matter?" — the answer is your explanation.
3-MINUTE CHECK BEFORE YOU FINISH
Before you turn in your writing, read each sentence one at a time. Ask yourself:
1. Is this one complete thought, or did I smoosh two together?
2. Did I spell the tricky words right?
3. Does my first sentence tell the reader what the whole paragraph is about?
