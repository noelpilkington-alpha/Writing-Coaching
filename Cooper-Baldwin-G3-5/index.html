THE HABIT TO BUILD

Clean Sentence Boundaries

Give each idea its own space

Cooper, your paragraph had the right idea — but all of your ideas were packed into one long sentence with no stopping points. When several complete ideas run together, the grader can lose track of each one. Here is the start of your paragraph:

YOU WROTE (Q11, the opening)
“This is why Jason wanted to put a statues under the sea he wanted to protect the coral reefs swimmers accidentally hurt animals if he put statues under the sea around the statues instead the reefs can rest.”

That is four or five complete ideas with no boundaries between them. The fix is not to chop it into tiny pieces — it is to give each complete idea a clean boundary: end it with a period and start the next, or join two closely related ideas with a connecting word like because or so.

SAME IDEAS, WITH CLEAN BOUNDARIES
Jason placed his statues under the sea to protect the coral reefs. Real coral reefs are fragile, and swimmers can accidentally hurt them. Now visitors come to see the statues, so the reefs get a chance to rest.

Same idea, same evidence — but now each thought has room to breathe, and every sentence is complete on its own.

Which version gives each idea a clean boundary?
A Jason placed his statues under the sea to protect the coral reefs. Swimmers can accidentally hurt the reefs, so the statues give them somewhere else to go.
B Jason wanted to protect the coral reefs he put statues under the sea swimmers hurt the reefs so the statues can help.
THE RULE
After each complete idea, ask: “Is this a whole thought?” If yes, end it with a period and start the next one. Only join two ideas together when they belong together — and then use a connecting word like because or so. No idea should run straight into the next with nothing between them.
