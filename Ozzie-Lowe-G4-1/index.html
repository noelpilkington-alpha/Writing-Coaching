Error #1 — Spelling Patterns

The words you are misspelling follow a pattern you can fix
🔎 Pattern: You spell words the way they sound instead of the way they are written. This is a single habit, and one technique fixes all of it: slow read-back.

These spelling mistakes appeared across multiple questions on this test:

YOUR SPELLING ERRORS

Q1 — 1 out of 2: prestigius → prestigious

Q8 — 2 out of 3: compleamets → compliments

Q10 — 2 out of 3: concince → convince

Q11 — 13 out of 20: muess → mess

Which version is spelled correctly?
A "He won the prestigius award."
B "He won the prestigious award."
THE FIX: SLOW READ-BACK
After you finish writing, go back and point to each word with your finger (or your cursor) and say it in your head syllable by syllable:

• com-pli-ments (not "compleamets")
• con-vince (not "concince")
• pres-ti-gious (not "prestigius")
• mess (not "muess")

If a word looks weird when you point to it, it probably is. Stop and fix it right there.
WHY THIS KEEPS HAPPENING
Your brain moves fast. You have creative ideas and you want to get them down. That is a strength. But your fingers skip letters because they are trying to keep up with your brain. The slow read-back is your safety net: it catches what your fast brain missed.
YOUR PERSONAL SPELLING LIST
Before your next test, practice writing these 4 words correctly 3 times each:

• prestigious (pres-ti-gious)
• compliments (com-pli-ments)
• convince (con-vince)
• mess (just one syllable, no "u")

Error #2 — Appositive Structure

Q3 — 1 out of 2 — the appositive describes the person, not the award

An appositive is a phrase set off by commas that gives more information about the noun right before it.

WHAT YOU WROTE
"Peter Tabichi, an award winning Global Teacher Prize, is from Kenya..."

The problem: you made the prize the appositive instead of describing Peter Tabichi. The appositive should tell us something about the person.

Which sentence uses the appositive correctly?
A "Peter Tabichi, an award winning Global Teacher Prize, is from Kenya."
B "Peter Tabichi, a Kenyan math and science instructor, won the Global Teacher Prize."
THE RULE: APPOSITIVE = ANOTHER WAY TO SAY THE NOUN
An appositive answers: "Who is that?" or "What is that?"

• Peter Tabichi → Who is he? → a Kenyan math and science instructor
• The appositive goes right after the name, surrounded by commas

Test it: Remove the appositive. Does the sentence still make sense?
"Peter Tabichi is from Kenya." Yes. Good.
"Peter Tabichi won the Global Teacher Prize." Yes. Good.
Tap to see the corrected sentence
Compare it to what you wrote
CORRECTED VERSION
"Peter Tabichi, a Kenyan math and science instructor, won the prestigious Global Teacher Prize."

The appositive "a Kenyan math and science instructor" describes Peter Tabichi (the person). The prize is now in the rest of the sentence where it belongs.
QUICK CHECK BEFORE YOU WRITE AN APPOSITIVE
Ask yourself: "Does the phrase between the commas describe the noun right before it?"

• "Peter Tabichi, an award winning Global Teacher Prize" — Is Peter Tabichi a prize? No. Wrong.
• "Peter Tabichi, a Kenyan instructor" — Is Peter Tabichi an instructor? Yes. Correct.

Error #3 — Comma Splices

Q1 — 1 out of 2 and Q5 — 1 out of 2

A comma splice happens when you join two complete sentences with just a comma. That is not enough glue.

WHAT YOU WROTE (Q5)
"His dedication has transformed the school, because of his efforts, enrollment has doubled..."

This has two complete thoughts joined by a comma:
• "His dedication has transformed the school" (complete sentence)
• "Because of his efforts, enrollment has doubled" (complete sentence)

Which version correctly joins these two sentences?
A "His dedication has transformed the school, because of his efforts, enrollment has doubled."
B "His dedication has transformed the school. Because of his efforts, enrollment has doubled."
THE RULE: TWO COMPLETE SENTENCES NEED REAL GLUE
When you have two complete thoughts, you have three options:

• Period: "His dedication transformed the school. Enrollment has doubled."
• Comma + conjunction: "His dedication transformed the school, and enrollment has doubled."
• Connecting word: "His dedication transformed the school, so enrollment has doubled."

What you cannot do: just a comma by itself between two complete sentences.
HOW TO SPOT A COMMA SPLICE
Cover up everything after the comma. Is what is left a complete sentence? Now cover up everything before the comma. Is that also a complete sentence? If both sides are complete sentences and there is only a comma between them, you have a comma splice. Add a period, or add a connecting word.
YOUR Q1 HAD THE SAME ISSUE
On Q1, you tried to combine sentences into one long run-on: "the award he received was the prestigius Global Teacher P..." (you ran out of space). When a sentence gets that long, break it into two. Short and clear beats long and tangled.

Question 11 — Paragraph Review

13 out of 20 — "Surprise Kick"
Developing: 13/20 Creative opening Underdeveloped Spelling errors

WHAT YOU DID WELL
Ozzie, you opened with a question to hook the reader: "Have you ever wondered why Cody didn't like mud." That is a strong creative instinct. You are thinking about grabbing attention, and that is exactly what good writers do.
WHAT HELD YOU BACK
Two things cost you points:

• Underdeveloped: Your paragraph starts with a great hook but then does not give enough detail. A 13/20 paragraph needs more sentences that develop the story — what happened next? How did the character feel? What did they do about it?

• Spelling: "muess" instead of "mess" is the same pattern from Cards 3 — your fast brain skipping ahead.

A MORE DEVELOPED VERSION (SAME IDEA)
"Have you ever wondered why Cody didn't like mud? Cody was waiting for Mud to mess up so he could prove that the dog was not worth keeping. Every time Mud tracked dirt across the kitchen floor, Cody pointed at the paw prints and said, 'See?' But then one afternoon, Mud did something no one expected. He kicked a soccer ball right back to Cody, sending it flying across the yard. Cody stood frozen for a second, then burst out laughing. That was the surprise kick that changed everything."

Same creative hook. Same character. But now we have details: what happened, what the character felt, and a clear ending. That is what moves a paragraph from 13/20 toward 16+/20.
THE DEVELOPMENT TRICK: ASK THREE QUESTIONS
After you write your opening sentence, ask yourself:

• What happened? (the events)
• How did the character feel? (the reaction)
• What changed? (the ending)

Answer all three in your paragraph and you will never be underdeveloped again.
QUICK FIX: QUESTION MARKS
Your opening: "Have you ever wondered why Cody didn't like mud." — That should end with a question mark, not a period. If the sentence starts with "Have you," "Do you," "Why did," or "What if" — it is a question. Use ? at the end.
