Thesis That Previews Your Essay

Your thesis needs to name your 3 body paragraph topics

Your thesis tells the reader what the essay is about, but not what specifically the three body paragraphs will cover:

YOUR THESIS
"Astronauts adjust their routines to survive and work effectively in space."

This is a reasonable claim, but it doesn't preview the three specific topics — hygiene, eating, and exercise — that your body paragraphs actually discuss. A reader finishing your intro doesn't know what's coming next.

FOCUSED THESIS
"Astronauts aboard the International Space Station must completely adapt their hygiene habits, eating routines, and exercise schedules to survive and work effectively in microgravity."

Notice the difference:

Your Thesis Focused Thesis
"adjust their routines" — vague, doesn't tell the reader which routines "hygiene habits, eating routines, and exercise schedules" — names all 3 body paragraph topics
THE RULE
A G8 thesis should contain a list of 3 items that match your 3 body paragraphs. Write your thesis after you outline your body paragraphs — then you know exactly what to preview.

Run-On in P3 + Staying On Topic in P4

Two paragraph-level issues to fix

Issue 1: Run-on sentence in Paragraph 3 (Eating)

Your Paragraph 3
Eating in space is also different most food is dehydrated and astronauts use velcro to keep things from floating away. Crumbs can float into equipment...

Two complete thoughts are fused together with no punctuation between them:

RUN-ON
"Eating in space is also different most food is dehydrated"
FIXED (TWO OPTIONS)
Period: "Eating in space is also different. Most food is dehydrated..."
Semicolon: "Eating in space is also different; most food is dehydrated..."

Issue 2: Off-topic evidence in Paragraph 4 (Exercise)

From Your Paragraph 4
Astronauts exercise for two hours a day because muscles and bones weaken... doing experiments and making sure everything is secured

This paragraph is about exercise, but "doing experiments and making sure everything is secured" is about general work duties — it doesn't belong here. Every sentence in a body paragraph must connect to the topic sentence.

STAYING ON TOPIC
Before you write each sentence, ask: "Does this connect to my topic sentence?" If your topic sentence is about exercise, then experiments and securing equipment don't fit. Replace off-topic evidence with more detail about exercise — like what types of exercise astronauts do or what happens if they skip it.

Strengthen Your Conclusion

Your conclusion works — but it could do more

Your conclusion is functional — it references all three body paragraph topics:

YOUR CONCLUSION
"By changing how they shower, eat, and exercise, astronauts adjust their routines to survive in space."

This restates the thesis, which is good. But a strong conclusion does two things: restates the main points and leaves the reader with a broader takeaway.

STRONGER CONCLUSION
"By changing how they shower, eat, and exercise, astronauts show that surviving in microgravity requires adapting every part of daily life. These adjustments are not just about comfort — they are essential for keeping astronauts healthy enough to carry out their missions. As space exploration expands to longer voyages, the ability to adapt routines will become even more important."

Notice what the stronger version adds:

  • Restates the 3 topics — shower, eat, exercise (same as yours)
  • Connects back to the thesis — "adapting every part of daily life"
  • Adds a broader takeaway — why this matters beyond the passage (longer voyages in the future)
CONCLUSION FORMULA
Sentence 1: Restate your 3 main points.
Sentence 2: Explain why these points matter together.
Sentence 3: End with a bigger-picture thought — a "so what?" that gives the reader something to think about.

Proofreading Checklist

Personalized to your recurring errors
Spelling — "their": thier → their. Remember: "I before E except after C" — their.
Spelling — "effectively": efffectively → effectively. Only two F's, not three.
Spelling — "dehydrated": deyhydrated → dehydrated. D-E-H-Y, not D-E-Y-H.
Spelling — "things": thinngs → things. Only one N.
Punctuation spacing: eat , → eat,. No space before a comma — the comma goes right after the word.
Run-on check: Read each sentence aloud. If you hear two complete thoughts with no period, semicolon, or conjunction between them, add punctuation.
On-topic check: Re-read each body paragraph's topic sentence, then check every other sentence — does it connect directly to that topic?
3-MINUTE RULE
Save the last 3 minutes of your test for proofreading. Read backward (last sentence first) to catch spelling — when you read forward, your brain auto-corrects. Focus especially on long words like "dehydrated" and "effectively" where extra letters sneak in.
