Give Each Idea a Clean Boundary

Your #1 fix — two complete ideas need more than a comma

Anna, this is the fix that moves your score up the most. A few times you joined two complete ideas with only a comma. That's called a comma splice — both halves could be their own sentence, so a comma alone isn't enough to hold them together. Here are two real ones from your test:

YOU WROTE (Q2)
"When dad was on a walk he found a pup in a tall box, he decided to take the pup home..."
YOU WROTE (Q11)
"Have you ever wondered how the game Pokemon was made, as a young boy Satoshi Tajiri loved catching bugs..."

In each one, both parts are complete ideas — "he found a pup in a tall box" and "he decided to take the pup home" can each stand alone. So they need a real boundary between them, not just a comma. You have two good ways to fix it:

FIX 1 — END THE FIRST IDEA WITH A PERIOD
"When dad was on a walk he found a pup in a tall box. He decided to take the pup home."
FIX 2 — JOIN THEM WITH A CONNECTING WORD
"When dad was on a walk he found a pup in a tall box, so he decided to take the pup home."

Both are correct. A connecting word like because, so, and, or when shows how the two ideas relate, and a period gives each idea its own clean space. Pick whichever fits the meaning best.

Which sentence gives each idea a clean boundary?
A He found a pup in a tall box, he decided to take it home.
B He found a pup in a tall box, so he decided to take it home.
THE RULE
If both halves of your sentence could stand alone, a comma by itself can't hold them together. Give each idea a clean boundary:

1. End the first idea with a period and start a new sentence, OR
2. Join them with a connecting word (because, so, and, when).

✗ "The seal was hurt, the family helped it." (comma splice)
✓ "The seal was hurt, so the family helped it." (clean boundary)

What an Appositive Really Is

A noun phrase that renames the noun next to it

Anna, on Question 3 you were asked to add an appositive to the sentence "Sally swam to the other seals." Here is what you wrote:

YOU WROTE (Q3)
"Sally playfully swam to the other seals in the tank."

This is a good sentence! But "playfully" is an adverb (it describes how she swam) and "in the tank" is a prepositional phrase (it tells where). Neither one is an appositive, so the question didn't get full credit.

WHAT AN APPOSITIVE IS
An appositive is a noun phrase that renames the noun right next to it, set off with commas. It answers "who or what is this?" by giving the noun a second name.

• Sally, a curious baby seal, swam to the other seals. ← "a curious baby seal" renames Sally
• Anna, the youngest in her family, spotted the pup. ← "the youngest in her family" renames Anna
YOUR Q3, WITH A REAL APPOSITIVE
"Sally, a curious baby seal, swam to the other seals."

See the difference? "a curious baby seal" is a noun phrase that renames Sally — that's an appositive. "Playfully" only describes the verb, so it's an adverb, not a renaming.

Which sentence contains a real appositive?
A Sally quickly swam to the other seals.
B Sally, a curious baby seal, swam to the other seals.
THE TEST TO RUN
Before you call something an appositive, ask: "Does this rename the noun?" If you can swap it in for the noun — "a curious baby seal swam to the other seals" still makes sense — it's an appositive. If it only tells how (playfully, quickly) or where (in the tank), it's not.
