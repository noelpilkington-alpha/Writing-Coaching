Fix 1: The Way In and the Way Out

Your introduction is two sentences. Your conclusion is the same two
THE RULE: GENERAL, SPECIFIC, THESIS; THEN THE MIRROR
An introduction makes three moves. GENERAL STATEMENT: a true sentence about the idea, not yet about Wilma. SPECIFIC STATEMENT: Wilma, named, with the fact that sets up the essay. THESIS: the point, and the three hows that will become your three body paragraphs. The conclusion runs it backwards: THESIS, IN NEW WORDS, then a SPECIFIC STATEMENT, then a GENERAL STATEMENT that says what the three bodies add up to. The check: count the moves in your first and last paragraphs. Three each. And the thesis names three things; the three body paragraphs prove them in that order.
YOU WROTE (INTRODUCTION)
“Wilma Rudolph perservered through many hardships like not being able to walk. Her perserverance can help us learn to never give up.” THESIS ONLY, NO WAY IN, NO THREE HOWS
Two sentences, and the second is a real thesis: it answers both halves of the prompt, how she persevered and what we learn. What is missing is the way in, and the three things the thesis promises to prove.
THREE MOVES IN
GENERAL STATEMENTMost people meet one wall in life they are told they cannot get past. SPECIFIC STATEMENTWilma Rudolph met hers as a small child, when polio left her leg in a metal brace and doctors said she would never walk again. THESISWilma showed perseverance by walking again when the doctors said she never would, by training until she was the fastest woman in the world, and by spending her life after the Olympics telling young people to keep trying, and her story teaches that a dream survives as long as the work does.
Your thesis, kept, with the three body paragraphs named inside it.
YOU WROTE (CONCLUSION)
“Wilma Rudolph perservered through not being able to walk and still won medals at the Olympics. Because of her we can learn to never give up.” THE INTRODUCTION AGAIN
The same two sentences, with the medals added. Nothing the three body paragraphs earned is in it.
THREE MOVES OUT
THESIS, IN NEW WORDSA brace, a track and a stage: Wilma Rudolph kept going at every one of them, and each time the going was the lesson. SPECIFIC STATEMENTThe girl the doctors wrote off walked at twelve, won three gold medals in Rome, and then spent decades telling children that dreams come true if you keep trying. GENERAL STATEMENTWhat we learn from her is that perseverance is not one brave moment but years of ordinary work, and that it is worth more when you hand it on.

Fix 2: After the Facts, This Shows

Each body paragraph gives facts from the passage and stops. The prompt’s second half never arrives
THE RULE: TOPIC SENTENCE, FACT, THIS SHOWS, AND WHAT WE LEARN
Each body paragraph is your G5.1 answer again. A TOPIC SENTENCE with the point (one way she persevered) and the how. A DETAIL · SHOW: a fact from the passage with its number or its words. A DETAIL · SO WHAT: This shows plus what the fact proves, and because this prompt has two halves, what we can learn from it. The check: in each body paragraph, find This shows, and find the word learn or teaches. Both missing means half the prompt is unanswered in that paragraph.
YOU WROTE (BODY PARAGRAPH 1)
“As a kid Wilma Rudolph got a illness that made it hard to walk. Doctors said she would never be able to walk again. Despite that Wilma never gave up hope. She exercised every day and at the age of 12 she finally walked again.” FACTS IN ORDER, THEN A STOP
Four facts from the passage, in the right order, and all of them correct. The first sentence is a fact, not a topic sentence: it does not say this is the first way she persevered. And the paragraph ends on the last fact, so the reader is never told what it shows or what to learn from it.
YOUR FACTS, IN THE FRAME
TOPIC SENTENCEWilma’s first act of perseverance was learning to walk again when the doctors said she never would. DETAIL · SHOWPolio left her left leg weak and in a metal brace for years, her family did exercises with her at home every day, and at twelve she took the brace off and walked without help. DETAIL · SO WHATThis shows that her perseverance was years of daily work rather than one brave moment, and it teaches us that the people around you are part of the effort.
Your four facts, kept, with a topic sentence in front and a This shows behind that answers the second half of the prompt.

The third body paragraph the passage was offering you, and the one that got the Olympics twice:

TOPIC SENTENCEWilma’s last act of perseverance came after the medals, when she did not stop working.
SHOWShe went on to teach, to coach and to speak, told her story to young people, and said that dreams can come true if you keep trying.
SO WHATThis shows perseverance turned outward, and it teaches us that the point of getting past a wall is to help the next person over it.

Two body paragraphs on one how. The passage’s last paragraph, her life after Rome, was the third how waiting to be used.

Fix 3: Vary the Sentences, Read Back the Word

Six points of the essay are sentences and editing. The word the essay is about was misspelled three times
THE RULE: ONE LONG, ONE SHORT; THEN A FINGER ON THE KEY WORD
Three points are for varied, formal sentences: join two short facts with because, but, so or when, and let one short sentence land after a long one. Three points are for editing. The check: find the word the prompt is about, here perseverance, and read every copy of it back letter by letter. Then read one sentence from each body paragraph aloud in your head: if they all have the same shape, join two.
YOU WROTE
“Wilma Rudolph perservered…”   “Her perserverance…”   “…got a illness…”   “…won three gold medals running.”
The prompt spells perseverance for you, twice. Copy the key word from the prompt rather than from memory. An before a vowel. And three gold medals in track and field is the passage’s phrase.
YOU WROTE (BODY PARAGRAPH 3, THE SHAPE OF THE SENTENCES)
“At the Olympics Wilma won three gold medals running. She was the first American woman to win that many medals at a single Olympics. Because of this she became a symbol of strength.” THREE SENTENCES, ONE SHAPE
Subject, verb, fact. Subject, verb, fact. Correct every time, and the rubric’s three sentence points go to variety.
THE SAME FACTS, TWO SHAPES
When Wilma won three gold medals in track and field at the 1960 Olympics in Rome, she became the first American woman to win that many at a single Games, and the girl in the brace became a symbol of strength. The brace was gone for good.

Three ways to vary a sentence, and one that only lengthens it:

START WITH WHEN OR AFTERAfter years of exercises at home, Wilma took off the brace at twelve.
JOIN WITH BUTDoctors said she would never walk again, but her mother never gave up hope.
A SHORT ONE AFTER A LONG ONEShe trained through school and college until she was one of the fastest women alive. Then she ran in Rome.
ONLY LONGERWilma won three gold medals and she was the first American woman to do that and she became a symbol of strength.
Three facts and two ands. Longer, not varied.
