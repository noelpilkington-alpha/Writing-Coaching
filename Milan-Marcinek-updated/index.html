Q3 Fix — Choosing the Right Conjunction

First: can you spot which conjunction fits? Then we'll break it down.
📈 You got "because" right on G6.1 but missed "but" on G6.2 — so you understand conjunctions, but telling apart cause vs. contrast is the sticking point. This same skill helps your essay writing: choosing the right connecting word makes your arguments clearer.

The question asked: "Which sentence combines the ideas using a logical conjunction?"

The two ideas: "Animated movies are fun." + "They also include valuable lessons."

These are two different ideas. Being fun and having lessons are not the same thing — lessons are an unexpected addition to something fun.

Which conjunction shows that lessons are a surprising addition to being fun?
A "Animated movies are fun because they include valuable lessons."
B "Animated movies are fun, but they also include valuable lessons."

Now here's the full picture of when to use each conjunction:

Conjunction What It Means Example
because Cause → Effect (one thing causes the other) "I stayed inside because it was raining."
but Contrast / Unexpected addition "The movie is short but it tells a powerful story."
so Result (one thing leads to the other) "It was raining, so I stayed inside."
THE RULE
Before choosing a conjunction, ask: What is the relationship between the ideas?
• Does one idea cause the other? → because
• Does one idea contrast or add something unexpected? → but
• Does one idea result from the other? → so
See another example to practice
"The dog was tired ___ it kept playing."
Ask: Is being tired and keeping playing a cause? No — it's a contrast. The dog kept playing despite being tired.
Answer: "but" — "The dog was tired but it kept playing."
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER FOR YOUR ESSAYS?
Choosing the right conjunction isn't just a multiple choice skill — it makes your essay arguments clearer. When you connect ideas with the wrong word, the reader gets confused about whether you're showing a cause, a contrast, or a result. Getting this right helps every paragraph flow better.

Signal phrases to use: "According to the passage..." / "The passage explains that..." / "The author states..." / "As described in the text..."

When you use passage evidence instead of personal stories, you show that you can read a text and pull out the important information to support your argument. That's exactly what the grading feedback is looking for — and it's the difference between good ideas and a high-scoring essay.

The Body Paragraph Pattern

First: can you spot what's missing? Then we'll build the full formula.
📈 This is part of a pattern across your last 2 tests. The structure of your writing is the consistent difference-maker.

Two students wrote body paragraphs about courage in animated movies. Which one would score higher?

Tap the body paragraph that follows the correct pattern:
A "Animated movies help children learn about courage. In Brave, Merida stands up for what she believes in. Many animated characters face scary problems. These movies are important for kids."
B "Animated movies help children learn about courage. In Brave, Merida stands up for what she believes in, even when others disagree. This shows kids that being brave means doing what's right, not what's easy. Many animated characters face scary problems but find their courage and try again. This inspires young viewers to keep trying, even when things get hard. Through these stories, children learn that courage is about facing challenges, not avoiding them."

Every body paragraph in a G6 essay should follow this pattern:

Body Paragraph Formula
Topic Sentence States the paragraph's main point
Evidence A specific fact, quote, or example from the passage
Explain why Why does that evidence matter? ("So what?")
More Evidence A second fact or example from the passage
Explain why Why does THIS evidence matter?
Closing Sentence Wraps up the paragraph's main idea

Here's a full example body paragraph about courage using passage evidence:

EXAMPLE: BODY PARAGRAPH ABOUT COURAGE
[Topic Sentence] Animated movies help children learn about courage.
[Evidence] In Brave, Merida stands up for what she believes in, even when others disagree.
[Explain why] This shows kids that being brave means doing what's right, not what's easy. (So what? Why does Merida's choice matter?)
[More Evidence] Many animated characters face scary problems but find their courage and try again.
[Explain why] This inspires young viewers to keep trying, even when things get hard. (So what? Why does trying again matter?)
[Closing Sentence] Through these stories, children learn that courage is about facing challenges, not avoiding them.
THE KEY RULE: "SO WHAT?"
Every piece of Evidence must be followed by an Explanation. Evidence without explanation is just a list of facts. After each piece of evidence, ask yourself: "So what? Why does this matter?" — the answer IS your explanation.
Why this matters for your score
The grading feedback looks specifically for evidence + explanation pairs. If you list two facts without explaining why they matter, you get fewer points than listing one fact and explaining it well. Quality of explanation beats quantity of facts every time.
