Proofread Before You Submit

A 60-second habit worth real points

Nathan, five small slips on this test cost you real points. None of them are because you don't know the right word — they are all the kind of thing a quick proofread catches. Here they are:

Actives like setting up → Activities like setting up
stregthen family relationships → strengthen family relationships
their is always a solution → there is always a solution
helped Jaylen fell better → helped Jaylen feel better
their is no worry → there is no worry
TWO QUICK PATTERNS TO WATCH
their / there / they're — this one showed up twice:
• their = belongs to them ("their car")
• there = a place OR introduces a fact ("there is always a solution")
• they're = they are

fell vs feel — easy to slip past:
• fell = past tense of fall ("I fell down")
• feel = to sense something ("it helped Jaylen feel better")
Which sentence is fully polished?
A The backyard camping trip helped Jaylen fell better, because their is always another way to have fun.
B The backyard camping trip helped Jaylen feel better, because there is always another way to have fun.
THE 60-SECOND FINAL PASS
When you finish a paragraph, before you submit, do this:

1. Read it out loud in your head, one sentence at a time
2. At each sentence, ask: does every word look right? Does it sound right?
3. Run a quick check on every their / there and every fell / feel
4. Look at any word that seems too short or too long — "Actives" and "stregthen" both look slightly off if you stop on them

Sixty seconds. Catches almost every slip on the list above.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Two of these slips were inside your Q11 paragraph ("their is no worry") and two more were inside sentence items that were otherwise correct (Q7, Q8, Q10). Zero slips on your next test is worth at least one full point of raw score — and it is the kind of improvement that sticks for every future test, not just this one.

Put the Appositive on the Right Noun

The Q1 move that cost half a point

Nathan, Q1 is worth looking at carefully because you lost half a point on a question that was completely within your skill range. Let's look at what happened:

The question asked
Revise sentence 5 by adding an appositive to give more detail about Willow State Park.
WHAT YOU WROTE
The Thompsons, a family of campers, camp at Willow State Park every summer.

The sentence is grammatically fine, and "a family of campers" is a correct appositive. The problem is that the prompt asked you to describe Willow State Park, and your appositive describes the Thompsons. Same move, wrong noun.

THE APPOSITIVE RULE
An appositive is a noun phrase that renames or gives detail about the noun right next to it. So if the prompt says "add an appositive about X", the appositive has to sit next to X, describing X.

The question here points at Willow State Park, so your appositive has to come right after those words:
• "The Thompsons camp at Willow State Park, a quiet wooded campground along the lake, every summer."
• "The Thompsons camp at Willow State Park, a family-friendly state park in the mountains, every summer."
The prompt says: add an appositive that gives more detail about Willow State Park. Which sentence actually does that?
A The Thompsons, a family of campers, camp at Willow State Park every summer.
B The Thompsons camp at Willow State Park, a peaceful campground surrounded by pine forest, every summer.
The "Point at the Right Noun" Routine
STEP 1 Read the prompt and find the exact noun the appositive is supposed to describe
STEP 2 Write the sentence, then put a comma right after that noun
STEP 3 Drop in your description (2–6 words is usually right), then close with another comma
STEP 4 Re-read and check: does my appositive give more detail about the same noun the prompt named?
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Appositives show up on almost every G5 writing test. You already know the move — the Thompsons sentence proves it. All you are adding is the habit of checking which noun the prompt is pointing at. Lock that in and Q1-style questions become automatic full credit.

POLISHED PARAGRAPH

VR field trips are a good idea for schools because they let students take amazing field trips with lower cost and fewer safety worries. Lower cost is a big benefit: normal field trips are very expensive, but a VR trip can be done for only $300 instead of $1,000. Safety is another benefit. Before VR, teachers had to worry about where every student was and spend time rounding them all up, but with VR there is almost no safety risk at all. To sum it up, VR helps teachers plan better, safer, cheaper lessons for every student.

WHAT YOU DID WELL
• You opened with a one-sentence claim that names both reasons — that is textbook topic-sentence work
• You gave a concrete number from the passage ($300 vs $1,000)
• You used "because" to link reasons to evidence, the exact move Q2, Q4, and Q6 asked for
• You closed with a sum-up sentence that names the real benefit (better lessons for teachers)
