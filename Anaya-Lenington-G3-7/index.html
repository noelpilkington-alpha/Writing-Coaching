Appositive = Naming Phrase

Q4 — 1 out of 2
📝
What happened on Q4: The question asked you to add an appositive about bees. You wrote a participial phrase ("carrying pollen") instead of a naming phrase. A participial phrase describes an action. An appositive renames or identifies the noun.
FROM G3.7 Q4 — YOUR ANSWER
"Bees buzz from flower to flower, carrying pollen."

"Carrying pollen" describes what bees are doing. That's a participial phrase (an action phrase). An appositive tells us what bees are. It renames or identifies the noun with a naming phrase set off by commas.

Tap to see the appositive version
Same sentence, correct grammar structure
WITH AN APPOSITIVE (NAMING PHRASE)

"Bees, important pollinators in the garden, buzz from flower to flower."

What changed: "Important pollinators in the garden" renames what bees ARE. It's a naming phrase set off by commas. That's an appositive.

THE QUICK TEST
Try replacing the phrase with "which is" or "who is." If it makes sense, it's an appositive. "Bees, which are important pollinators..." works. But "Bees, which are carrying pollen..." sounds like an action description, not a renaming.
Which sentence contains an appositive (naming phrase) about bees?
A Bees, buzzing loudly, moved between the flowers.
B Bees, nature's tiny helpers, spread pollen from plant to plant.
C Bees, searching for nectar, visited every flower in the field.
REMEMBER
Appositive = naming phrase. It answers "What is this thing?" not "What is this thing doing?" When the test asks for an appositive, write a phrase that could follow "which is" or "who is" naturally.

Spelling + Grammar Fixes

Q7 run-on, Q8 subject-verb, Q9 spelling

Anaya, you made three different types of errors across Q7, Q8, and Q9. Each one is easy to fix once you see the pattern.

Error 1: Subject-Verb Agreement (Q8 — 2 out of 3)
WRONG "Something that changes as the pumpkins grows is that it turns from flower to pumpkin."
RIGHT "Something that changes as the pumpkin grows is that it turns from flower to pumpkin."
THE RULE
If your subject is singular ("the pumpkin"), the verb gets an "s" ("grows"). If the subject is plural ("the pumpkins"), the verb drops the "s" ("grow"). You can't have both with "s" at the same time. Quick check: "the pumpkin grows" or "the pumpkins grow."
Error 2: Spelling — "backed" should be "baked" (Q9 — 2 out of 3)
WRONG "People can use pumpkins after they are picked for backed snacks."
RIGHT "People can use pumpkins after they are picked for baked snacks."
MEMORY TRICK
"Baked" comes from "bake." Drop the "e," add "-ed": bak + ed = baked. "Backed" comes from "back" (like backing up). When you mean cooking in an oven, the word is always baked.
Error 3: Run-on sentence (Q7 — 2 out of 3)
WRONG "Bees help pumpkins grow, because they gather pollen from flowers while going to each one they grow pumpkins."
RIGHT "Bees help pumpkins grow because they carry pollen from one flower to another. This allows each flower to turn into a pumpkin."
THE FIX
When your sentence starts getting long and confusing, split it into two shorter sentences. Each sentence should have one clear idea. If you can't read your sentence aloud in one breath, it's too long.
Which sentence has correct grammar and makes sense?
A As the pumpkins grows, people pick them for backed snacks.

C As the pumpkin grows people pick it because they want to make baked snacks while it is still growing on the vine they pick it.
PROOFREAD CHECKLIST
Before you submit, check for these three things: 1) Read each sentence aloud. If it sounds too long or confusing, split it. 2) Find every subject-verb pair and make sure they match (singular with singular, plural with plural). 3) Sound out any word that looks off. "Backed" and "baked" sound different when you say them.

Add Depth to Your Paragraph

Q11: 17 out of 20 — good, needs more depth
📝
Why 17 out of 20? Your paragraph has a good topic sentence and is well organized. To reach 19 or 20, you need more depth in your supporting details. Each detail from the passage needs an explanation that tells why it matters or what it shows.

Anaya, your paragraph about pumpkins/learning to cook has a clear main idea. The next step is adding an extra sentence after each detail that explains what that detail shows or why it matters to the reader.

The Detail + Explanation Pattern
DETAIL A specific fact or example from the passage (names, numbers, what happened).
EXPLAIN Why it matters or what it shows about your main idea. This is the sentence that earns extra points.
EXAMPLE FROM THE PUMPKIN PASSAGE
Detail: "Pumpkin seeds need warm soil before they can sprout."
Explanation: "This shows that growing pumpkins takes patience because farmers have to wait for the right temperature before they can even start planting."

The explanation connects the fact (warm soil needed) to a bigger idea (growing pumpkins requires patience). Without it, the reader just has a fact sitting there.
Which version adds real depth with the Detail + Explanation pattern?
A Pumpkins grow from seeds. The seeds need warm soil. Bees help the flowers. Then pumpkins grow big and orange. People pick them in fall.
B Growing pumpkins is a long process that depends on many things going right. For example, bees must carry pollen from one pumpkin flower to another. This step is important because without bees doing this work, the flowers would never turn into pumpkins at all.
YOUR DEVELOPMENT CHECK
After you write each detail sentence, ask yourself: "So what? Why does this matter?" Then write one more sentence that answers that question. That's your explanation. Do this for at least two details in your paragraph and you'll reach 19 or 20 out of 20.
