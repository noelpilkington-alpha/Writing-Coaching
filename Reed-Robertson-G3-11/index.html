Develop the Paragraph with Real Detail

Question 11 — your biggest chance to earn points back

Reed, your paragraph on butterflies had the right shape — a topic sentence, some middle steps, and a closing. But the middle named the stages without explaining the actual changes, and a couple of the facts slipped. Here is what you wrote:

YOU WROTE (G3.11 Q11, paragraph scored 13 out of 20)
"A caterpillar's body goes through many important stages to turn into a butterfly. First, the egg has to hatch a caterpillar. Then, the caterpillar must grow into a shell. After, the caterpillar turns into a butterfly. These are the important stages a caterpillar's body must go through to turn into a butterfly."

The reader cannot tell what really happens at each step. "Grow into a shell" is vague, and the closing just repeats the opening. The passage gave you real details — use them.

Which middle sentence earns more paragraph points?
A Then the caterpillar goes through its next stage and changes.
B The caterpillar eats leaves and grows quickly, then forms a hard case called a chrysalis around itself.
NAME THE CHANGE, DON'T JUST NAME THE STAGE
A weak sentence says what stage comes next. A strong sentence says what actually changes in that stage and uses a detail from the passage.

✗ "The caterpillar must grow into a shell." (vague, and not quite right)
✓ "The caterpillar forms a hard case called a chrysalis, and inside it the body reorganizes into a butterfly." (specific, real change)

Combining with an Appositive

Question 2 — a comma alone cannot join two sentences

Reed, Question 2 asked you to combine two sentences using an appositive — a phrase that renames a noun, set off with commas. Your answer joined the ideas with just a comma instead, which leaves two complete sentences stuck together. Here is what you wrote:

YOU WROTE (G3.11 Q2, scored 1 out of 2)
"Hundreds of penguins stand close together, they form a big group called a huddle."

"Hundreds of penguins stand close together" is a complete sentence. "They form a big group called a huddle" is also a complete sentence. A single comma cannot hold two complete sentences together. An appositive fixes this by renaming the noun instead of starting a whole new sentence.

Which version correctly combines the sentences with an appositive?
A Hundreds of penguins stand close together, they form a big group called a huddle.
B Hundreds of penguins, a group called a huddle, stand close together.
THE RULE
An appositive renames a noun and sits between two commas. It does not start a new sentence — it slides extra information into the one you already have.

✗ "Hundreds of penguins stand close together, they form a huddle." (two complete sentences, only a comma)
✓ "Hundreds of penguins, a group called a huddle, stand close together." (appositive renames the noun)
IF YOU ARE NOT USING AN APPOSITIVE
When two complete thoughts are not being combined into one, end the first with a period and start the second as its own sentence: "Hundreds of penguins stand close together. They form a big group called a huddle." That is also correct — just not the appositive the question asked for.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
This is a small, exact fix. When a question asks you to combine with an appositive, rename the noun with commas instead of joining two sentences with one comma. That habit earns the second point on questions like this one.

MODEL POLISHED PARAGRAPH (full-credit level)

A caterpillar's body goes through several big changes to become a butterfly. First, the egg hatches and a tiny caterpillar comes out. The caterpillar eats leaves and grows bigger and bigger. Next, it forms a hard case called a chrysalis around itself, and inside the case its body slowly reorganizes. Finally, the chrysalis opens and a butterfly comes out with colorful wings, ready to fly. Each stage changes the caterpillar's body a little more until it is no longer a caterpillar at all.
