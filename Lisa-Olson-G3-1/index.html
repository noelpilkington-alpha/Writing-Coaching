THE SUBJECT + VERB CHECK
Every sentence needs two pieces:
• A subject — the person or thing doing something (I, people, dogs, my family)
• A verb — the action or state-of-being (is, are, should, walks, eats)

If your answer starts with "Swimming..." or "A dog park..." or "Exercise it...", ask yourself: who is doing the swimming? What is a dog park? Who should exercise it? Add that piece at the front.
THE RESTATE TRICK
When a prompt asks "What is your favorite thing to do with a pet?", start your answer by restating the question:

• "My favorite thing to do with a pet is ___."
• "One place dogs should be allowed is ___."
• "One rule people should follow is ___."

Restating forces a subject and verb into your answer every time. It is the single fastest way to turn fragments into full sentences.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Three questions worth 3 points each lost you around 4-5 points total because of fragments. On a 45-point test, that is almost 10% of your score. Fix this one habit and you jump from 73% toward 83% — with the exact same ideas you already had.

• Usage — do the words fit together (use to vs used to)?

• Spelling — do the tricky words look right?

Fix #3 — What an Appositive Actually Is

A tiny grammar move that shows up on almost every writing test

From Q1.

Q1 asked you to combine two sentences into one using an appositive. The original sentences were about a family with two dogs named Lucy and Rocket.

Which sentence uses an appositive?
A My family has two dogs, and their names are Lucy and Rocket.
B My family has two dogs, Lucy and Rocket.
FROM Q1 — YOU WROTE
"Me and my family has two dogs and they names are Lucy and Rocket."

Two things to notice here:

  1. "and they names are" turns this into a compound sentence, not an appositive. The question specifically asked for an appositive.
  2. Two small grammar slips: "Me and my family has" should be "My family and I have," and "they names" should be "their names."
APPOSITIVE VERSION
"My family has two dogs, Lucy and Rocket."
WHAT IS AN APPOSITIVE?
An appositive is a noun or short phrase that renames another noun right next to it. It gives the reader more information about the noun — like a nickname or a label.

The formula is simple:
• Noun, short phrase that renames the noun.

Examples:
• "My dog, Rocket, loves to run." — "Rocket" renames "dog"
• "I have a pet, a small hamster." — "a small hamster" renames "pet"
• "We live in Austin, a city in Texas." — renames "Austin"
APPOSITIVE vs CONJUNCTION
If you find yourself writing "and," "but," or "their names are" in the middle — stop. That is a conjunction, not an appositive.

Take those words out. Put a comma where the conjunction was. What is left on the right side of the comma? If it renames the noun before the comma, you just built an appositive.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Appositives come up on basically every writing test at G3 and above. Once you see the pattern — noun, renaming noun — you will catch it every time. One small move, guaranteed 2 points.
