Naming Phrases vs Comma Splices

Q3 — 1 out of 2 weighted points

Quentin, on Q3 you needed to use a naming phrase (a short phrase that renames or describes a noun). Instead, you joined two complete sentences with a comma. That is called a comma splice, and it cost you a point.

Q3 — YOUR ANSWER (1 out of 2)
"Sally swam to the other seals, the other seals are here parents."

Two problems:

  • Comma splice: "Sally swam to the other seals" is a complete idea. "The other seals are her parents" is another complete idea. Joining them with just a comma creates a comma splice.
  • Spelling: "here" should be "her" (her parents, not here parents).

The question wanted you to add a naming phrase after "seals" to describe who they are. A naming phrase sits between commas and adds detail without creating a new sentence:

A STRONGER ANSWER USING A NAMING PHRASE
"Sally swam to the other seals, her parents, and they welcomed her back."
HOW A NAMING PHRASE WORKS
A naming phrase renames or describes the noun right before it. It sits between two commas:

• Sally swam to the seals, her family, who were waiting nearby.
• The zookeeper, a kind woman named Dr. Lee, helped Sally heal.

The test: Can you remove the phrase between the commas and still have a complete sentence? If yes, it is a naming phrase. If what comes after the comma is a whole new sentence, you have a comma splice.
Which sentence uses a naming phrase correctly?
A The family found Sally, she was very small.
B The family found Sally, a tiny baby seal, on the beach.
REMEMBER
A naming phrase describes the noun. A comma splice adds a whole new sentence. If what comes after the comma could stand alone as its own sentence, you need a period instead.

Use the Right Connector

Q5 — 1 out of 2 weighted points

Quentin, on Q5 you chose the wrong connecting word and also created another comma splice. Two errors in one sentence cost you a point.

Q5 — YOUR ANSWER (1 out of 2)
"However there was no sign of the seals, the family placed Sally in a fish box..."

Two problems:

  • Wrong connector: "However" means "but" or "on the other hand." The sentence is giving a reason: because there was no sign of the seals, the family did something. The right word is "Since" (meaning "because").
  • Comma splice: After you state the reason, you joined a whole new idea with just a comma. You need a comma after the reason clause, but the structure must be: "Since [reason], [result]."
A STRONGER ANSWER
"Since there was no sign of the other seals, the family placed Sally in a fish box to keep her safe."
CHOOSING THE RIGHT CONNECTOR
Read what the sentence needs before picking a word:

• "However" = contrast, surprise, something unexpected. ("She studied hard. However, she failed the test.")

• "Since" or "Because" = the reason something happened. ("Since no one was there, the family had to act.")

• "Therefore" = the result of what happened before. ("The seals were gone. Therefore, the family took Sally home.")

Ask yourself: Is this a reason, a contrast, or a result? Then pick the connector that matches.
The seals were not nearby. The family decided to take Sally home. Which connector fits?
A However the seals were not nearby, the family decided to take Sally home.
B Since the seals were not nearby, the family decided to take Sally home.
THE PAYOFF
Q5 was worth 2 points and you earned 1. Picking the right connector and avoiding the comma splice would have earned the full 2. Before you use a connector, ask: is this a reason, a contrast, or a result?

Run-On Sentences

Q7 — 2 out of 3 weighted points

Quentin, on Q7 you wrote two complete ideas as one sentence with no punctuation between them. This is called a run-on sentence, and it cost you a point.

Q7 — YOUR ANSWER (2 out of 3)
"The most meaningful moment in the story is when they first decide to help the baby seal this shows that they care about..."

The problem: "The most meaningful moment...is when they first decide to help the baby seal" is one complete idea. "This shows that they care about..." is a second complete idea. They are fused together with no period, no comma, nothing.

THE FIX: ADD A PERIOD
"The most meaningful moment in the story is when they first decide to help the baby seal. This shows that they care about animals and want to do the right thing."
THE RULE: TWO COMPLETE IDEAS NEED A PERIOD
If you can put a period between two parts and both parts make sense on their own, they are two sentences. Give each one its own period.

Test it:
• "The most meaningful moment is when they decide to help the baby seal." ← Complete? Yes.
• "This shows that they care about animals." ← Complete? Yes.

Both are complete, so they need a period between them. Never run two complete ideas together without punctuation.
Which version correctly separates two complete ideas?
A The family found Sally on the rocks they knew she needed help.
B The family found Sally on the rocks. They knew she needed help.
YOUR HABIT TO BUILD
After you write an answer, look for the word "this" in the middle of your sentence. If "this shows" or "this means" appears without a period before it, you probably have a run-on. Add the period.

Detail + Explanation

Q8 and Q11 — adding depth to your answers

Quentin, on Q8 and Q11 you had good ideas but did not develop them enough. Both answers needed more explanation to earn full points.

Q8 — YOUR ANSWER (2 out of 3)
"Yes naming the seal gave it charecter."

Two problems:

  • Spelling: "charecter" should be "character".

A STRONGER ANSWER

On Q11 (your paragraph at 16 out of 20), you have good structure but your ideas need more development. When you make a point, add one more sentence explaining why it matters or what it shows.

THE "SO WHAT?" TEST
After you write a point, ask yourself: "So what? Why does that matter?"

• "Naming the seal gave it character." → So what? It made the family care about her as a living being, not just an animal they found.

• Write that "so what" answer as your next sentence. That is how you develop an idea.
