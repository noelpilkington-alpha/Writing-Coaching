Q3 Fix — Combine Sentences

Can you spot the difference? Then we'll break it down.
📈 Combine Sentences started strong at 92% (G5.1–G5.2) but has been slipping to 62% (G5.3–G5.4). The pattern each time: how you join two ideas with the same subject. You scored 100% on G5.1 and G5.2, so you can do this — let's figure out what's changed.

The question asked you to combine: "She sat in the 'colored' section." and "She refused to move farther back."

YOUR ANSWER
"She sat in the colored section, and refused to move back."

The issue: when the same person is the subject of both actions, putting a comma before "and" suggests a new clause is coming — but then there's no new subject. This makes the combination incomplete. Which version fixes it?

Tap the correctly combined sentence:
A "She sat in the colored section, and refused to move farther back."
B "She sat in the 'colored' section and refused to move farther back."

Here's the rule:

Same Subject, Two Actions Two Different Subjects
"She sat in her seat and refused to move."
No comma — same person doing both things		 "She sat in her seat, and the driver called the police."
Comma — different people doing different things
THE QUICK TEST
Before you add a comma before "and," ask: Is there a new subject after "and"?
• Same subject does both actions → no comma ("She walked in and sat down.")
• Different subject after "and" → comma ("She walked in, and he stood up.")
See more examples
No comma (same subject):
"Colvin stood her ground and refused to leave."
"The driver stopped the bus and radioed for help."

Comma (different subjects):
"Colvin stood her ground, and the driver called the police."
"The bus stopped, and the officers came aboard."

Body Paragraph Building Blocks (use for each body paragraph)
Topic Sentence States the paragraph's main point — one clear reason or argument
Evidence A specific fact, quote, or example from the passage
Explain Why Why does that evidence matter? ("So what?")
More Evidence A second fact or example from the passage

Closing Sentence Wraps up the paragraph's main idea

At this point, spelling is the main thing separating 80% from 95%. When you use a word multiple times (like "emissions"), one misspelling gets counted each time it appears.
Quick trick: the "key word" check
Before you submit, scan your essay for topic-specific words — words related to the passage's main subject (like "emissions," "trains," "transportation"). These are the words most likely to be misspelled. Circle them and double-check each one.
3-MINUTE PROOFREADING RULE
Save the last 3 minutes for proofreading. Read your essay backward (last sentence first) — reading backward forces you to see each word on its own instead of auto-correcting in your head.
