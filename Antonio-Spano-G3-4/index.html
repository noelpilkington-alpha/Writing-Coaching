Answer BOTH Parts of Two-Part Prompts

Q6–Q10 — the prompt often asks two things in one

Antonio, this one is pure lost points. On G3.4 Question 10, the prompt asked two things. You nailed the first. You didn't answer the second. Here's what you wrote:

YOUR G3.4 Q10 ANSWER (2 of 3 points)
"One rule that people should follow when visiting natural places is that you shouldn't feed any harmful foods to the animals."
💡
The prompt was TWO parts: "What is one rule people should follow? Why is it important?" You answered the what perfectly. You didn't answer the why. That's 1 point lost on a 3-point question.

This is a pattern worth watching every time on Q6–Q10. These are 3-point single-sentence questions, and the prompt often has two parts joined by the word "and" or a second question mark. Miss the second part and you're capped at 2 of 3 — no matter how good the first part is.

How to Spot a Two-Part Prompt
CLUE The word "and" appears between two question words. Example: "What is one rule and why is it important?"
CLUE There are two question marks in the prompt. Example: "What is one rule? Why is it important?"
CLUE You see two question words (what, why, how, who). If you see both "what" AND "why," that's two parts.
THE ONE-SENTENCE FIX
Answer both parts in ONE sentence joined by "because" or "which is important because".

Formula: [Answer to Part 1], because [Answer to Part 2].
Prompt: "What is one rule people should follow when visiting natural places, and why is it important?" Which answer earns full credit (3 of 3)?
A One rule is that you shouldn't feed harmful foods to the animals.
B One rule is that you shouldn't feed harmful foods to the animals, because it can make them sick and change how they act in the wild.

Evidence Sandwich — Turn Retelling Into Analysis

Paragraph (Q11) — how 17/20 becomes 18–19/20

Your structure is solid (17/20 across 4 tests — that's a real ceiling). To push to 18–19/20, add one "this shows" or "which means" sentence after each supporting detail. That turns retelling into analysis.

Right now your supporting details retell what the passage said. A higher-scoring paragraph uses the same details but adds one sentence of your own thinking after each one. This is called an Evidence Sandwich.

The Evidence Sandwich
TOP Set up the detail: One sentence that introduces what's coming. "The rules of modern sports were created in Britain."
MIDDLE The detail itself: The fact from the passage, either paraphrased or pulled out. "Rules showed what was allowed and what was not allowed."
BOTTOM "This shows..." or "Which means...": One sentence of analysis — your own thinking about WHY that detail matters. "This shows that sports became fair for everyone who played."
Paraphrasing: Putting It in Your Own Words
DoDo Not
Change the words but keep the meaning. "Factory workers found their jobs boring and wanted to play sports instead." Copy the passage word-for-word. That's not your own writing, and it does not show your thinking.
Show you understood by explaining the idea in your own words. Skip the detail entirely and make a general claim with no passage evidence behind it.
Quoting: Pulling a Short Phrase From the Passage
DoDo Not
Use a short phrase from the passage when the exact words matter. Paraphrasing is always fine too — quotes are not required. Feel like you have to quote. Paraphrasing in your own words is just as strong (often stronger) on a 3rd-grade paragraph.
Follow any pulled phrase with your own "this shows..." analysis sentence. End with a detail. Always add one more sentence explaining why that detail matters.

✍️
Small proofread slips are 1–2 points per test. Fixing them is the easiest gain on your whole test — no new skill needed. Just one last careful read.
Proper Nouns — Always Capitalize
COUNTRY Britain, France, Mexico, Canada, the United States — always capital letters.
CITY London, Austin, Rome, Tokyo — always capital letters.
NAME Layla, Antonio, Sally — people's names always start with a capital letter.
Their / There / They're — Quick Guide
their Belongs to them. "The workers thought their work was boring."
there A place, or the word that starts "there is/there are." "The sports started over there."
they're Short for "they are." "They're playing cricket right now."
Which sentence has the proper-noun slip?
A Cricket and golf started in britain in the 1700s.
B Layla picked up the flower and smiled.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
These slips aren't skill gaps — they're proofread gaps. One careful read at the end of your paragraph catches them all. Country names, city names, people's names, and homophones like their/there/they're. That's 1–2 points per test, the easiest points on the entire exam.
