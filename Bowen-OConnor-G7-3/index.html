Proofread Before You Submit

Three small errors in G7.3 — all catchable in a 2-minute pass

Bowen, your G7.3 essay had three small errors that a proofreading pass would have caught. None of them are about "not knowing" the rules — you know all three. They slip in when you are drafting and never get re-read. Here they are:

ERROR 1 — WRONG VERB FORM
"the language begun with La Gomera's first inhabitants"
"Begun" needs a helper verb (has begun, had begun). Standing alone as the past tense of "begin," the correct form is began.
FIXED
"the language began with La Gomera's first inhabitants"
ERROR 2 — PLURAL SPELLING
"This helped communitys stay together even when they lived far apart."
Words ending in consonant + y change to -ies in the plural. And the passage itself uses "communities" — so glancing at the passage while proofreading catches this one for free.
FIXED
"This helped communities stay together even when they lived far apart."
ERROR 3 — A/AN BEFORE A VOWEL SOUND
"El Silbo is a important part of La Gomera's identity."
"Important" starts with a vowel sound, so it needs an, not a. Classic drafting slip.
FIXED
"El Silbo is an important part of La Gomera's identity."
Which proofreading pass catches all three of these errors fastest?
A Read the essay silently from start to finish, looking for anything that feels off.
B Read each sentence out loud one at a time and cross-check spellings against the passage.
THE 2-MINUTE PROOFREAD CHECKLIST
Before you submit, run one pass for each of these:
• Verb forms. Past tense of every action verb — is it the simple past (began, saw, went) or a compound form (has begun, has seen, had gone)? If it is alone, use simple past.
• Key vocab from the passage. Look at how the passage spells words you borrowed. "Communities," "inhabitants," "cultural" — mirror the source.
• A vs. An. Scan every "a" and "an." Does the next word start with a vowel sound? (Not letter — sound. "An hour" has an "a," but it sounds like "owr.")
WHY THIS MATTERS
Three small errors = one conventions point — and the difference between 17/20 and 18/20. Conventions is the cheapest point on the rubric to earn because you already know the rules. You just need 2 minutes of re-reading at the end.

Your #1 Growth: Deeper Analysis

"This shows..." is the start of a great sentence — not the end

Bowen, you already use the evidence-plus-analysis move. Every body paragraph has a "This shows..." or "This proves..." sentence right after the evidence. That is exactly what a 17/20 essay does. Here is what a 19 or 20 essay does differently: the analysis sentence answers a deeper "why" instead of restating the evidence in new words.

🔍
The formulaic trap. "This shows that El Silbo is a part of their culture still today" is true, but it just restates the evidence (Cyro learning El Silbo = El Silbo still taught). The grader is waiting for the next layer: because why? What does Cyro learning it mean for the island's identity?
The Two Layers of an Analysis Sentence
LAYER 1 What the evidence shows (fact restated). "This shows El Silbo is still taught."
LAYER 2 Why that matters for the thesis (deeper meaning). "This means that a language older than modern Spain survives not by accident but because each generation actively chooses to keep it alive."

Here is one of your own sentences shown at both layers:

LAYER 1 ONLY (what you wrote — partial credit)
"Thanks to the government, El Silbo is a required class, and people like Cyro are proud to learn it. This shows that El Silbo is a part of their culture still today."
The analysis sentence just restates the evidence. Cyro = proud = still today. True, but shallow.
LAYER 1 + LAYER 2 (what earns full credit)
"Thanks to the government, El Silbo is a required class, and people like Cyro are proud to learn it. This shows that El Silbo is still part of La Gomera's culture today — but more importantly, it means the island's identity no longer depends on isolated elders passing the whistle down by chance. Because the government made it required, every child inherits the language, which turns a fragile tradition into something the whole community actively protects."
Same evidence. Same "This shows..." start. But now the follow-up answers why it matters for the thesis about identity.
Which follow-up sentence is real analysis (Layer 2), not just restatement?
A This shows that the whistles travel far, so people can hear them from a long way off.
B This proves that El Silbo was not just a cultural habit but a practical tool: in terrain where shouting fails, a whistle that carries two miles let villagers share warnings, news, and help in real time, binding the island together.
THE "BECAUSE X, THIS MEANS..." MOVE
After every "This shows..." sentence, try adding a second sentence that starts with one of these:

• "Because X, this means..."
• "More importantly, this reveals..."
• "What makes this significant is..."
• "In other words, ___ tells us that..."

These forcing phrases make your brain push one layer deeper instead of restating the fact.
CROSS-CHECK BACK TO THE PASSAGE
Before you write the analysis sentence, re-read the exact line in the passage that gave you the evidence. Ask: "What does the author want me to take from this detail?" The answer is your Layer 2.

Example: the passage says El Silbo "was later used by Spanish settlers." You picked that fact. Before analyzing, re-read it. The author is showing you the language crossed cultures. That is your Layer 2 — not just "it has been around a long time."
WHY THIS MATTERS
You already have 3 body paragraphs with evidence and a "This shows..." sentence. Adding one Layer 2 sentence per body = 3 extra sentences of real analysis in your essay. On the Ideas row (8/10), that is worth +1 to +2 points. Paired with the proofread pass, that is the jump from 17/20 to 19 or 20 — and 85% to 90%+.
