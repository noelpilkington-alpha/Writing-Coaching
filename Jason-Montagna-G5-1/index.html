G5.1 Q8 — RESTATES SCENARIO (SCORED 1 OUT OF 3)
The prompt asked: How might the story have changed without the coffee spill scene?
You wrote: "The story might have changed because without the coffee spill scene there would be no main conflict to drive the plot."
This says what would be missing ("no main conflict") but does not explain HOW the story would actually play out differently. A grader needs you to describe the change: what would happen instead? How would the characters act differently? What ending would you get?

Jason, the difference between naming the gap and describing the change is this: naming the gap says "something would be missing." Describing the change says "here is what would happen instead, and here is why that matters." The second version earns full credit because it shows you understand cause and effect in the story.

HOW TO ANSWER "HOW WOULD IT CHANGE?"
✓ "Without the coffee spill, Crista would never have been put in a position where she had to choose between telling the truth and hiding her mistake. The story would probably end without her learning the lesson about owning up, because there would be nothing to own up to."

This version names the specific change (Crista never faces the choice), explains the result (no lesson learned), and connects it to the theme (owning up). That is HOW, not just WHAT.
Which answer actually describes HOW the story changes?
A The story might have changed because without the coffee spill there would be no main conflict to drive the plot.
B Without the coffee spill, Crista would never face the choice of owning up or staying quiet, so the story would end without her learning Ms. Kessler's lesson about honesty.
THE "HOW" FORMULA
When a question asks "How would X change?" your answer needs three parts:

1. Name the cause: "Without the coffee spill..."
2. Describe the effect: "...Crista would never face the choice of owning up..."
3. Connect it to the bigger picture: "...so the story would end without her learning the lesson."

If your answer only does step 1 (names the cause), you are answering WHAT is missing. Steps 2 and 3 are where the HOW lives.
THE SELF-CHECK: "DID I DESCRIBE A CHANGE?"
After writing your answer, ask yourself: "Did I describe something that would happen differently?" If your answer only says "there would be no conflict" or "the plot wouldn't work," you have not described a change yet. Push one more sentence: what happens instead?

Spelling + Grammar

Q11 — "show" should be "shows," "studys" should be "studied"
🔍
Two fixable errors in Q11: (1) "New research on horses show" should be "shows" — the subject is "research" (singular), so the verb needs an "s." (2) "studys" is not a word — the past tense of "study" is "studied." These are small errors that cost points because they interrupt the grader's reading.
G5.1 Q11 — SUBJECT-VERB AGREEMENT
You wrote: "New research on horses show that they can communicate with their expressions."
The subject is "research" (singular). The phrase "on horses" is just describing what the research is about — it does not change the subject. So the verb must match "research": shows, not "show."
G5.1 Q11 — SPELLING ERROR
You wrote: "University studys horses and revealed that..."
"Studys" is not a real word. The past tense of "study" is "studied." When a word ends in "y" after a consonant, you drop the "y" and add "-ied" to make it past tense.
CORRECTED VERSIONS
✓ "New research on horses shows that they can communicate with their expressions."
✓ "University researchers studied horses and revealed that..."

Two small fixes, two fewer distractions for the grader.
Which sentence has correct subject-verb agreement?
A New research on horses show that they can communicate with their expressions.
B New research on horses shows that they can communicate with their expressions.
SUBJECT-VERB AGREEMENT: FIND THE REAL SUBJECT
When a sentence has a prepositional phrase between the subject and the verb, your brain sometimes matches the verb to the wrong word. The fix:

1. Find the verb ("show/shows").
2. Ask: "Who or what is doing this?" The answer is your subject.
3. Mentally skip any phrases starting with "of," "on," "in," "with," "for" — those are just descriptions.
4. Match the verb to the real subject.

"New research on horses shows..." ← subject = research (singular) → verb = shows
PAST TENSE OF "-Y" WORDS
When a verb ends in a consonant + "y," you drop the "y" and add "-ied":

study → studied
carry → carried
worry → worried

You do NOT just add "s" to make it past tense. "Studys" is not a word in any form.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Grammar and spelling errors in Q11 pull your paragraph score down because they distract the grader and signal that the writing was not proofread. Fixing these two patterns — subject-verb agreement and past-tense spelling — removes friction from the grader's reading and lets your good ideas shine through.

Detail + Explanation in Your Paragraph

Q11 — 15 out of 20 — needs more passage detail and what-it-shows reasoning
🔍
The D+E gap in Q11: Your paragraph mentions that horses communicate with expressions and that a university studied them, but it does not pull specific details from the passage or explain what those details show. At G5 level, a strong paragraph pairs a concrete detail from the text with a sentence that explains why that detail matters.
G5.1 Q11 — PARAGRAPH EXCERPT (SCORED 15 OUT OF 20)
You wrote: "New research on horses show that they can communicate with their expressions. University studys horses and revealed that..."
This gives a general claim (horses communicate with expressions) but no specific passage detail (like the number of expressions, or a specific example of what horses do) and no explanation of what it means or why it matters.

Jason, think of Detail + Explanation as a two-step move. Detail is a specific fact or example pulled straight from the passage. Explanation is your sentence that says what that detail shows, proves, or means. Without both parts, your paragraph stays on the surface.

The Detail + Explanation Pattern
DETAIL A specific fact from the passage: "According to 'Say What, Horse?' scientists have identified 17 different facial expressions that horses use."
EXPLAIN What that detail shows: "This proves that horses have a complex communication system, not just simple reactions."
Tap to see a developed paragraph with D+E

MODEL PARAGRAPH WITH DETAIL + EXPLANATION

New research on horses shows that they can communicate through facial expressions in surprising ways. According to the article "Say What, Horse?" scientists have identified 17 different facial expressions that horses use to share how they are feeling. This is surprising because many people assume horses only show emotions through body language like stomping or running, not through their faces. For example, horses raise the skin above their eyes when they experience negative emotions like fear. This detail shows that horses have specific, readable signals for different feelings, which means humans could learn to understand what a horse needs just by watching its face. Overall, this research proves that horses are far more expressive than most people realize.

Notice: each bolded pair is a Detail followed by an Explanation. The paragraph has two D+E pairs plus a closing sentence. That is the structure that earns full marks.

HOW TO ADD D+E TO YOUR PARAGRAPH
After each detail sentence from the passage, ask yourself: "So what? What does this show?" Then write one sentence that answers that question. That sentence is your Explanation.

Detail: "Horses have 17 facial expressions."
So what? "This means their communication is more complex than most people think."

If you have two D+E pairs plus an opening and a closing sentence, your paragraph is at full-credit level.
