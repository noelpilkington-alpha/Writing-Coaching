General, specific, thesis. Then formal sentences all the way down
THE RULE: THE THESIS NAMES THE THREE PARTS
The introduction runs general statement, specific statement, thesis, and the thesis names the three things the body paragraphs will explain, in order. The conclusion runs the same road back: thesis in new words, specific statement, general statement. The check: can you count three parts in your thesis sentence? Do your three topic sentences match them?

The one multiple-choice point. Q3 asked which sentence combined two ideas using a logical conjunction: Animated movies are fun. They also include valuable lessons. The right answer joined them with but: fun, and yet also serious. The conjunction has to match the relationship between the two ideas.

CONTRAST: BUTAnimated movies are fun, but they include valuable lessons.
RESULT: SOChildren watch the same films many times, so the lessons repeat.
REASON: BECAUSEThe lessons stay because they arrive inside a story.

Fun does not cause the lessons. So is a result word.

THE PASSAGE

How the Bicycle Changed Everyday Life

The first bicycles did not look like bicycles at all. In 1817, a German inventor named Karl von Drais built a wooden machine with two wheels and no pedals. Riders sat on it and pushed themselves along the ground with their feet. It was heavy, it was expensive, and on the rough dirt roads of the time it was uncomfortable. Only wealthy men bought one, and most of them gave up after a few rides.

Over the next sixty years, inventors added pedals to the front wheel and then made that wheel enormous. The high-wheel bicycle of the 1870s could go fast, but the rider sat almost five feet above the road. A stone or a dog in the path could send the rider flying over the handlebars. Riding one took strength, nerve and money, so the bicycle stayed a toy for young, athletic, well-off men.

Everything changed in the 1880s. A British maker named John Kemp Starley built what he called a safety bicycle: two wheels of the same size, a chain that turned the back wheel, and a seat low enough to step off. A few years later, air-filled rubber tires replaced solid ones, and the bone-shaking ride became smooth. Prices fell as factories copied the design. By the 1890s, clerks, teachers and factory workers could afford a bicycle, and so could women. Women rode in growing numbers, and long skirts gave way to practical clothing. One reformer of the time said the bicycle had done more to give women freedom than anything else in the world.

The bicycle also changed the ground it rolled on. Cyclists in the United States formed clubs that campaigned for smooth, paved roads years before cars became common. Many of the first paved roads between towns were built because riders demanded them. A worker with a bicycle could live several miles from a job and still arrive on time, so towns began to spread outward along the new roads.

Today, cities around the world paint bicycle lanes onto their streets and set up bicycle-sharing stations on their corners. A machine that began as a rich man’s wooden toy became one of the most common ways in the world to get to school and to work.

THE PROMPT
Write an expository essay explaining how the bicycle changed everyday life. Use information from the passage to explain why early bicycles were hard to use and who rode them, how the safety bicycle and air-filled tires changed who could ride, and how bicycles changed roads and towns.
Make sure your response includes the following: five clear paragraphs, an introduction with a strong thesis, three body paragraphs with topic sentences, and a conclusion (5 points). Use evidence and explain it: support each point with facts or examples from the passage and explain what they show (5 points). Stay organized and on topic: use transitions, keep your ideas in order, and focus on the main idea (4 points). Use strong sentences: vary sentence types and use clear, formal language (3 points). Edit your writing: check your spelling, punctuation, and grammar (3 points).
