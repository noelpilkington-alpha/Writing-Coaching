Name the Moment, Don't Cite Sentences

Q7 — 2 out of 3 — describe what happened
📝
What happened: The question asked which moment in the story was most meaningful. You wrote "sentence three to six" instead of describing what actually happened in those sentences. The grader wants to see that you understood the moment, not just where it is.
YOUR ANSWER (Q7)
"I think the most meaningful moment in this short text story is sentence three to six, because if the family didn't spot..."
WHY SENTENCE NUMBERS DON'T WORK
When you write "sentence three to six," the reader doesn't know what happened there without flipping back. Your job is to show that you understand the moment by describing it in your own words. Sentence numbers are a shortcut that skips the understanding part.
Citing vs. Describing
CITING "The most meaningful moment is sentence three to six..."
DESCRIBING "The most meaningful moment is when the family spotted Sally on the beach and decided to help her get back to the ocean..."
Which answer correctly names a meaningful moment from the passage?
A The most meaningful moment is paragraph two, sentence one, because it is important.
B The most meaningful moment is when the family found Sally struggling on the sand and gently carried her to the water, because without their help she might not have survived.
C I think the third part is the most meaningful because it is where the action happens.
YOUR HABIT TO BUILD
When a question asks about a moment, picture the scene in your head first. Then write what happened in that scene, not where it is in the text. Replace sentence numbers with: "when [character] [did something]..."

Add a Detail + Explanation

Q11 — 16 out of 20 — your paragraph needs more development
📚
Why this matters: Your paragraph has a clear main idea and decent structure. To move from 16/20 toward 18 or 19/20, you need to add one more specific passage detail plus an explanation of what it shows.

Brevan, your Q11 paragraph shows you understand the topic. But to earn more points, you need to go deeper: pick a specific fact from the passage and then explain what it shows about your main idea. Right now the paragraph stays general when it should get specific.

The Detail + Explanation Pattern
DETAIL A specific fact from the passage (names, numbers, what happened).
EXPLAIN Why it matters or what it shows about the main idea. This is the sentence most students leave out.
EXAMPLE FROM YOUR PASSAGE
Detail: "Tajiri spent hours as a kid exploring fields and catching bugs near his home in Japan."
Explanation: "This shows that a simple childhood hobby can spark an idea that becomes something millions of people enjoy."

The explanation connects the fact to the bigger idea (childhood curiosity leading to big achievements). Without it, the reader just has a fact with no meaning attached.

B Detail: Tajiri spent hours exploring rice paddies and streams to find insects. Explanation: This shows that his curiosity about nature gave him the idea to create a game built around collecting creatures.
YOUR NEXT STEP
In your next paragraph, after you write a passage detail, pause and ask: "What does this show about my main idea?" Then write one more sentence answering that question. That pair (detail + explanation) is what "developed" means on the rubric.
