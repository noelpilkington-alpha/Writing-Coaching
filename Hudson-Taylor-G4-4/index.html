Proofread: Near-Miss Word Errors

Q1 — 1.5 out of 2 | Q3 — 1 out of 2 | Q7 — 2 out of 3

You lost points on three different questions because of small word errors. These are words that look right at a glance but are actually the wrong word. Let's see the pattern:

"is" (Q1) → "in"
"to" (Q3) → "too"
"quite" (Q7) → "quiet"

Notice the pattern: each wrong word is only one letter different from the correct word. Your brain knows what you mean, so it skips right over the error. You need a strategy to catch these.

Q1 — YOU WROTE
"A study is Seattle showed that calm music helps people relax and feel less stress."
Q7 — YOU WROTE
"I like to listen to music in the car, because it normally quite in the car."

Q7 also repeats "in the car" — once you fix the spelling, try replacing the second "in the car" with a word like "there."

THE FINGER-POINT METHOD
After you finish writing, go back to your first sentence. Point your finger at each word one at a time and ask: "Is this the exact word I mean?" This forces your brain to slow down and see the actual letters instead of guessing.
Common near-miss pairs to watch for
is / in — "is" means something exists, "in" means inside a place
to / too — "to" goes before a verb, "too" means also or very
quite / quiet — "quite" means fairly, "quiet" means no noise
were / where — "were" is past tense of "are", "where" is a place

If you catch yourself writing one of these, pause and check: which meaning do I actually need?

Detail + Explanation for Q11

Q11 — 17 out of 20

Your paragraph scored 17/20, which is a big jump from 13/20 last time. You are clearly using the Detail + Explanation pattern. Let's look at how to pick up those last 3 points.

FROM YOUR PARAGRAPH
"I think there are a few lessons you can learn from this story..."

What is working:

  • You have a clear structure with a topic sentence and supporting details.
  • Your writing flows and makes sense to the reader.

What would push you to 19-20:

  • More specific development: Instead of "a few lessons," name the exact lesson and then support it with a detail from the passage.
  • Deeper explanations: After each detail, explain not just what happened but why it matters to your main point.
THE 17 TO 20 BRIDGE
You have the structure down. The difference between 17 and 20 is depth. For each detail you include, ask yourself: "So what? Why does this matter to my main point?" Then write that answer as your explanation sentence.
Example: shallow vs. deep explanation
Shallow: "The builders used heavy stones. This shows they worked hard."

Deep: "The builders used stones weighing several tons each without any modern machines. This shows that they were not only strong but also brilliant problem-solvers, because they had to invent new ways to move materials that we would use cranes for today."

The deep version adds specific details (several tons, no machines) and explains why it matters (brilliant problem-solvers, invented new ways).
