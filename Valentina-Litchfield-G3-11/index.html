#1 Growth Area: The Exclamatory Rewrite on Q1

"Rewrite as an exclamatory sentence" means add strong feeling — and an exclamation mark

Q1 on G3.11 asked you to rewrite sentence 12 as an exclamatory sentence. Here is what you wrote:

FROM G3.11 Q1
Original: "Penguins also huddle together for warmth."
Your rewrite: "Penguins huddle together for warmth."
Scored 0.5 out of 1. You dropped the word "also," which is fine — but the sentence is still a plain declarative (a statement that ends with a period). An exclamatory sentence needs strong feeling and an exclamation mark.
WHAT IS AN EXCLAMATORY SENTENCE?
An exclamatory sentence expresses strong feeling (surprise, excitement, wonder) and ends with an exclamation mark (!). It often starts with words like "What", "How", or "Wow".

• Declarative (the original): "Penguins huddle together for warmth."
• Exclamatory (what Q1 asked for): "What a smart way penguins huddle together for warmth!"
• Another way: "How clever penguins are to huddle together for warmth!"

Trick: if your sentence ends with a period, it cannot be exclamatory.
Building an Exclamatory Sentence
STEP 1 Start with a feeling word: "What a", "How", or "Wow".
STEP 2 Add an adjective that shows feeling: "smart", "amazing", "clever".
STEP 3 Keep the main idea from the original sentence.
STEP 4 End with an exclamation mark, not a period.
RESULT "What a smart way penguins huddle together for warmth!"
Which rewrite is a true exclamatory sentence?
A Penguins huddle together for warmth.
B How amazing that penguins huddle together for warmth!
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q1 is worth 1 point. Half credit (0.5) is the difference between 87% and 88.5% on this test alone. "Rewrite as an exclamatory/interrogative/imperative" shows up on every G3 test — once you lock in the feeling-word + exclamation-mark pattern, Q1 becomes a reliable full-credit question.

#2 Growth Area: The Appositive on Q2

An appositive is a noun phrase that renames — not two sentences joined with "and"

Q2 on G3.11 asked you to combine two sentences using an appositive. Here is what you wrote:

FROM G3.11 Q2
Sentence 13: "Hundreds of penguins stand close together."
Sentence 14: "They form a big group called a huddle."
Your combination: "Hundreds of penguins stand close together and they form a big group called a huddle."
Scored 0.5 out of 1. You joined the two sentences with "and" — that's a compound sentence, not an appositive. An appositive renames a noun using a noun phrase (no verb).
REMINDER: WHAT IS AN APPOSITIVE?
An appositive is a noun phrase that sits next to another noun and renames it. It does not have a verb like "form" or "are" or "stand."

• Two sentences joined with "and" (not appositive): "Hundreds of penguins stand close together, and they form a big group called a huddle."
• Appositive version: "Hundreds of penguins stand close together in a big group called a huddle."
• Even cleaner: "Hundreds of penguins, a big group called a huddle, stand close together."

The phrase "a big group called a huddle" is a pure noun phrase — it renames what the penguins form.
Building an Appositive from Sentence 14
STEP 1 Sentence 14 says: "They form a big group called a huddle." Find the noun phrase: "a big group called a huddle".
STEP 2 Drop the verb "form" — appositives have no verbs.
STEP 3 Put that noun phrase next to what it renames, with commas on both sides.
RESULT "Hundreds of penguins, a big group called a huddle, stand close together."
Which combined sentence uses a true appositive?
A Hundreds of penguins stand close together, and they form a big group called a huddle.
B Hundreds of penguins, a big group called a huddle, stand close together.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Appositives keep showing up on these tests — Q1 on G3.10 asked for one, and Q2 on G3.11 asked for one. The rule is the same both times: noun phrase, no verb, commas on both sides. Lock this in and you pick up a reliable 0.5 point on every test where it appears.
