Q3: What's an Appositive?

A short description tucked inside the sentence

The question asked you to rewrite the sentence and add an appositive.

YOU WROTE
Music can help you learn, certain music can help you focus for example playing low music can help you focus.

There are two problems here. First, this is a comma splice — two complete sentences joined only by a comma. Second, there's no appositive. Also, "help you focus" appears twice, which makes the sentence repetitive.

An appositive is a short description placed right after a noun, set off by commas. It tells the reader who or what something is.

WITH AN APPOSITIVE
Music, a powerful learning tool, can help you focus when you study.
THE APPOSITIVE PATTERN
Noun, [short description], rest of sentence.

Think of it like a whisper to the reader: "Music — oh, by the way, it's a powerful learning tool — can help you focus."

More examples:
• Classical music, a calm and steady style, is great for studying.
• The piano, one of the most popular instruments, is fun to learn.

Answer the Prompt First, Then Use Details

Don't just retell — explain the lesson

Your paragraph about Ferdinand Cheval scored 18/20 and is full of amazing details. But most of the paragraph retells Cheval's story instead of explaining the lesson readers can learn.

The prompt asked: "What lesson can readers learn?" Your paragraph tells us everything Cheval did — but the lesson only appears near the end. A reader has to wait to find out why you're telling them all this.

THE FIX: PUT THE LESSON FIRST
Start your paragraph by answering the prompt directly in your topic sentence. Then use your details to support that lesson — not just retell the story.
Paragraph Plan — "What lesson can readers learn from Cheval's story?"
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail  •  W = Why it matters  •  CS = Concluding Sentence
TS Readers can learn from Ferdinand Cheval that hard work and determination can turn a small idea into something extraordinary.
D1 Cheval was just a mail carrier who tripped over a rock in 1879.
W This shows that great things can start from the simplest moments — even tripping over a stone.
D2 He spent years building his palace at night with an oil lamp, using concrete, wire, and lime.
W This shows his incredible determination — he didn't give up even though it took decades.
D3 Today, over 100,000 people visit his palace every year, and it's in the Museum of Modern Art.
W This shows that his hard work paid off — one person's dream became something the whole world admires.
CS Cheval's story shows that if you never give up on your dream, you can create something amazing.

See how every detail connects back to the lesson (hard work and determination)? You already had all these details. You just need to tell the reader what they mean after each one.

Grammar Fix: "I think...because"

A small fix that makes a big difference

On Q10, you wrote a sentence with a common grammar mistake:

YOU WROTE
I think people enjoy fast music more than slow music is because fast music is more upbeat and has a better vibe.

The problem is "I think...is because" — those two phrases don't go together. When you start with "I think," you just need "because" (no "is").

FIXED
I think people enjoy fast music more than slow music because fast music is more upbeat and has a better vibe.

See? Just remove the "is" and the sentence works perfectly. Your idea was great — the grammar just needed a small adjustment.

THE RULE
I think...is because... → I think...because...
The reason I like it is because... → I like it because...

Pick one or the other:
• "I think X because Y." (starts with "I think")
• "The reason is that Y." (starts with "The reason")

Don't mix them together.
