Write an Appositive

Q4 — 0 out of 2, Q5 — 1 out of 2 — naming phrase between commas
💡
What happened: On Q4 you wrote a comma splice with no appositive at all. On Q5 you copied a phrase from the passage but turned it into a fragment (no main clause). Both questions were testing whether you can add a naming phrase between commas.
YOUR ANSWER (Q4)
"The are still popular, They are known for their golden spines."
YOUR ANSWER (Q5)
"The Poky Little Puppy, a best-selling story."
WHAT IS AN APPOSITIVE?
An appositive is a naming phrase that renames or describes a noun, placed right next to it and set off by commas. It answers: "Who or what is this?"

The sentence must still be complete around the appositive. If you remove the appositive, the sentence should still make sense on its own.
Correct Appositive Pattern
Q4 You wrote: "The are still popular, They are known for their golden spines." (comma splice, no appositive)
FIX "Little Golden Books, a series known for their golden spines, are still popular today."
Q5 You wrote: "The Poky Little Puppy, a best-selling story." (fragment, no main clause)
FIX "The Poky Little Puppy, a best-selling story, has been loved by children for decades."
THE PATTERN
Noun, [appositive phrase], rest of sentence.

The appositive sits between two commas and renames the noun before it. The sentence must be complete without it.
• "Little Golden Books, a popular children's series, have sold millions of copies."
• "The Poky Little Puppy, the best-selling title in the series, is about a curious dog."
Which sentence correctly uses an appositive about Little Golden Books?
A Little Golden Books are still popular, they have golden spines.
B Little Golden Books, a beloved children's series.
C Little Golden Books, a beloved children's series, have been around since 1942.
REMEMBER
When a question says "add an appositive," do three things: (1) Find the noun. (2) Add a comma + a phrase that renames it + another comma. (3) Make sure the rest of the sentence is complete (has a verb and makes sense on its own).

Give a REAL Reason

Q6 — 1 out of 3 — circular reasoning ("stood out because they stood out")
📝
What happened: You wrote "I thing that the golden spines stood out because, they stood out." This says the same thing twice. The word "because" needs to introduce a new piece of information that explains why.
YOUR ANSWER (Q6)
"I thing that the golden spines stood out because, they stood out."
WHY THIS DOESN'T WORK
"Stood out because they stood out" is circular reasoning. It repeats the claim instead of explaining it. The word "because" promises the reader a reason, so what comes after must be different from what came before.

Also: "thing" should be "think" (spelling).
Circular vs. Real Reason
CIRCULAR "The golden spines stood out because they stood out." (same idea repeated)
REAL "The golden spines stood out because the shiny gold color was different from other books on the shelf."
REAL "The golden spines stood out because most other children's books at the time had plain white or brown covers."
THE "BECAUSE" TEST
After you write "because," cover up everything before it. Does the part after "because" give new information the reader didn't already know? If it just restates what you already said, it's circular. Go back to the passage and find a specific fact to use instead.
Which answer gives a REAL reason (not circular)?
A "I think people noticed the golden spines because they were very noticeable on the shelf."
B "I think the golden spines stood out because the bright gold color made them look different from the other plain books on store shelves."
C "I think the golden spines stood out because they were very special."
YOUR NEXT STEP
Whenever you write "because," stop and re-read what comes after it. Ask yourself: "Am I saying something NEW, or just repeating myself?" If it sounds the same, go back to the passage and find a specific fact that explains why.

The Detail + Explanation Pattern

Q11 development — the habit that builds longer, stronger paragraphs
📚
Why this matters: Your paragraph had 3 sentences. To reach 16/20, you need 5–6 sentences. The easiest way to add good sentences (not filler) is the Detail + Explanation pattern: one sentence gives a fact from the passage, the next sentence says what it shows.
The Detail + Explanation Pattern
DETAIL A specific fact from the passage (names, numbers, what happened).
EXPLAIN What it shows or why it matters to the main idea. This is the sentence most students skip.
EXAMPLE FROM YOUR PASSAGE ("DIVE INTO A SUNKEN MUSEUM")
Detail: "Jason deCaires Taylor places his statues on the ocean floor near coral reefs."
Explanation: "This helps protect real reefs because tourists visit the statues instead of touching the natural coral."

The explanation connects the fact to the bigger idea (the museum helps the ocean). Without it, the reader just has a fact with no meaning.

YOUR PARAGRAPH, DEVELOPED TO 16/20

[TOPIC] Jason deCaires Taylor makes life-sized statues that sit on the ocean floor.
[DETAIL 1] He builds these statues in the Caribbean using special cement that lets coral and sea plants grow on them.
[EXPLAIN] This means his art actually becomes part of the reef over time, which helps sea creatures find new homes.

What changed: Two Detail + Explanation pairs added. Each detail comes from the passage. Each explanation says why it matters. A closing sentence wraps up the main idea. That's the difference between 10/20 and 16/20.
