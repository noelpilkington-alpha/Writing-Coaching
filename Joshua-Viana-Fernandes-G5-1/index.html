Your Lead Card: Comma Placement with Conjunctions

Q3 — 1 out of 2 · Q6 — 2 out of 3 · Q7 — 2 out of 3
🔍
The pattern across G5.1: On three separate questions, you put the comma after the conjunction instead of before it. The rule is simple: the comma goes BEFORE the joining word (but, and, because), never after it.

Joshua, this is the single most valuable fix in your test. Three questions all have the same error: the comma landed on the wrong side of the conjunction. Once you see the pattern, it clicks fast.

G5.1 Q3 — COMMA AFTER "BUT" (SCORED 1 OUT OF 2)
You wrote: "I stood up straight but, my backpack knocked Ms. Kessler's coffee mug onto the floor."
The comma is after "but." It should be before "but." The conjunction is the joining word, and the comma goes right before it to signal the pause.
G5.1 Q7 — COMMA AFTER "BECAUSE" (SCORED 2 OUT OF 3)
You wrote: "I think Crista felt guilty even after leaving the classroom because, she knocked over Ms. Kessler's coffee mug..."
Same pattern. The comma landed after "because" when it should go before "because" (or not be there at all, since "because" already signals the connection).
THE FIX: COMMA BEFORE THE CONJUNCTION
✓ "I stood up straight, but my backpack knocked Ms. Kessler's coffee mug onto the floor."
✓ "I think Crista felt guilty even after leaving the classroom because she knocked over Ms. Kessler's coffee mug..."

With "but" and "and," the comma goes right before the joining word. With "because," you usually don't need a comma at all because "because" already connects the ideas.
Which sentence has the comma in the right place?
A I wanted to tell the truth but, I was too scared to say anything.
B I wanted to tell the truth, but I was too scared to say anything.
THE RULE: COMMA BEFORE, NEVER AFTER
When you join two complete ideas with a conjunction (and, but, so, or), the comma goes directly before the joining word.

✓ "I stood up straight, but my backpack knocked the mug off."
✗ "I stood up straight but, my backpack knocked the mug off."

With "because," you usually need no comma at all:
✓ "She felt guilty because she broke the mug."
✗ "She felt guilty because, she broke the mug."
THE QUICK CHECK
Before you submit, scan your answer for conjunctions (but, and, so, because). If you see a comma, ask: "Is it before the joining word or after?" If it is after, move it to the left of the conjunction. That one move would have saved points on Q3, Q6, and Q7.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Three questions on G5.1 lost partial credit from this one habit. Q3 lost 1 point, Q6 and Q7 each lost 1 point. That is roughly 3 points on a 45-point test from a single, repeating comma error. Fix this one pattern and your score jumps immediately.

THE "PERIOD CHECK" BEFORE YOU SUBMIT
Before you move to the next question, look at the last word of your answer. Ask yourself:

1. Does my answer end with a period (or question mark)?
2. Does the last sentence make sense on its own, or does it trail off?
3. If I covered up everything except the last sentence, would a reader understand what I meant?

If the answer to any of those is "no," finish the thought before you move on.
WHY ANSWERS GET CUT OFF
Usually it happens because you are running out of space in your head or running out of patience. The fix is simple: finish the sentence you are on before you stop writing. Even if it means trimming the beginning, always land the ending. A shorter answer with a complete thought earns more than a longer answer that cuts off mid-sentence.

Joshua, spelling errors on a writing test signal to the grader that you didn't proofread. The tricky thing about these two words is that they have double letters in unexpected places. Once you learn the pattern, they stick.

G5.1 Q6 — SPELLING ERROR
You wrote: "...it was really embarrasing but I owned up to it."
The correct spelling is embarrassing — two r's AND two s's. Think: "I turn really red, really suddenly" (double r, double s).
G5.1 Q11 — SPELLING ERROR
You wrote: "Did you know horses were able to cummunicate with us before we ever knew it?"
The correct spelling is communicate — one m, then two m's in the middle? No. It is c-o-m-m-u-n-i-c-a-t-e. Two m's, but no double u. Think: "co-" (together) + "mmunicate" (share meaning).
MEMORY TRICKS FOR THESE TWO WORDS
Embarrassing: two r's + two s's. "I am so embarrassed I turn really red and feel so silly."

Communicate: comm- (like "community") + -unicate. If you can spell "community," you can spell "communicate" — they share the same root.

THE SPELLING SCAN
Before you submit, do a quick scan for any word that looks long or feels tricky. If you are not sure, sound it out slowly in your head, syllable by syllable:

com-mu-ni-cate — hear that double "m"?

If a word feels uncertain, slow down and write each syllable deliberately. That extra two seconds prevents a point deduction.

THE DETAIL + EXPLANATION PATTERN
Every time you write a fact or detail from the passage, follow it with a sentence that says "This means..." or "This shows..."

Detail: "Scientists have identified 17 facial expressions."
Explanation: "This shows that horse communication is much more complex than most people think."

Detail alone = reporting. Detail + Explanation = thinking. Graders reward the thinking.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Your paragraph already scores well because your hook and evidence are strong. Adding one explanation sentence after each detail could push you from 17/20 toward a higher score. It does not require more research or new ideas. It only requires one extra sentence that says what the detail means.
