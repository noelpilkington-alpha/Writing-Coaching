Q1: Make Appositives Descriptive

Add detail that helps the reader picture it

The question asked you to rewrite the sentence and add an appositive for Willow State Park:

YOU WROTE
Every summer, the Thompsons camped at Willow State Park, a camping ground.

You put the appositive in the right place and used the comma correctly — that's good! But the description "a camping ground" doesn't add much. The reader already knows it's a camping ground because the sentence says they "camped" there.

A strong appositive gives the reader new information that helps them picture the place, person, or thing:

MORE DESCRIPTIVE
Every summer, the Thompsons camped at Willow State Park, a beautiful state park with tall trees and a sparkling lake.
THE APPOSITIVE TEST
After you write an appositive, ask yourself: "Does this tell the reader something they didn't already know?"

• "a camping ground" — the reader already knows this. Too bare.
• "a beautiful state park with tall trees" — now the reader can picture it. That's the goal.

More examples:
• Kim, the youngest Thompson child, unfolded the tent.
• The backyard, a wide grassy space behind their house, was the perfect practice spot.

Q5: Don't Forget Question Marks!

A question needs the right punctuation

The question asked you to rewrite the sentence as an interrogative (a question). You came up with a great question:

YOU WROTE
Why do the Thompsons camp at Willow State Park every summer.

The sentence itself is perfect — you turned a statement into a question using "Why do." But you ended it with a period instead of a question mark. That small mistake cost you half a point.

FIXED
Why do the Thompsons camp at Willow State Park every summer?
THE QUESTION MARK CHECK
Whenever you write a sentence that starts with one of these words, it must end with a question mark:

Who • What • When • Where • Why • How • Do • Does • Did • Is • Are • Can • Will

Your sentence started with "Why do" — that's a question word, so it needs a ? at the end. Always double-check your ending punctuation when you write an interrogative sentence.

Q11: Add more evidence and explain each detail

Your VR Field Trips paragraph scored 18/20 — that's very strong. You had a clear topic sentence and specific evidence. But at only 4 sentences, it needed more development. Here's what to add:

You mentioned the Louvre in France and not needing permission slips or a bus — that's one main point. A strong expository paragraph uses 2-3 details, each followed by an explanation of why it matters.

YOUR ENDING

This sentence doesn't finish. It sounds like you ran out of time or space. Always complete your concluding sentence so it wraps up your main point clearly.

3. Use the G5 paragraph plan:

Paragraph Plan — "VR Field Trips: Worth the Investment?"
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail from the article  •  W = Why it matters  •  CS = Concluding Sentence
TS The author shows that VR field trips are a good and useful idea for schools.
D1 VR lets students visit places like the Louvre in France without leaving the classroom.
W This shows that VR makes it possible for students to experience faraway places they might never get to visit in real life.
D2 Schools don't need permission slips, buses, or extra money for VR trips.
W This matters because it makes field trips easier and cheaper for schools, so more students can go on them.
D3 Students can take VR field trips any time, not just on one special day.
W This shows that VR gives students more chances to learn by exploring, not just reading about a place.
CS For all these reasons, VR field trips are a smart investment that helps every student learn more.

Notice the pattern: Detail → Why it matters, repeated three times. Each "W" sentence explains what the detail shows. That's what takes a paragraph from good to great.
