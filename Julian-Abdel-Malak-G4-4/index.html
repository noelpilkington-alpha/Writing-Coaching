Use Specific Details in Your Paragraph

Question 11 — 13/20. This is your biggest opportunity.
🔍 Pattern alert: Your sentence answers earned 22 of 25 points, but the paragraph came in at 13 of 20. The gap is the same thing every time — the paragraph stays general instead of using the specific facts from the article.

Julian, the article for Question 11 was about Ferdinand Cheval, a mail carrier who built an entire palace out of stones. There were rich, specific facts you could have used — but your paragraph stayed broad. Read your version first:

YOU WROTE (G4.4 Q11 — scored 13/20)
"If you have passion you can do anything you put your mind to. Some people will laugh at you and make fun of you. When they see when your done they won't be laughing. So never give up no matter what people say."

The message is fine — but it could be about anyone. There is nothing in it that proves you read the Ferdinand Cheval article. That is what cost the points.

Which sentence would earn more paragraph points?
A Some people will laugh at you, but if you have passion you can do anything you put your mind to.
B Ferdinand Cheval spent 33 years building a palace out of stones he collected on his mail route, even though people laughed at him.
THE FIX: NAME THE SPECIFIC FACTS
A strong paragraph proves you read the article. After your topic sentence, give 2–3 specific details pulled straight from the passage, and explain what they show. From the Ferdinand Cheval article you could have used:

• He built an entire palace out of stones
• It took him 33 years
• He collected the stones on his mail route
• People mocked him while he worked

DETAIL 1 A specific fact: he built a palace out of stones over 33 years.
DETAIL 2 A specific fact: he collected the stones on his mail route while people mocked him.

MODEL PARAGRAPH (article-grounded)

Ferdinand Cheval's story shows that passion can help you create something amazing even when other people doubt you. Precise Appositives & Catching Missing Words

Questions 3 and 10 — small fixes, real points
🔍 Pattern alert: Two of your point losses came from the same habit — going general instead of specific (a vague appositive on Q3) and reading too fast (a dropped word on Q10). Both are caught with one slow, careful read.

Julian, these two were close. Question 3 asked you to add an appositive — a short phrase that adds specific information about a noun. Here is what you wrote:

YOU WROTE (G4.4 Q3 — 1 of 2 points)
"Music, a way to study, can help you learn"

The problem: "a way to study" is vague and circular — it doesn't tell the reader anything new about how music helps learning. A good appositive adds a real, specific fact. (It was also missing its end punctuation.)

Which appositive adds real, specific information?
A Music, a thing that helps, can lower your stress.
B Music, a calming activity for your brain, can lower your stress.
THE FIX: MAKE THE APPOSITIVE SPECIFIC
An appositive should answer "what exactly is this noun?" with a real detail, not a repeat of the sentence.

✗ "Music, a way to study, can help you learn." (vague — what way?)
✓ "Music, a steady background sound, can help you focus while you study." (specific — tells how)

Remember the two commas around the appositive, and don't forget the period at the end.

Now Question 10. This one was just a missing word — the idea was right, but a word got dropped:

YOU WROTE (G4.4 Q10 — 2 of 3 points)
"Some people enjoy fast music more than slow music because it could the type of person that they are."

Read it slowly: "because it could the type" is missing the word be. It should read "because it could be the type of person that they are." Your brain filled in the missing word when you wrote it — a slow re-read catches it.

COMPLETE SENTENCE

THE PROOFREAD TRICK FOR MISSING WORDS
Read each sentence slowly and out loud in your head, touching each word as you go. When you read fast, your brain auto-fills words that aren't actually on the screen. Reading slowly forces you to see exactly what you typed — that is how you catch a dropped "be," "is," or "the."
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Both of these were one point each — the kind of points that decide whether you land at 78% or back in your steady 80s. The skill is the same on both: be specific, and read slowly enough to see every word.
