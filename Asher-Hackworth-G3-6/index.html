THE RULE — THE "WHO + DOING WHAT" TEST
Before you move on from any answer, ask: "Who is doing what?"

✗ "Pet them." → Who? (missing subject)
✓ "I would pet them." → Who: I. Doing what: would pet.

For comma splices: if you have a comma between TWO complete thoughts, either:
• Swap the comma for a period (two sentences).
• Add a connector: and, but, so, because.
Which is correctly punctuated?

B Anna ran to the beach. She found a baby seal.

Read the Prompt — Identify vs Rewrite

You sometimes do MORE than the prompt asks

Asher, here’s a pattern across your rushed tests: when a prompt says IDENTIFY, you often REWRITE the whole sentence. That’s extra work that costs points.

FROM G3.5 Q5 (Apr 2026)
Prompt: "Identify the appositive in this sentence: Fido, a big brown dog, loves the park."
You wrote: "Fido is a big brown dog, who is what he looks like."
(You REWROTE the sentence. The answer they wanted: “a big brown dog.”)
THE DIFFERENCE — TWO DIFFERENT PROMPTS
"Identify the appositive" → Just name the phrase. Example: "a big brown dog"
"Rewrite this sentence using an appositive" → Write the full new sentence.

"Identify the subject" → Just name the subject. Example: "Fido"
"Rewrite with a new subject" → Write the full new sentence.
Prompt: "Identify the appositive in: Fido, a big brown dog, loves the park." Which is correct?
A Fido is a big brown dog who loves to play at the park.
B a big brown dog
YOUR 2-STEP READING HABIT
1. Before writing, underline the first verb in the prompt: Identify. Rewrite. Combine. Change. Explain.
2. Match your answer to that verb exactly. If the prompt says "identify," don’t rewrite. If it says "rewrite," don’t just identify.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Doing MORE than asked usually scores the same as doing LESS — partial credit. A careful read of the prompt saves you from wasted effort AND missed points. 1–2 points per test, locked in.

Paragraph — Polish, Don't Rebuild

Your G3.4 paragraph was the strongest in the group

Asher, your G3.4 paragraph was a 7-sentence chronological narrative — that’s polished work. You already know how to structure a paragraph. What’s missing is the one move that pushes paragraphs from 15–17/20 into the 18–20/20 range: an explicit thesis sentence.

"The pup was stuck. The dad found the pup early in the morning. He freed it because it got stuck. Then the dad took it home. The pup was happy. The family loved the pup. The pup had a good life."
Missing: Thesis Event 1 Event 2 Event 3 Closing

The chronological structure is strong. The characters and events are clear. What’s missing is one opener that tells the reader WHAT the paragraph is about and WHY it matters.

Which is a stronger paragraph opener?
A The pup was stuck.
B One cold morning, a trapped pup found its whole life changed by a stranger’s kindness.

Your same story — with one new opener
MODEL POLISHED PARAGRAPH (18–20/20)

One cold morning, a trapped pup found its whole life changed by a stranger’s kindness. The dad found the pup early in the morning, tangled and scared. He carefully freed it, even though it was stuck tight. Then the dad took it home and gave it a warm place to sleep. The pup was happy for the first time in days. The family loved the pup and named it right away. From that cold morning on, the pup had a good life.

Your Paragraph Upgrade — Thesis First
THESIS Name the main idea AND the point ("X happened because of ___")
EVENT 1 First thing that happened
EVENT 2 Next thing — use "Then," or "Next,"
EVENT 3 What happened after — more detail
CLOSE Tie back to the thesis — what the story means
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
You already write stronger paragraphs than most students in the cohort. ONE explicit thesis sentence is the move that takes you from 15/20 to 18–19/20. That’s a 3-point jump on the single biggest question.
