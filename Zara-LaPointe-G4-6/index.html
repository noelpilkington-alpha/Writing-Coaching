Your #1 G4.6 Error: Pick the Connector the Prompt Asks For

"and" vs "because/but/so" — they are not interchangeable on Q4

Zara, on G4.6 Q4 the prompt gave you exact options — and you used a different word that sounded fine but did not do the job the prompt was asking for.

FROM G4.6 Q4 (May 2026) — 0.5/1.0
Prompt: "Combine sentences 5 and 6 into one sentence using because, but, or so to show how the ideas are related."
Sentence 5: Cars, wagons, bicycles, and people all moved at the same time.
Sentence 6: This often caused crashes.

You wrote: "Cars, wagons, bicycles, and people all moved at the same time, and this often caused crashes."
The prompt said "because, but, or so" — not "and." The two ideas have a cause-and-effect link, so the connector needed to show that.
Same G4.6 Q4 prompt. Which combined sentence earns full credit?
A Cars, wagons, bicycles, and people all moved at the same time, and this often caused crashes.
B Cars, wagons, bicycles, and people all moved at the same time, so this often caused crashes.
THE CONNECTOR CHEAT SHEET
When the prompt lists connectors, pick the one that matches the relationship between the two ideas:

• because → the second idea gives the REASON for the first. (Traffic was dangerous because people walked when cars were coming.)
• so → the second idea is the RESULT of the first. (Everyone moved at once, so crashes happened.)
• but → the second idea CONTRASTS or SURPRISES. (The streets were crowded, but no signals existed.)
• and → just ADDING two equal things. (Cars stopped and pedestrians crossed.)

The prompt asked for an appositive — you used "which" instead
FROM G4.6 Q3 (May 2026) — 0.5/1.0
Prompt: "Combine sentences 21 and 27 into one sentence by adding an appositive that gives more information about Morgan's idea."

You wrote: "Garrett Morgan built an early traffic signal, which helped save lives and reduce crashes."
The prompt asked for an appositive (a noun phrase that renames). "Which" starts a relative clause, not an appositive. Close, but a different grammar move.
APPOSITIVE VERSION — 1.0/1.0
→ "Garrett Morgan built an early traffic signal, a life-saving invention that reduced crashes."

The phrase in bold is a noun phrase that renames "traffic signal." That is an appositive. Notice: no "which" and no new verb.
Prompt: "Combine these sentences by adding an appositive." Sentence 1: Garrett Morgan was an inventor. Sentence 2: He invented the traffic signal. Which rewrite uses an appositive?
A Garrett Morgan, who invented the traffic signal, was an inventor.
B Garrett Morgan, the inventor of the traffic signal, built an early version of the device.
APPOSITIVE VS. RELATIVE CLAUSE
• Appositive = a noun phrase that renames. No new verb. Example: Garrett Morgan, an inventor, built the signal.
• Relative clause = starts with "who," "which," or "that" AND has a verb. Example: Garrett Morgan, who built the signal, was an inventor.

Both are useful, but when the prompt says "add an appositive," you need the noun-phrase version — not the "which" version.
