Fix Run-On Sentences

Q6, Q7, Q9, Q10 & Paragraph — Your biggest pattern

A run-on sentence happens when you jam two or more complete thoughts together without proper punctuation. This showed up in four sentence answers and your paragraph.

HOW TO FIX RUN-ONS
1. Read your sentence out loud. If you run out of breath, it's too long.
2. Find the spot where one complete thought ends and another begins.
3. Fix it: use a period, or add a conjunction (and, but, so, because).

Quick test: Count your verbs. If your sentence has 3+ verbs, you probably need to split it.
See more examples of run-on fixes
Run-on: She sat down the driver yelled at her she still didn't move.
Fixed: She sat down. The driver yelled at her, but she still didn't move.

Run-on: Colvin was brave, she changed history.
Fixed: Colvin was brave, and she changed history.

Build Your Paragraph

Q11 — Organize and develop your ideas

Your paragraph scored 12/20. You had some good ideas, but there are several issues to fix.

YOUR PARAGRAPH
The author argues that the US travel is not very efficient or clean. He says that high-speed trains are much faster and less polluting than cars. They're also the greatest way to travel in 2018 four to open the Brightline express this train has been very popular. Should we add trains in the other parts of America? The author argues that there are strong reasons to say yes.

Issues to fix:

  • Run-on in the middle: "...in 2018 four to open the Brightline express this train has been very popular" jams multiple thoughts together. It looks like "four" should be "Florida opened."
  • Expository tone: This is expository writing, so use "explains" or "shows" instead of "argues." You are explaining information, not making an argument.
  • Ending with a question: An expository paragraph should not end with a question. End with a concluding sentence that restates your main point.
  • Missing specific evidence: Add details from the passage with page-worthy facts and numbers.

Here's the G5 paragraph plan you should follow:

Paragraph Plan — TS → D1 → W → D2 → W → D3 → W → CS
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail from the article  •  W = Why it matters  •  CS = Concluding Sentence
TS The author explains that the United States should invest in high-speed trains because they are faster, cleaner, and more efficient than cars.
D1 High-speed trains can travel much faster than cars and produce less pollution.
W This shows that trains are a better option for both speed and the environment.
D2 In 2018, Florida opened the Brightline Express, and it quickly became very popular.
W This shows that when people have the option to ride trains, they choose to use them.
D3 Other countries already use high-speed rail to connect major cities.
W This shows that the idea already works successfully around the world.
CS Overall, the article explains that high-speed trains would make US travel faster, cleaner, and more popular.
KEY REMINDERS FOR EXPOSITORY WRITING
1. Use "explains," "shows," or "describes" — never "argues" or "proves."
2. Do NOT end with a question. End with a concluding sentence that restates your main point.
3. After each detail, write a "Why it matters" sentence. Ask yourself: "So what? Why does this detail support the main point?"
