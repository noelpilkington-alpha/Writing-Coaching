Your #2 Fix: "Because" Needs to Actually Connect

Q5 was close — one missing word away from full credit
🔍 Pattern alert: On Q5 you were asked to combine two sentences using because, but, or so. You used "because" but dropped a word, which turned it into two ideas glued together instead of one connected sentence.

Here are the two sentences you were combining:

13. His dedication has transformed the school.
14. Due to his efforts, enrollment has doubled, and more students are now attending college.
YOU WROTE (G4.1 Q5, scored 0.75/1)
"His dedication has transformed the school because of his efforts, enrollment has doubled, and more students are now attending college."

The word "because" needs to connect a cause to a result. You wrote "because of his efforts" — but the first half of your sentence ("his dedication has transformed the school") was already the result. So the "because" attaches to the wrong part and the sentence stops making a clean cause-result link.

Which version uses "because" to correctly connect the two ideas?
A His dedication has transformed the school because of his efforts, enrollment has doubled, and more students are now attending college.
B His dedication has transformed the school because enrollment has doubled and more students are now attending college.
THE RULE: CAUSE ON ONE SIDE, RESULT ON THE OTHER
When you use because, but, or so to combine two sentences, each word has a job:

• because — the thing after it is the reason (RESULT because CAUSE)
• but — the thing after it is a contrast
• so — the thing after it is the result (CAUSE so RESULT)

Quick test: say the sentence out loud. If you cannot hear a clear "this is why that happened," the connector is in the wrong spot.
ALL THREE VERSIONS WORK
Same two sentences, three connectors, all full credit:

• because → "His dedication has transformed the school because enrollment has doubled and more students are now attending college."

• so → "His efforts have doubled enrollment, so his dedication has clearly transformed the school."

• but → (not a good fit here — "but" needs contrast, and these two ideas agree)
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
You were 0.25 away from full credit on Q5, and the fix is one word. Saying the sentence in your head before you submit catches this every time. The next card is the bigger one: your paragraph.

Topic Sentence Specific Detail Missing: Cody's change Missing: "This shows..." Closing is a fragment

You have a good topic sentence and one real detail from the story (Mud streaking down the field and scoring the first goal). Two things are missing that the prompt specifically asked for:

  • The prompt asked about Cody's opinion changing — but Cody only shows up in sentence 1. The rest of the paragraph is about Mud. You told what Mud did, but did not come back to what Cody now thinks.
  • The last sentence "All in all, Mud good in the match" is missing a word — it should be "Mud was good" or "Mud played well." A fragment in the closing drops points.
Which move would push this paragraph from 14/20 toward 18–20/20?
A Add another detail about Mud's skills, like a specific pass or teammate he worked with.
B After each detail about Mud, add a sentence like "This shows Cody that..." to connect the action back to how Cody's opinion is changing. Also fix the closing fragment.

YOUR PARAGRAPH AT 18–20/20

At first, Cody thought Mud was not good enough and was ready to take over for him. This shows that Cody had a low opinion of Mud before the game even started. Then Mud streaked down the field, passed a defender, and scored the first goal of the match. This shows Cody that Mud is actually a skilled player, which starts to change Cody's mind. By the end of the game, Cody has to admit Mud played well, and his whole opinion flips from doubt to respect. All in all, the events of the game force Cody to see Mud in a new way, and his opinion changes for the better.

The G4 Paragraph Map (for "explain how" prompts)
TOPIC Say what the character thought at the start
EVENT 1 First thing from the story that starts to change things
SHOWS "This shows that [character]..." — link the event back to the character's mind
EVENT 2 Second thing that pushes the change further
SHOWS "This shows that [character]..." — link it back again
CLOSING Say clearly how the character's opinion has changed by the end
