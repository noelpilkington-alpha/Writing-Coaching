Question 3 Fix — What Is an Appositive?

Q3 — 1 out of 2

The question asked you to combine two sentences using an appositive.

YOU WROTE
"Sally swam to the other seals, because she wanted to play with them."

Your sentence is grammatically correct and makes sense. But the question did not ask for any combination — it asked specifically for an appositive. You used "because," which is a conjunction. That is a different structure entirely.

Which sentence uses an appositive to combine the ideas?
A "Sally swam to the other seals, because she wanted to play with them."
B "Sally, a playful pup, swam to the other seals."
WHAT IS AN APPOSITIVE?
An appositive is a noun phrase placed right next to another noun to rename or describe it. It is set off by commas:

• Sally, a playful pup, swam to the other seals.

The underlined part is the appositive. It renames "Sally." You can remove it and the sentence still works: "Sally swam to the other seals."

A conjunction (because, and, but, so) connects two complete ideas. An appositive adds a description inside the sentence using commas — no connecting word needed.
HOW TO SPOT THE DIFFERENCE
Ask yourself: "Did I use a connecting word (because, and, but, so, when)?"

• If yes → that is a conjunction, not an appositive.
• If no, and you added a renaming phrase between commas → that is an appositive.

The test will tell you which one to use. Read the instructions carefully before combining.

Questions 2 & 4 — Keep the Original Meaning

Q2 — 1 out of 2 | Q4 — 1 out of 2

Both questions asked you to combine sentences — but your combined versions changed what happened in the story.

Q2 — YOU WROTE
"Dad freed the pup into the ocean after finding it tangled in a fishnet and brought it home after it had been hurt."

The problem: your sentence says Dad freed the pup into the ocean AND brought it home. That is confusing — he cannot do both at the same time. The original story has a clear order: found it tangled → brought it home → cared for it → freed it later. Your combined sentence jumbles that timeline.

Q4 — YOU WROTE
"Anna and Jim ran to the beach when they found a baby seal in a fishnet."

The problem: "when" makes it sound like finding the seal caused them to run. But in the story, they ran to the beach first, and THEN found the seal. The order matters.

Which combined sentence keeps the original order: ran to beach THEN found the seal?
A "Anna and Jim ran to the beach when they found a baby seal in a fishnet."
B "Anna and Jim ran to the beach and found a baby seal tangled in a fishnet."
THE RULE: COMBINE WITHOUT CHANGING THE ORDER
When you combine two sentences, the new sentence must keep the same order of events and the same cause-and-effect as the originals.

Words like "when," "after," and "because" change the relationship between events. Before you use one, ask:

• "Does this word change which event came first?"
• "Does this word add a cause that wasn't there?"

If yes, pick a simpler connector like "and" that just puts events side by side without changing the order.
A QUICK CHECK YOU CAN USE
After you write your combined sentence, read it back and ask: "Could someone who never read the story understand what happened in the right order from my sentence alone?"

If your sentence makes the reader guess which event came first, it needs fixing.

Question 7 — Sentence Fragment

Q7 — 2 out of 3
YOU WROTE
"I think the most meaningful moment in the story. Is when Anna and Jim release Sally..."

You put a period in the middle of one thought. Everything after that period — "Is when Anna and Jim release Sally..." — is a fragment. It cannot stand alone as a sentence because it starts with "Is" and has no subject.

Which version is a complete sentence (no fragment)?
A "I think the most meaningful moment in the story. Is when Anna and Jim release Sally back into the ocean."
B "I think the most meaningful moment in the story is when Anna and Jim release Sally back into the ocean."
HOW TO CATCH FRAGMENTS
A fragment is a group of words with a period that cannot stand on its own. The easiest check:

• Look at the words after each period you wrote.
• Ask: "Does this part have its own subject (who/what) and verb (action)?"
• If not → remove the period and connect it to the sentence before it.

In your case: "Is when Anna and Jim release Sally" has no subject — it starts with "Is." That is a dead giveaway.
WHY THIS HAPPENS
When you are writing a long thought, your brain pauses naturally — and your hand puts a period there. But a pause in your thinking is not always the end of a sentence. Before you put a period, check: "Can the next part stand alone?" If not, use a comma or just keep going.
