Topic Sentence Matching

Q6 — 0 out of 1 — intro sentences must preview the paragraph's topic

Jack, Q6 asked: "Which sentence would best introduce a paragraph about new activities at the library?" You picked the wrong option. Here is the key principle:

THE TOPIC SENTENCE RULE
An intro sentence does not just need to be true about the subject. It needs to preview the specific content of the paragraph that follows. If the paragraph is about new activities, the intro sentence must name or point toward activities, not just mention the library in general.

Think of it this way: the intro sentence is a promise to the reader. It says "this paragraph will be about X." If the paragraph delivers information about new activities (yoga classes, reading clubs, art workshops), the intro sentence needs to signal activities, not just "the library is great."

The passage describes a new lakeside library. A paragraph lists new activities: book clubs, children's storytelling hours, and outdoor yoga. Which sentence best introduces that paragraph?
A The new lakeside library is a beautiful building that the whole community can enjoy.
B Beyond lending books, the new library offers a range of activities and programs for residents of all ages.
🧩
The matching test: When you see a "best intro sentence" question, read the paragraph content first. Ask yourself: what specific topic do all the details share? Then pick the sentence that names that specific topic. A sentence can be true and still be wrong if it previews a different topic than what the paragraph actually discusses.
QUICK CHECK FOR TOPIC SENTENCE QUESTIONS
1. Read the paragraph details — what do they have in common?
2. Name the shared topic in one or two words (e.g., "new activities")
3. Pick the answer choice that uses those words or closely related ones
4. Eliminate choices that are true but about a different aspect of the subject

Essay Development

Q11 at 14/20 — each body paragraph needs passage detail + explanation

Jack, your essay scored 14/20. The grader wants to see you pull specific details from the passage and then explain what each detail proves about your thesis. Right now, your body paragraphs tend to make general claims without enough evidence from the text.

Here is the difference between a 14/20 body paragraph and an 18/20 body paragraph:

14/20 Pattern (what you are doing now)
TOPIC State the idea for this paragraph
CLAIM Make a general statement about it
STOP Move to next paragraph without passage evidence or explanation
18/20 Pattern (your next level)
TOPIC State the idea (you already do this)
DETAIL A specific fact or quote from the passage
EXPLAIN "This shows..." — what the detail proves about your thesis
DETAIL A second specific fact from the passage
EXPLAIN "This matters because..." — what the second detail proves
CLOSING Wrap back to the paragraph's central idea
The passage says the new lakeside library "attracted 2,000 visitors in its first month." Which body paragraph sentence earns more essay points?
A The library got a lot of visitors when it first opened, with 2,000 people coming in the first month.
B The library attracted 2,000 visitors in its first month alone. This shows that the community was eager for a public space dedicated to learning, proving that the investment in the lakeside location was worthwhile.
THE "SO WHAT?" TEST
After every detail from the passage, imagine the grader asking: "So what? Why does this matter for your thesis?"

Your next sentence answers that question. Detail alone = partial credit. Detail + explanation = full credit. Every time you skip the explanation, you leave points on the table.

Sentence starters that work: "This shows that..." / "This matters because..." / "This proves that..." / "In other words..."

Detail + Explanation in Action

Concrete examples from the lakeside library passage

Jack, let's see the Detail + Explanation pattern applied to the lakeside library passage you wrote about. Here are three passage details and how each one becomes a full-credit body paragraph moment:

Example 1: Library Location
DETAIL "The town chose to build the library on the lakeside because the old downtown location had no parking."
EXPLAIN "This shows that the town planners prioritized accessibility, recognizing that a library only works if people can actually get there."
Example 2: Community Response
DETAIL "Residents organized volunteer reading groups within the first week of opening."
EXPLAIN "This matters because it proves the library did not just provide books — it created a gathering place that inspired people to build community on their own."
Example 3: Overcoming Doubt
DETAIL "Critics initially called the project a waste of money, but attendance exceeded projections by 40%."
EXPLAIN "This proves that even when something seems like a lost cause, a well-executed plan can win over the skeptics with real results."

Notice the pattern: each DETAIL is a specific fact from the passage, and each EXPLAIN connects that fact back to the larger argument. The explanation is always one sentence that answers "so what?"

YOUR BODY PARAGRAPH FORMULA
For each body paragraph in your next essay, aim for:

• 1 topic sentence (what this paragraph is about)
• 2 passage details (specific facts, numbers, or descriptions from the text)
• 2 explanation sentences (one after each detail, starting with "This shows..." or "This matters because...")
• 1 closing sentence (ties back to your thesis)

That is 6 sentences per body paragraph. If you hit that formula, you are writing at the 18/20 level.
