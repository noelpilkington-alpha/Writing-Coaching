Your #1 Error: Cut Redundancy

Q1 — 1 out of 2
🔍 Pattern alert: Your Q1 answer says the same thing twice. Repeating an idea does not add detail — it wastes words and costs clarity points.

Zane, Q1 asked you to write about Peter Tabichi's accomplishment. Here is what you wrote:

YOUR ANSWER — Q1
"In this event, Science teacher Peter Tabichi recived an award and the award he received was the prestigious Global Teacher Prize."

Do you see the repetition? You said "received an award" and then "the award he received" — that is the same fact stated twice in one sentence. The sentence also has a spelling error ("recived"), but the bigger issue is that the repetition fills space without adding new information.

Which sentence says the idea clearly without repeating?
A Science teacher Peter Tabichi received an award and the award he received was the Global Teacher Prize.
B Peter Tabichi, a science teacher from Kenya, received the prestigious Global Teacher Prize for his dedication to his students.

CLEAR, NO-REPEAT VERSION
"Peter Tabichi, a science teacher from Kenya, received the prestigious Global Teacher Prize for his dedication to his students."

One mention of "received." One mention of the prize. The rest of the sentence adds NEW information (where he is from, why he won). That is how you earn full points.
THE "SAY IT ONCE" RULE
After you write a sentence, re-read it and ask: "Did I already say this?" If you see the same word or idea appearing twice, keep the stronger version and replace the repeat with a new detail (who, where, when, why, or how).
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Redundancy makes your writing feel like it is padding instead of informing. On a scored test, repeated information does not earn extra credit — it tells the grader you ran out of ideas. Saying it once and adding something new shows deeper understanding.

Your #2 Error: Explain WHY, Not Just WHAT

Q6 — 2 out of 3
🔍 Pattern alert: You named a challenge but did not explain what made it difficult. The question wants you to tell us WHY it was hard, not just WHAT happened.

Zane, Q6 asked you to describe a challenge you faced at school. Here is what you wrote:

YOUR ANSWER — Q6
"Yes, I have faced a challenge at school when I had to write a seven sentence paragraph essay."

You named the challenge (writing a seven-sentence paragraph). But you stopped there. The question wants you to explain what made it difficult. Was it hard to think of enough details? Did you struggle with organizing your ideas? Did you run out of things to say?

Which answer better explains WHY the challenge was hard?
A I faced a challenge when I had to write a seven sentence paragraph.
B I faced a challenge when I had to write a seven sentence paragraph because I could only think of two details, and I did not know how to stretch my ideas into enough sentences.

FULL-CREDIT VERSION
"Yes, I faced a challenge at school when I had to write a seven-sentence paragraph because I could only think of two details from the passage, and I struggled to explain why each detail mattered without repeating myself."

This version names the challenge, then uses "because" to explain WHAT made it hard. That is the difference between 2 out of 3 and 3 out of 3.
THE "BECAUSE" CHECK
Whenever a question asks about a challenge, problem, or experience, use this formula:

[What happened] + because + [why it was hard / what made it matter]

If your answer does not have a "because" (or a similar reason word like "since" or "so"), you are probably only answering WHAT, not WHY.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
On a 3-point question, naming the topic earns you partial credit. Explaining WHY it mattered is what earns the final point. One extra clause starting with "because" is often the difference between 2 out of 3 and full marks.
