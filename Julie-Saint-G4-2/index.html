#1 Fix: An Appositive Is Not a Verb

Q2 and Q10 — the same miss, twice, hiding inside a strength

Here is the most interesting thing on this test. Your appositive question, Q4, earned full marks — a big, confident appositive placed perfectly. But look at what happened on two other questions:

QUESTION 2 — 1 OUT OF 2 — “EXPAND THE FRAGMENT INTO A COMPLETE SENTENCE”
“A bluebonnet, a type of flower, with bright blue petals.”
QUESTION 10 — 1 OUT OF 3 — “WRITE ONE SENTENCE TO DESCRIBE IT”
“Chrismas, a tradition you put up a tree and get presents.”

Both answers have a lovely appositive tucked between commas — and neither one is a sentence, because nothing in them ever happens. The appositive renames the noun, but no verb ever arrives. You have gotten so quick with appositives that they are filling the space where the action belongs.

THE COVER-THE-COMMAS TEST
An appositive is extra — the sentence must survive without it. So run this check: read the sentence with the appositive skipped. “A bluebonnet … with bright blue petals” — still nothing happening. “Chrismas.” — one word. If what is left is not a complete sentence, the appositive was decorating a fragment. Add the verb: what does the bluebonnet DO? What IS Christmas?
BOTH ANSWERS, WITH THEIR VERBS
✓ “A bluebonnet, a type of flower, shows off bright blue petals every spring.”
✓ “Christmas, a tradition where you put up a tree and get presents, is my favorite part of winter.”
Which one is a complete sentence?
AThe state fair, an event with rides and games.
BThe state fair, an event with rides and games, comes to town every October.
WHY THIS IS THE #1 FIX
These two questions gave back three points — the single biggest share of the gap between this 79% and your 89%. And the fix is one habit: after you write your appositive, cover the commas and make sure the sentence still has its verb. Your appositives are already excellent. Now make sure they always ride inside a real sentence.

#2 Fix: Answer the Exact Question

Q7 — 1 out of 3 — a good sentence aimed at the wrong target

Question 7 asked: Write one sentence that describes a change you see in nature at the start of spring. Here is the answer:

QUESTION 7 — 1 OUT OF 3
“People get allergies if they are allergic to flowers.”

That is a complete, correct sentence — about people. The question asked about a change in nature: flowers blooming, trees budding, days getting longer. The sentence is connected to spring, but it never touches the thing the question actually asked for, and it cost two points.

Compare that with two answers from the same test that aimed dead-center and earned every point:

SAME TEST, FULL MARKS
✓ Q8 asked for a unique flower and what makes it interesting: “A unique flower that I've seen is a rose because it has a deep red color.”
✓ Q9 asked for a special place: “A place that is special to me is my home because that is where I live.”

Both sentences contain the exact things the question named. That is the whole trick.
The question asks: “Describe a change you see in nature at the start of spring.” Which answer earns full marks?
AAt the start of spring, the bare tree branches fill up with new white blossoms.
BIn spring, everyone starts buying sunscreen at the store.
THE KEY-WORD CHECK
Before writing a Q6–Q10 answer, find the two or three key words in the question — here they were change, nature, spring. Then check your finished sentence: are those words (or their meaning) actually inside it? If the question names a target, your sentence has to hit that target — not one standing next to it.

Your G4.2 Paragraph

16 out of 20 — a real writer’s opening; two sentences from the next level

The robots paragraph earned 16 out of 20, and it deserves a compliment first. Here it is:

“You might think that robot are scary, but they actullay help you with simple tasks at home and at a workplace. Robots can help by bringing papers to offices, help visitors find their way, and can clean floors. Robots can save many people time and effort. Don't be scared of robots be happy that they were made.”
A real hook (“You might think… but”) Specific benefits from the passage States the payoff (saves time and effort) No explanation after the evidence Two thoughts collide in the closer

The compliment: that opening move — naming the reader’s doubt and turning it around — is a genuinely sophisticated way to start a paragraph, and it answers the question immediately. Most students never try it.

The four missing points live in two places. First, the evidence gets listed but never explained — after naming what robots do, one sentence should say what that means for people. Second, the closer fuses two commands together: “Don't be scared of robots be happy that they were made” needs a break between the two thoughts.

Tap to see your paragraph one level up
Your hook and all your evidence stay — one explaining sentence gets added
YOUR PARAGRAPH, DEVELOPED (closer to 20/20)

“You might think that robots are scary, but they actually help you with simple tasks at home and at a workplace. Robots can help by bringing papers to offices, helping visitors find their way, and cleaning floors. This shows that robots take over the small, boring jobs so people can spend their time on work that matters more. Robots can save many people time and effort. Don't be scared of robots. Be happy that they were made.”

One bolded sentence in the middle does the heavy lifting: it explains what your evidence proves. The rest is a period between the two closing thoughts and the read-back fixes from earlier.

SO WHAT?
Your paragraph voice is already ahead of your grade — the hook proves it. The next level is one habit: after your list of evidence, add one sentence that starts “This shows that…” and tells the reader why the facts matter. That sentence is worth two to three points on every paragraph you write from here on.
