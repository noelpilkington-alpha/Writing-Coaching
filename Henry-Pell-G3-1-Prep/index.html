Getting ready for Alpha Standardized Writing G3.1
G3.1 PRE-TEST PREP
Your first writing test is coming.Eleven questions, 45 points, and nothing on it is new

Henry, here is the honest truth about first writing tests. The students who struggle on them almost never struggle because of writing. They struggle because they did not know how the test works: they raced through it, left a hard box empty, or typed two quick lines where twenty points were waiting. Every one of those slips can be avoided before you ever see the first question.

That is what these cards are for. No scores yet, no history, just the shape of the test and the five habits that separate a strong first test from a rough one. The test uses skills you already use every day: fixing a sentence, answering a question with a reason, writing a paragraph about something you read.

What G3.1 Looks Like

Eleven questions, 45 points, one question worth almost half
10 points
Q1–Q5 · Edit a sentence
You read a short text and fix or improve five of its sentences: combine two sentences into one, turn a statement into a question, add an appositive, finish a fragment, choose the best joining word. Each is worth 2 points: one for doing the job the instruction names, one for a clean sentence.
15 points
Q6–Q10 · Write one sentence
Five questions about the text or about your own opinion. Each wants one complete sentence that answers and gives a reason, explanation or example. Each is worth 3 points: one for the answer, one for the reason, one for a clean sentence.
20 points
Q11 · The paragraph
You read a second text, an article, and write one well-organized paragraph that answers a question about it, using facts from the article. Twenty of the 45 points live in this one box.
THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT FACT ON THIS CARD
Question 11 is worth as much as Questions 1 to 7 combined. Most first-time students spend their effort evenly across all eleven questions and leave the paragraph thin. Plan from the start to give the paragraph your longest, best effort: aim to spend at least 20 minutes on it.

One more number to know going in: 90% is a pass, which is 41 of the 45 points. That is the line the whole testing loop aims at, not necessarily on the first try. Every test tells us exactly what to work on next.

PRACTICE IT IN YOUR ALPHAWRITE LESSONS
Every lesson on combining sentences, fragments, appositives, or writing a paragraph from a prompt is a piece of this test. The pieces are the same; the test just puts them in a row.

The Game Plan

The order to do things in, and the rule that costs the most points
THE PLAN, IN ORDER
1. Read the text twice. First read: understand the story. Second read: notice the names, numbers and facts. The questions all come from the text, and the second read is where the answers hide.
2. Q1 to Q5: find the job word. Combine, question, appositive, complete sentence. Do that job and only that job. If the instruction asks what type of sentence it is, the answer is one word, not a rewritten sentence.
3. Q6 to Q10: answer, then because. One sentence that answers the question and carries its reason. Card 4 is all about this.
4. Q11: read the article twice, plan, then write six sentences. Card 5 is the recipe. Aim to spend at least 20 minutes here.
5. The final pass. Go back to Q1 and read every answer once, slowly. No box empty, every sentence ends with a period or question mark, the tricky words (their, there, to, too) came out right.
THE RULE THAT COSTS THE MOST POINTS: NEVER LEAVE A BOX BLANK
A blank box scores zero every time. A rough attempt earns partial credit, and often more than you expect: a one-sentence answer with a reason is already 2 or 3 points, and a paragraph with two facts is already in double figures. There is no prize for finishing fast, and there is always plenty of test time. Aim to give the whole test at least 40 minutes.

The Because Habit

Q6 to Q10: the third point on every one-sentence answer
THE RULE: THE ANSWER, THEN BECAUSE, IN ONE SENTENCE
Each of Q6 to Q10 is three points: one for answering the question, one for a reason, explanation or example, one for a clean complete sentence. The question almost always says explain, and that word is a point by itself. The check: did I answer the question? Did I say why, with a because, a reason or an example? Is it one whole sentence?

The pattern across the whole testing group: answers that state an opinion and never say why earn 2 of 3, over and over. Answers that carry their reason earn 3 of 3. The second half of the sentence is the difference.

A 2-OUT-OF-3 ANSWER
“Yes, I would like to learn to sail.” AN ANSWER, NO REASON
A 3-OUT-OF-3 ANSWER
Yes, I would like to learn to sail because being out on the water with only the wind to move you sounds like freedom.

Three answers that carry their reason, and one that hands in a piece:

BECAUSEThe most important moment was when the girl returned the wallet, because it showed she valued honesty over the money.
SOI would train the puppy every evening so that it learned the rules before it grew too big to argue with.
AN EXAMPLEPractice makes you better at hard things; for example, I could not ride a bike in April and by June I rode to school.
A PIECEBecause it was the right thing to do.
A reason with no answer in front of it, and not a whole sentence. Put the answer first: I would have returned the wallet because it was the right thing to do.
The question: Would you like to visit the rainforest? Explain your answer in one sentence. Which answer earns all three points?
AI would love to visit the rainforest because I have never seen animals like that outside a book.
BI would love to visit the rainforest.
The question: What lesson does the story teach about patience? Which answer earns all three points?
ABecause the boy waited all summer for the tomatoes.
BThe story teaches that patience pays off, because the boy who waited all summer ate the only tomatoes on the street.
Cover everything from because onward. Which one still leaves a whole sentence?
ABecause the bridge was closed for repairs.
BWe walked the long way home because the bridge was closed for repairs.
PRACTICE IT IN YOUR ALPHAWRITE LESSONS
Every one-sentence answer: say the answer in full, add because, so or for example, and finish the thought. Then read it back with the because part covered.

The Paragraph Recipe

Q11: 20 points, one box, one recipe you can repeat
THE RECIPE: SIX SENTENCES
The TOPIC SENTENCE answers the question in its own words and names the two reasons the paragraph will prove. Then each reason gets a DETAIL · SHOW, a fact you can point to in the article, and a DETAIL · SO WHAT: This shows plus what the fact proves. The CONCLUDING SENTENCE puts the two reasons together. The check: does sentence one answer the question? Can you point to both facts in the article? Does each fact have its This shows?

Here is a short practice article, written for this card, and the question a test might ask about it:

PRACTICE ARTICLE

The Garden on the Roof

Three years ago the roof of Lincoln Middle School was bare tar that soaked up the sun. Then a science class covered it with 40 planting boxes. In the first summer the boxes grew 300 pounds of tomatoes, beans and lettuce, which went to the school kitchen and to families in the neighborhood. The rooms under the roof stayed cooler too: the janitor measured the top floor at 6 degrees cooler on hot afternoons, so the school ran its air conditioning less. Students who had never spoken to each other weeded side by side, and the garden club grew from eight members to 45.

THE QUESTION
Explain how the roof garden helped Lincoln Middle School. Use details from the article.
THE TOPIC SENTENCE, POINT + HOW
POINTThe roof garden helped Lincoln Middle School in two ways: HOWit put food on tables, and it made the building cooler and friendlier.
The question’s own words, then two reasons. Now a reader knows what the next sentences will prove.
YOUR TURN: BUILD THE PARAGRAPH. TAP EACH THIS SHOWS AND THE SECOND FACT
TOPIC SENTENCE · POINT + HOW
The roof garden helped Lincoln Middle School in two ways: it put food on tables, and it made the building cooler and friendlier.
DETAIL 1 · SHOW
In the first summer the 40 boxes grew 300 pounds of tomatoes, beans and lettuce, which went to the school kitchen and to families in the neighborhood.
DETAIL 1 · SO WHAT: which sentence goes next?
AThis shows that a bare roof became a source of real meals for the school and the street around it.
BThe janitor measured the top floor at 6 degrees cooler on hot afternoons.
DETAIL 2 · SHOW: a fact that proves the cooler-and-friendlier reason. Which one?
AThe students won a prize from the city for the best school garden.
BThe top floor measured 6 degrees cooler on hot afternoons, and the garden club grew from eight members to 45.
DETAIL 2 · SO WHAT: which sentence goes next?
ASo the garden helped the school in many ways.
BThis shows that the garden changed the building itself and the people inside it, cooling the rooms and bringing strangers together over the weeds.
CONCLUDING SENTENCE · TOGETHER
Food for the neighborhood and a cooler, friendlier school came from the same 40 boxes, and that is why a roof of tar was worth turning into a garden.

Answer first. Two facts from the article, each with its This shows. An ending that adds the reasons up. Every full-credit paragraph in this testing group has this shape.
PLAN IT IN THE BOX FIRST
Before you write the paragraph, type a four-line plan at the top of the answer box: your answer, your two facts, your two This shows. Write the paragraph under it, then delete the plan before you submit. Planning in the box takes two minutes and it is the difference between a paragraph that wanders and one that scores.
PRACTICE IT IN YOUR ALPHAWRITE LESSONS
Every paragraph lesson is this recipe: answer, fact, This shows, fact, This shows, together. Run it until it is automatic.

The Rubrics: How Every Question Is Scored

See the target before test day. Aim at it, then check against it

Every question is scored against a rubric, a list of exactly what full credit contains. Here are the three, in the grader’s own terms, so that on test day you are aiming at a target you have already seen.

Q1–Q5 · EDIT A SENTENCE · 2 POINTS EACH
Skill, 1 point: the sentence does exactly the job the instruction names and keeps the original meaning. An appositive has to be a noun phrase that renames (my dogs, Lucy and Rocket); a that clause or an and is not one. Doing a different job scores 0 here, however good the sentence.
Conventions, 1 point: a reader can read the sentence smoothly with the meaning never in doubt, even with one or two slips. It drops to 0 only for a fragment, a run-on, or errors so dense they get in the way.
So: 2 = the job done, in a clean sentence. 1 = the job done with a messy sentence, or a clean sentence doing the wrong job. 0 = neither, or an empty box.
Q6–Q10 · WRITE ONE SENTENCE · 3 POINTS EACH
Answer, 1 point, and reason, 1 point: the sentence clearly answers the question and gives a reason, explanation or example that adds something, saying why, how, or what happens as a result. An answer with no reason, or a reason that only repeats the event, earns the answer point alone. A feeling counts as a reason.
Conventions, 1 point: first, is it a complete sentence? A fragment, a sentence starting at Because with no main part, or two sentences on one comma scores 0 here. Then, if it reads smoothly, 1, even with a slip or two.
So: 3 = answer, reason, whole sentence. 2 = usually the reason missing. 1 = an answer that is not a sentence. 0 = one word, off topic, or an empty box.
Q11 · THE PARAGRAPH · 20 POINTS
Ideas and content, 0 to 10: 10 is exceptional; 8 to 9 fully developed; 7 facts present, explanation partial; 5 to 6 accurate facts listed with no This shows; 3 to 4 thin; 1 to 2 opinion only; 0 off topic. Three caps to know: a paragraph that never answers the specific question is capped at 5. A paragraph with no facts from the article is capped at 6. A paragraph under about 40 words is capped at 6.
Organization, 0 to 5: 5 has an opening with a central idea, details in a sensible order, and a closing that ties back. One of those weak or missing is 4. A bare one-sentence answer is 0 or 1.
Conventions, 0 to 5: 5 has few errors; one clear pattern of errors is 3; a one- or two-sentence answer scores 0 to 2 no matter how clean it is. Misspelling the same word twice counts once.
So: the recipe on card 5 is built from this rubric. Answer first (the cap at 5), facts from the article (the cap at 6), six sentences (the 40-word cap and the conventions floor), a This shows after each fact (the climb from 6 to 8 or 9).
WHAT TO DO WITH THE RUBRICS: TWO MOVES
Before you answer, know which rubric you are aiming at: a one-sentence answer needs its because; the paragraph needs the answer first and facts with their This shows. After you answer, use the rubric as your checklist. For the paragraph you do not even have to remember it: the prompt prints its own list under Your paragraph should. Check your paragraph against that list, line by line, before you submit.
The instruction: Combine the two sentences into one. A student writes two correct sentences with a period between them. What does the skill point do?
AIt is earned, because both sentences are correct.
BIt is lost, because the sentences were not combined, however clean they are.
A paragraph about the roof garden is five clean sentences about how to grow tomatoes, and never says how the garden helped the school. Which cap applies?
AThe ideas score is capped at 5, because the specific question was not answered.
BNo cap, because the sentences are clean and on the topic of gardens.
Which one-sentence answer earns the conventions point?
AI would keep the stray cat, it followed me home in the rain.
BI would keep the stray cat because it followed me home in the rain.
PRACTICE IT IN YOUR ALPHAWRITE LESSONS
Before you submit any lesson answer, name the rubric line it is aiming at. The habit of aiming is what the test scores.
