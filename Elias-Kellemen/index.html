Q1: What's an Appositive?

A short description tucked inside the sentence

The question asked you to combine two sentences using an appositive:

YOU WROTE
A colony has one leader, that controls all of the other bees, and she is the queen bee.

You had the right idea — you tried to add information about the queen bee inside the sentence. But what you wrote is a "that" clause, not an appositive. An appositive is a short renaming phrase placed right after a noun, set off by commas.

WITH A PROPER APPOSITIVE
A colony has one leader, the queen bee, who controls all of the other bees.
THE APPOSITIVE PATTERN
Noun, [short renaming phrase], rest of sentence.

The appositive renames the noun. It answers the question: "Who is that?" or "What is that?"

More examples:
• The queen bee, the colony's leader, controls all the other bees.
• Honey, a sweet golden liquid, is made inside the hive.
• Worker bees, the smallest bees in the colony, collect nectar from flowers.

Notice how each appositive is just a short phrase (not a full clause with a verb like "that controls"). It simply renames the noun before it.

Q4: Question Marks Matter

How does the honey become food for the bees during the winter?

This is so close to a perfect answer. One tiny punctuation mark cost you half a point.

THE QUESTION MARK CHECK
Every time you write a sentence that starts with one of these words, it must end with a question mark:

Who • What • When • Where • Why • How • Do • Does • Did • Is • Are • Can • Will

Quick test: Read your sentence out loud. Does your voice go up at the end? Then it's a question — use ? not .

Tighten Your Sentences

Q3 — Combine two sentences:

YOU WROTE
The bees bring the nectar back to the hive and after that they turn the nectar into honey.

"And after that" is wordy. When you combine sentences with "and," you don't need extra filler words.

TIGHTER VERSION

THE TIGHTENING TRICK
After you write a sentence, read it back and ask:

• Did I say the same word twice? If so, cut one.
• Can I replace 3+ words with 1-2 words? "And after that" → "and then." "They need to go to" → "they fly to."

Shorter sentences are usually stronger sentences.
