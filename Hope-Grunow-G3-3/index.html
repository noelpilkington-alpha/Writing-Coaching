Question 1 Fix — Changing Sentence Types

Can you spot the problem? Then we'll fix it together.
📈 You've missed Changing Sentence Types questions on 2 of your last 4 tries — but you got it perfect on G3.1 and G3.2, which proves you CAN do this. The pattern: small details like capital letters get missed when you're moving fast.

The question asked you to change this sentence into a question: "Anna named the seal Sally."

Which question is written correctly?
A Did Anna name the seal sally?
B Did Anna name the seal Sally?
YOU WROTE
"Did Anna name the seal sally?"
CORRECT VERSION
"Did Anna name the seal Sally?"

Your question structure was great — you correctly used "Did" at the beginning and added a question mark. The only thing that cost you points was the lowercase "s" in "sally." Sally is a name, so it always gets a capital letter.

REMEMBER THIS RULE
When you change a sentence into a question, the words might move around, but names always keep their capital letters. Before you move on, check: "Did I capitalize every name?"
Why this matters for your writing
In your paragraph writing, capitalizing names correctly shows that you're paying attention to detail. Grading feedback always checks for proper capitalization of names — it's one of the easiest points to keep!

Question 3 Fix — Adding Appositives

First, can you tell the difference? Then we'll break it down.

The question asked you to rewrite this sentence and add an appositive (a short description set off by commas): "Sally swam to the other seals."

Which sentence correctly adds an appositive?
A Sally swam to the other seals kindly.
B Sally, the baby seal, swam to the other seals.
YOU WROTE
"Sally swam to the other seals kindly"
BETTER VERSION
"Sally, the baby seal, swam to the other seals."

You added a word to the sentence, which shows good effort! But "kindly" describes how she swam — it doesn't tell us who Sally is. An appositive is a little description that renames the noun, tucked between commas.

HOW TO SPOT AN APPOSITIVE
An appositive answers the question: "Who or what is that?"
• "Sally" → Who is Sally? → "the baby seal"
• Put it right after the name, with commas on both sides: "Sally, the baby seal, swam..."
See another example
Sentence: "My dog ran across the yard."
Ask: Who is my dog? → Rocket, a golden retriever
With appositive: "My dog, Rocket, a golden retriever, ran across the yard."

You actually did this perfectly on G3.1: "My family has two dogs, Lucy and Rocket." That was a great appositive!
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Appositives make your writing more interesting because they pack extra information into a single sentence. In your paragraph writing, using appositives helps you give the reader more detail without needing extra sentences.

Question 5 Fix — Connecting Words

This one is about picking the right word to connect ideas.
📈 On G3.1 you got the "because, but, so" question 100% correct. This time the question was a little different — instead of combining two sentences, you had to fill in a blank with a connecting word. Same skill, different format.

The question gave you a sentence with a blank at the beginning:

"___ there was no sign of the seals, the family placed Sally in a fish box and motored into the bay."

Which word best fills the blank?
A Later there was no sign of the seals, the family placed Sally in a fish box and motored into the bay.
B Because there was no sign of the seals, the family placed Sally in a fish box and motored into the bay.
YOU WROTE
"Later there was no sign of the seals, the family placed Sally in a fish box and motored into the bay."
CORRECT ANSWER
"Because there was no sign of the seals, the family placed Sally in a fish box and motored into the bay."

"Later" tells you when, but the sentence needs a word that tells you why. The family put Sally in the box because they couldn't find the other seals. The word "because" connects the reason to the action.

QUICK CHECK FOR CONNECTING WORDS
When you see a blank in a sentence, ask yourself:
• Does this sentence explain WHY something happened? → Use because or since
• Does one idea go against the other? → Use but
• Does one thing lead to another? → Use so
See it in action
Why? "I wore my coat because it was cold."
Against? "It was cold, but I still went outside."
Lead to? "It was cold, so I wore my coat."
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Connecting words are the glue that holds your writing together. In your paragraph, using words like "because" and "so" shows the reader how your ideas are connected — not just what happened, but why.

Questions 7 & 8 Fix — Writing Complete Sentences

Two questions, one skill: giving a full answer.

Both of these questions asked you to write one complete sentence with an explanation. Let's look at them together.

Which answer is a complete sentence with an explanation?
A When the seal found its family.
B The most meaningful moment was when the seal found its family because it showed that the family's hard work paid off.

Question 7: "What do you think was the most meaningful moment in the story? Explain why in one sentence."

YOU WROTE
"When the seal found its family."
STRONGER VERSION
"The most meaningful moment was when the seal found its family because it showed that helping others is always worth the effort."

Your answer names the right moment, but it starts with "When" and stops — that makes it a fragment (an unfinished thought). To fix it, start with a full subject ("The most meaningful moment was...") and then add because + your reason.

Question 8: "Was it a good idea to name the seal Sally? Explain your opinion in one sentence."

YOU WROTE
"No because I feel like that is not a good name for seals."
STRONGER VERSION
"No, it was not a good idea to name the seal Sally because giving it a name might make it harder to let it go back to the wild."

This one was closer — it IS a complete sentence and you gave a reason. But the reason "that is not a good name for seals" doesn't explain why. A stronger answer gives a specific reason the reader can understand.

THE "ANSWER + BECAUSE" PATTERN
Whenever a question says "explain," use this pattern:
[Repeat the question as a statement] + because + [your reason]

Example: "Would you help the seal?" → "I would help the seal because every animal deserves to be with its family."

You already did this perfectly on Question 6 and Question 9!
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
This same skill is what makes paragraphs strong. Every time you give evidence in a paragraph, you need to explain why it matters. The "answer + because" pattern trains you to always give your reason.

Question 11 Fix — Building a Strong Paragraph

Your paragraph had a good start — it just needs more.
📈 This is part of a pattern across your last 3 tests. Your paragraph scores have gone 70% → 65% → 55%. The reason is the same each time: your ideas are there, but there aren't enough details. The structure of your writing is the consistent difference-maker.

The question asked you to write one well-organized paragraph explaining how Satoshi Tajiri's childhood influenced the creation of the game.

Which paragraph would earn more points?
A It influenced Tajiri because he loved catching bugs when he was little and then when he was older he loved video games.
B Satoshi Tajiri's childhood had a big influence on the creation of the game. When he was young, he loved exploring nature and catching bugs. This love of collecting creatures later inspired the idea of catching and collecting characters in a game. As he grew older, he also became interested in video games and wanted to combine his two interests. Because of his childhood hobbies, Tajiri created a game that millions of people enjoy today.
YOU WROTE
"It influenced Tajiri because he loved catching bugs when he was little and then when he was older he loved video games."

You had the right idea — you mentioned catching bugs and loving video games, which are both from the article. That's a great start! The issue is that your paragraph is only one sentence and doesn't include enough detail. Here's what a stronger version looks like:

Tap to see a stronger paragraph
Think about what's missing from yours first
STRONGER PARAGRAPH
[Topic Sentence] Satoshi Tajiri's childhood had a big influence on the creation of the game.
[Detail from article] When he was young, Tajiri loved exploring nature and catching bugs near his home.
[Explain why it matters] This love of collecting creatures gave him the idea for a game where players could catch and collect characters.
[More detail] As he grew up, Tajiri also became interested in video games and wanted to combine his two interests.
[Explain why] Because of his childhood hobbies, he was able to create something that felt real and exciting to players.
[Closing sentence] Tajiri's childhood love of nature and games came together to inspire one of the most popular games ever made.
Paragraph Building Blocks
Topic Sentence State the main idea — answer the question right away
Detail Give a fact or example from the article
Explain why Tell the reader WHY that detail matters
More Detail Give a second fact or example from the article
Explain why Tell the reader WHY this detail matters too
Closing Wrap up your main idea in different words
THE BIG DIFFERENCE
Your paragraph had the right ideas but was only 1 sentence. A strong paragraph needs at least 4–6 sentences. After every detail from the article, ask yourself: "So what? Why does this matter?" That answer becomes your next sentence.
A quick planning trick for paragraph questions
Before you start writing, jot down in the margin:
1. Topic sentence (answer the question)
2. First detail from the article
3. Why it matters
4. Second detail from the article
5. Why it matters
6. Closing sentence

This takes about 30 seconds and makes sure you don't stop too early!
