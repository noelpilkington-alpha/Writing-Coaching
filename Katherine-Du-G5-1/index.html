Make the Appositive Actually Add Detail (Q1)

An appositive is only worth full credit when it paints a clearer picture

Katherine, on Q1 the prompt asked you to revise sentence 2 to include an appositive that gives more details about Ms. Kessler and “helps paint a clearer picture of her.” Your revision added an appositive — which is great — but it was very short, so Q1 came in at 0.5 out of 2.

YOU WROTE (Q1)
"Today Ms. Kessler, my teacher, wasn't at her desk."
“My teacher” is a valid appositive — but it doesn't add any picture the reader didn't already have. An appositive is supposed to tell the reader something new.
THE RULE
An appositive is a noun phrase, tucked between commas, that renames a noun and gives the reader more information. When a prompt says “paint a clearer picture,” the appositive has to add something the reader didn't already know.

Weak: Today Ms. Kessler, my teacher, wasn't at her desk. (reader already knew that)
Strong: Today Ms. Kessler, a calm woman with a coffee mug and a stack of grading papers, wasn't at her desk. (reader now pictures her)
A STRONGER APPOSITIVE (KATHERINE'S IDEA, FULLER DETAIL)
"Today Ms. Kessler, the kind fifth-grade language arts teacher who always arrived before the morning bell, wasn't at her desk."
Same sentence, same meaning. The appositive now shows the reader who Ms. Kessler is — her subject, her personality, her usual habit. That's what earns full credit.
Which revision includes an appositive that paints a clearer picture?
A Today Ms. Kessler, a person, wasn't at her desk.
B Today Ms. Kessler, a warm and patient teacher who always greeted students at the door, wasn't at her desk.
THE “PAINT A PICTURE” TEST
Before you submit a Q1 appositive, ask yourself: “If I deleted this appositive, would the reader lose a real detail about the person?” If the answer is no, the appositive is too thin. Add one of these:

• their job or role (the fifth-grade teacher)
• a personality trait (patient and warm)
• a habit or appearance (who always arrived early)

Stack two of those together and the appositive earns full credit.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q1 was worth 2 points and came in at 0.5. A fuller appositive recovers 1.5 points by itself — and Q1-style appositive questions appear on nearly every G5 test. This is a habit that compounds.

Combine With a Connector, Not a Comma (Q3)

A comma alone can't hold two complete thoughts together

Katherine, on Q3 you were asked to combine sentences 7 and 8 into one complete sentence. You had the right idea — you put both events into one sentence — but the way they were joined created a comma splice. That's why Q3 came in at 0.5 out of 3.

YOU WROTE (Q3)
"I stood up straight, my backpack knonked Ms. Kessler's coffee mug onto the floor."
Two things to catch: the comma splice (two complete thoughts joined with just a comma) and a spelling slip (“knonked” → “knocked”).
THE RULE
“I stood up straight” is a complete thought. “My backpack knocked Ms. Kessler's coffee mug onto the floor” is also a complete thought. A single comma can't hold two complete thoughts together — that's a comma splice.

The fix is to join them with a connector word that shows the relationship, or to make one of them a dependent clause:

Option 1 — connector word: I stood up straight, but my backpack knocked Ms. Kessler's coffee mug onto the floor.
Option 2 — dependent clause: When I stood up straight, my backpack knocked Ms. Kessler's coffee mug onto the floor.
A STRONGER COMBINE (KATHERINE'S IDEAS, CLEAN CONNECTOR)
"When I stood up straight, my backpack knocked Ms. Kessler's coffee mug onto the floor."
Same events, same order. The word “when” turns the first part into a dependent clause, so the comma now correctly links a dependent clause to a complete thought. Clean combine, full credit.
Which version is a clean combine of “I stood up straight” and “My backpack knocked the mug onto the floor”?
A I stood up straight, my backpack knocked the mug onto the floor.
B When I stood up straight, my backpack knocked the mug onto the floor.
THE COMMA-SPLICE CHECK
Before you submit any combine, ask: “Could each half stand alone as its own sentence?”

If the answer is yes, a comma alone is not enough. Pick one:
• Add a connector word: and, but, so, because, when, while
• Turn one half into a dependent clause with when/while/since/because
• Replace the comma with a period and start a new sentence

Any of those three fixes turns a comma splice into a proper combine.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q3 was worth 3 points and came in at 0.5 — the biggest single point gap on Q1–Q10 for this test. Catching the comma splice and the “knonked” spelling slip on a read-back pass recovers most of those 2.5 points.

YOUR PARAGRAPH — POLISHED TO 19–20/20

New research reveals many new horse abilities. For example, research at the University of Sussex in England shows that horses have 17 different facial expressions, which are even more expressions than dogs and chimpanzees! Furthermore, Sussex researchers found out that horses could also read some human facial expressions. Horses would get frightened when they saw photos of angry humans but feel calm when they saw photos of happy humans. Research also shows that horses are able to use body language to communicate. In conclusion, research proves that horses may have been watching and studying us for years while humans have just started watching them.

ONE QUOTE-PUNCTUATION FIX
Inside your paragraph you wrote: “Take the blanket off.”, the horizontal bar meant...

When a quoted phrase is part of a list item, you don't need both a period inside the quote and a comma after it. One piece of punctuation does the job. Use a comma inside the quote for list items: “Take the blanket off,” the horizontal bar meant...
