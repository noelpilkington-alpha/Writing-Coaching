#1 Focus: Sentence Types

Q2: a statement got labeled a command

James, Question 2 asked: "What type of sentence is the following: Historians believe El Silbo began with the island's earliest inhabitants." Here is the label you chose:

YOUR ANSWER (G7.3 Q2)
"Historians believe El Silbo began with the island's earliest inhabitants." → you chose imperative

An imperative sentence commands someone to do something, and it usually starts with the verb: "Believe the historians." This sentence does the opposite — it states a fact about what historians believe, and it ends with a period. That makes it declarative.

THE TWO-SECOND SHORTCUT
Ask two questions: (1) Is it bossing someone around? If no one is being told to do anything, it is not imperative. (2) What is the ending mark? A period on a fact = declarative. A question mark = interrogative. An exclamation point = exclamatory. A command (often starting with the verb) = imperative.
CORRECT ANSWER
Declarative — the sentence states what historians believe and ends with a period. Nobody is being ordered to do anything.
One of these is declarative. Which one?
A "Practice your whistling every day after school."
B "Students on La Gomera practice their whistling in required classes."
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Sentence-type questions are pure points once the two-second shortcut is a habit: is anyone being told to do something, and what is the ending mark? Run those two checks and this question type is automatic — that is one of the two multiple-choice points back already.

#2 Focus: The Appositive Test

Q4: the commas grabbed the verb instead of a renaming phrase

Question 4 asked: "Which sentence correctly uses an appositive?" Here is the option you picked:

YOUR ANSWER (G7.3 Q4)
"Cyro, was a young islander, who loved his goats."

Read it aloud and you can hear the problem: the commas wrap the verb ("was a young islander"). Lift that part out and what is left is "Cyro who loved his goats" — there is no working spine of a sentence. An appositive is a noun phrase that renames the noun beside it — it never contains the sentence's main verb.

THE CORRECT OPTION
"Cyro, a young islander, returned home to keep El Silbo alive."
Remove the appositive and a complete sentence remains: "Cyro returned home to keep El Silbo alive." That is the test.
THE REMOVE-IT TEST
Lift out whatever sits between the commas. If a complete sentence remains and the removed phrase renames the noun next to it, it is a real appositive. If the sentence breaks, the commas grabbed the verb — and that option is wrong, every time.
Which sentence uses a real appositive?
A "La Gomera, a small island near Africa, has kept El Silbo alive for centuries."
B "La Gomera, is a small island, that has kept El Silbo alive."
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
The remove-it test turns appositive questions from a judgment call into a mechanical check: take the phrase out, and the sentence must still stand. That is the second multiple-choice point back — and appositives are also a great tool for your own essays, packing extra detail into one smooth sentence.
