Answer the EXACT Question

When a question asks "why," give a reason
🔍 Pattern alert: On G3.2 this showed up twice — Question 5 and Question 6. Both answers were true, but neither matched the exact thing the question was asking for.

Carson, look at Question 6. The passage is about the Hershey Bears "Teddy Bear Toss," where fans threw 45,650 stuffed animals onto the ice. The question asked why the event is so popular. Here is what you wrote:

YOU WROTE (G3.2 Q6 — earned 1 of 3 points)
"The name of the team is bears."

That sentence is true — but it tells us the team's name, not why the event is popular. The question asked for a reason, and the answer went to a different topic.

The question asks: "Why is the Teddy Bear Toss so popular?" Which answer actually answers it?
A The fans throw stuffed animals onto the ice.
B The Teddy Bear Toss is popular because the stuffed animals are donated to children who need them.
THE FIX: MATCH THE QUESTION WORD
Before you answer, find the question word and let it tell you what to do:

• Why? → give a reason (use "because")
• How? → tell the way something happens
• What does this show? → explain what it means, then give the reason

Question 5 lost points the same way — you wrote "They are very generous." That's a good start, but it stops too soon. Add the reason: "They are very generous because they donated 45,650 stuffed animals to children."

Reread for Complete Sentences

A quick second read catches missing words and end marks
🔍 Pattern alert: On G3.2, both Question 1 and Question 2 lost a point to a tiny slip — a missing word in one, and a spelling slip plus a missing period in the other. A reread would have caught both.

Carson, here is your Question 1. The task was to rewrite a sentence in a shorter, clearer way. You had the right idea, but one important word got left out:

YOU WROTE (G3.2 Q1 — earned 1 of 2 points)
"That day, Hershey Bears their record by collecting 45,650 stuffed animals."

Read it slowly: "Hershey Bears their record" is missing the action word. The Bears broke their record — but the word "broke" never made it into the sentence. The idea was in your head; it just didn't all reach the page.

Which version is a complete sentence?
A That day, the Hershey Bears their record by collecting 45,650 stuffed animals.
B That day, the Hershey Bears broke their record by collecting 45,650 stuffed animals.
THE FIX: THE 10-SECOND REREAD
Before you move to the next question, read your sentence out loud in your head and ask two things:

1. Does it have an action word? Every sentence needs a word that tells what someone or something does (broke, scored, collected).
2. Does it end with the right mark? A telling sentence ends with a period. A question ends with a question mark.

On Question 2 you wrote: "Fans tossed bears on to the ice becausee the bears scored a goal" — "because" had an extra letter, and the sentence had no period at the end. The reread catches both.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Questions 1 through 5 are each worth 2 points. A missing word or a missing period turns a 2 into a 1. You clearly know what you mean — the reread just makes sure all of it lands on the page so you keep the full points.

Develop Your Paragraph

Great plan — now add a detail to each part
🔍 Pattern alert: Your Question 11 paragraph scored 12 of 20. The structure is genuinely good — what holds the score down is that each part stays a little thin and list-like.

Carson, your Question 11 was about "One Book, One Camel" — the camel library that carries books around Garissa, Kenya. Look at what you wrote — you used a clear First / Next / Last plan, which is exactly right:

"First, the camels go all around Kenya. Next, they give them to schools to help them read and write. Last, family's in Kenya love the books."
Clear First / Next / Last plan Each part is thin A bit list-like

You have the bones of a strong paragraph. The fix is not to change your plan — it is to add one specific detail from the article to each point so it stops feeling like a list and starts feeling like a story.

Which version of the "Next" sentence earns a higher paragraph score?
A Next, the books are good and the kids like them a lot.
B Next, the camels carry the books to schools in Garissa so students can learn to read and write.

Same plan — one detail added to each part
YOUR PLAN, DEVELOPED (higher-scoring version)
"The camel library brings books to families in Garissa, Kenya. First, the camels travel all around the dry countryside, going to villages that are far from any library. Next, they carry the books to schools so students can learn to read and write. Last, families in Kenya love the books because reading is now a part of their day. The camel library makes books a treasure for everyone in Garissa."
THE FIX: ADD A DETAIL TO EACH PART
Keep your First / Next / Last plan exactly as it is. Then, for each part, ask: "What is one specific detail from the article I can add here?" A specific detail is a name, a place, a number, or what someone did — like Garissa, the camels traveling the countryside, or learning to read and write. One detail per part turns a list into a paragraph.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Question 11 is worth 20 points — almost half the whole test. Your plan is already worth real credit. Adding one specific detail to each part is the single fastest way to move your paragraph from 12 of 20 toward the high teens, and your whole score into the 80s.
