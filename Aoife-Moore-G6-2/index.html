None of these are ideas problems. Every one of them is a your-brain-moved-on-but-your-fingers-stuck problem. A 4-minute proofread pass catches all of them.

THE BACKWARDS-SENTENCE TRICK
When you proofread forward, your brain auto-fills what it expected to see, not what is actually on the page. That is why "Amimated" slipped through.

Read each sentence starting from the last sentence and working backward. Not word-by-word backward — sentence-by-sentence. Your brain has to process each one on its own, without the story flow carrying you past errors. Spelling and typo catches jump from ~50% to ~90% with this one trick.
Your essay has been through one proofread pass. Which version caught all the spelling errors?
A Animated kids movies teach many valuble life lessons. In the begining, we see Grug and his daughter build a better relationship.
B Animated kids movies teach many valuable life lessons. In the beginning, we see Grug and his daughter build a better relationship.
THE COST OF A SKIPPED PROOFREAD
Every 2–3 spelling errors on a G6 essay costs 1 point on the mechanics row of the rubric. Your essay had 8 slips — that is ~3 points left on the table. A 4-minute backward-read catches most of them, and your score jumps from 15/20 to 17/20 or 18/20 on that change alone.

Your #1 MC Miss — Conjunction Logic

Q3: picking the word that matches the relationship between ideas

Aoife, you got 9 out of 10 multiple choice questions right. The one miss was Q3 — combining two sentences with a conjunction. This one is worth studying because the pattern comes up in essays too (Card 3 showed some of your comma splices, which are the same idea).

Here is the Q3 item you saw, where the prompt asked you to combine these two sentences: "Animated movies are fun. They also include valuable lessons."

Which conjunction correctly combines the two sentences?
A Animated movies are fun because they include valuable lessons.
B Animated movies are fun but they include valuable lessons.
THE CONJUNCTION DECISION TREE
Before you pick a conjunction, read the two sentences and ask: what is the actual relationship between them?

• Cause and effect? → because, so, therefore, as a result
• Contrast or addition? → but, yet, however, although
• Both true together? → and, also, plus, in addition
• Condition? → if, when, unless, until

The grammar can be correct and the conjunction still wrong. Always name the relationship first, then pick the word that matches.
WHY THE Q3 TRAP WORKS
The wrong answer on Q3 used "because," which sounds smart and grown-up. That is the trap. "Because" means one thing causes the other. Animated movies aren't fun BECAUSE they have lessons — they are fun AND they have lessons. Two separate good things.

Whenever you see "because" as an option, stop and ask: is one of these actually causing the other? If you can't finish the sentence "this happens because of that," then "because" is wrong.

Your #2 Growth Area — One Topic Per Body Paragraph

Where the essay structure points went missing

Aoife, this is the single biggest lift on your essay. The G6 prompt asks for 5 clear paragraphs: intro + 3 body paragraphs + conclusion. Each body paragraph covers ONE topic. Your essay had an intro, a conclusion, and — in between — one giant middle paragraph that packed four different topics into a single block.

Here is your middle paragraph, with the topic shifts marked. Watch how many times the topic changes inside one paragraph:

YOUR MIDDLE PARAGRAPH (CONDENSED, WITH TOPIC SHIFTS MARKED)
[TOPIC 1: problem-solving] "Another real world skill is animated movies teach is problem solving skills that help kids think creatively… Almost all animated movies with problem solving skills will help show kids different ways to solve problems…"

[TOPIC 2: compassion/empathy] "Animated movies also show kids how to be compassionate and grow empathy. Animated movies usually show their characters being selfless, kind, and compassionate with one another…"

[TOPIC 3: failing is okay] "Animated movies show kids that failing is okay and they do not have to be perfect all the time. A great example of this is Mike from the movie Monsters University…"

[TOPIC 4: bravery] "Animated kid films show children how to be brave by facing your fears and taking on scary things. Take the movie Brave…"

Four topics in one paragraph. Each one is good — you have real movie examples and real analysis. The problem is that the grader reads this as one paragraph, not four, and the rubric row for "5 clear paragraphs" can't give you credit when three of them are merged into one block.

THE ONE-TOPIC RULE
Every body paragraph gets one topic. When you find yourself starting a new idea, that is the signal to hit Enter and start a new paragraph.

A good test: can you write a one-word label for the paragraph? Body 1 = "family." Body 2 = "problem-solving." Body 3 = "bravery." If your label would need an "and" in it ("compassion AND failing AND bravery"), you are writing two or three paragraphs mashed together.
Which version is a properly structured body paragraph on one topic?
A Animated movies teach kids problem-solving by showing characters work through tough situations. In Finding Nemo, Marlin solves one problem after another to find his son. Animated movies also show kids how to be compassionate and grow empathy toward others.
B Animated movies teach kids problem-solving by showing characters work through tough situations. In Finding Nemo, Marlin solves one problem after another to find his son. This shows kids that tough situations can be broken into smaller steps, and that persistence matters when you are stuck.
BEFORE YOU START WRITING, PLAN THE THREE TOPICS
In your planning space (or at the top of your response box), write three one-word labels before you start drafting:

• Body 1: ________
• Body 2: ________
• Body 3: ________

For your G6.2 essay, the labels could have been: Family / Problem-Solving / Bravery. Three labels, three body paragraphs, guaranteed rubric credit for structure.
