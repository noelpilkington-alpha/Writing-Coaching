One Idea, One Sentence

Your #1 fix: stop joining two complete thoughts with a comma

Beau, your biggest point-leak on G4.5 was joining two complete thoughts with just a comma. When two sentences could each stand on their own, a comma is not enough to hold them together. Here are two real examples from your test:

FROM G4.5 Q3 (combine into one sentence)
"...a jingle dress dancer, the dress making soft jingle sound when dancing, she also spends..."
Two full thoughts are strung together with commas. The reader loses track of where one ends.
FROM G4.5 Q10
"One way powwow clothing is like a special outfit for my family, is that the annual wearing of it is similar to what we do at home."
A comma splits the subject from the rest, joining ideas that need a cleaner sentence.
Which version is written correctly?
A She is a jingle dress dancer, the dress makes a soft sound when she dances.
B She is a jingle dress dancer. The dress makes a soft sound when she dances.
THE RULE
If you have two complete thoughts — each one could be its own sentence — do not join them with just a comma. You have two clean choices:

1. Use a period and start a new sentence with a capital letter.
2. Add a joining word after the comma: and, but, so, or because.

✗ "She is a dancer, the dress makes a soft sound."
✓ "She is a dancer. The dress makes a soft sound."
✓ "She is a dancer, and the dress makes a soft sound."
THE "TWO SENTENCES?" TEST
Cover everything after the comma and read what is left. Is it a complete sentence? Now read what comes after the comma on its own. Is that a complete sentence too? If both are complete, the comma alone is not enough — use a period or add a joining word.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Comma splices cost you on Q3, Q10, and inside your paragraph — that is the single biggest reason G4.5 dipped. Fixing them is worth several points and costs you nothing but one careful read-back.

A Real Question Asks Something

Q5: a "?" at the end does not make a statement into a question

Beau, Question 5 asked you to rewrite the sentence as a question. You added a question mark, but the sentence underneath was still a statement — it just had a "?" tacked on the end. Here is what you wrote:

FROM G4.5 Q5 (rewrite as a question)
"I think the shawl dance began when shawls replaced the blankets young women once wore?"
"I think..." is a statement. Adding "?" does not turn it into a real question.

A real question asks something. It usually starts with a question word like Did, Does, Is, Was, When, Why, or How — and then it ends with a question mark.

Which one is a real question?
A I think the shawl dance began when shawls replaced the old blankets?
B Did the shawl dance begin when shawls replaced the old blankets?
THE RULE
To turn a statement into a question, do not just add a "?". Flip it so it actually asks:

✗ Statement: "The shawl dance began when shawls replaced blankets."
✗ Statement + "?": "I think the shawl dance began when shawls replaced blankets?"
✓ Real question: "Did the shawl dance begin when shawls replaced blankets?"

Start with a question word — Did, Does, Is, Was, When, Why, How — and end with a question mark.
QUICK CHECK
Read your answer out loud. Does it sound like a question someone would ask? If it sounds like you are telling somebody something, it is still a statement — flip it to start with Did, Does, Is, or Was.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q5 is a guaranteed point when you start with a question word. Watch for the words "I think" or "I believe" — those are statement-starters, so if you see them on a "rewrite as a question" item, flip the sentence.

MODEL POLISHED PARAGRAPH (19–20/20 level)

Native stories should be celebrated for two important reasons. My first reason is that people told these stories to children so their history would never be forgotten.
