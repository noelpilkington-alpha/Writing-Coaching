Q2: Comma + Conjunction Rule

Where the comma goes when you join two sentences

The question asked you to combine two sentences using a conjunction (and, but, or, so). Here's what you wrote:

YOU WROTE
Jaylen trudged inside, shoulders drooping but, Kim stayed behind, eyeing the car.

You had the right idea — use "but" to join the two parts. But the comma is in the wrong spot. Look at where it should go:

CORRECT
Jaylen trudged inside, shoulders drooping, but Kim stayed behind, eyeing the car.

The comma goes before the conjunction, not after it.

THE COMMA + CONJUNCTION RULE
When you join two complete sentences with a conjunction:

[Sentence 1] , but [Sentence 2].

The comma always comes right before the joining word:
• Kim had a plan, so she grabbed the tent.
• Jaylen was upset, but Kim cheered him up.
• They couldn't go to the park, and it started to rain.

Never put a comma after the conjunction: "but, Kim" is wrong. "but Kim" (with comma before "but") is right.

Run-On Sentences

Two sentences smashed together need a fix

In your VR Field Trips paragraph, you wrote a run-on sentence — two complete thoughts joined with just a comma:

YOU WROTE
VR headsets are changing learning, they are being used in classrooms.

This is called a comma splice. Each part is a complete sentence, so a comma alone can't hold them together. You have three ways to fix it:

FIX 1 — PERIOD (simplest)
VR headsets are changing learning. They are being used in classrooms.
FIX 2 — COMMA + CONJUNCTION
VR headsets are changing learning, and they are being used in classrooms.
FIX 3 — SEMICOLON (advanced)
VR headsets are changing learning; they are being used in classrooms.

You already tried a semicolon on Q3 — that's the right instinct! A semicolon works when two sentences are closely related. Just remember: a comma by itself is never enough to join two complete sentences.

THE "TWO SENTENCES" TEST
Read each part on its own. If both parts could stand alone as complete sentences, you need one of the three fixes above — not just a comma.

• "VR headsets are changing learning" ← complete sentence
• "They are being used in classrooms" ← complete sentence
• Comma alone? Not enough. Add a period, conjunction, or semicolon.

Spelling Spotlight

Words to practice before next time

You had several spelling errors across your test. The good news: these are all common words that you can memorize with practice.

exited → excited
reding → reading
dissapointed → disappointed
succed → succeed
activites → activities
SPELLING TRICKS
excited — Think "I'm excited to EXIT" — both have "exit" in them, but excited adds a c before the t.

disappointed — One s, two p's: dis-app-ointed. Think: "I was disappointed that I got one s but my pp was double."

succeed — Double c, double e: succ-eed.

activities — It comes from "active" + "ities": activities, not activites.

Try writing each word correctly three times before your next test.
