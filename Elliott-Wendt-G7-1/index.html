Q7 Fix — Matching a Sentence to the Topic

The only MC miss — a subtle one. Let's unpack it.
🔍 This question type asks you to pick the sentence that best matches a given topic. The trap: picking a sentence that's true and related, but names the cause rather than the actual benefit the topic is asking about.

The topic was: "Benefits for local businesses after solar panels were installed."

Which sentence most directly describes a BENEFIT that businesses got?
A "Businesses could stay open for longer."
B "The extra hours gave customers more time to shop."
Option What it names Fits "benefit" topic?
A — "Businesses could stay open for longer." The capability solar panels created (the cause) Partial — it's the setup, not the benefit
B — "The extra hours gave customers more time to shop." The downstream effect — more shopping, more sales Yes — this is the actual benefit
THE TEST FOR "BENEFIT" QUESTIONS
When a question asks for a benefit, ask: "What GOOD THING actually happened because of this?"
• "Could stay open longer" = the change (neutral on its own).
• "Customers had more time to shop" = a good thing for the business.
Capability → cause. Good outcome → benefit.
One more example
Topic: "Benefits of the medical clinic getting electricity"
Cause-level: "The clinic could operate equipment at night."
Benefit-level: "More patients could be treated quickly and safely, even after dark."
Both are true, but only the second names the benefit.

Intro: Somewhere in the world, you can find a small, rural village. Despite its distance from any advanced cities and outside power sources, you can see the village light up at night with electric lights. Why, you may ask? This village has gained access to solar panels, and with it electricity. This brought many changes to the village, and every one of them was benficial.

Body 1: One of these many benefits was the increased study time for students. Before the solar panels brought electricity to the village, childrens' houses were too dark in the evening to study...

Conclusion: ...You can visit its stores and taste the fresh foods they have to offer. If your sick, you can go to their clinic and be treated efficiently and effectively.

Fix-by-fix:
benficial → beneficial
childrens' houses → children's houses
"If your sick" → "If you're sick"
Somewhere in the world, you can find... → In a small corner of the world, you can find...
WHY "CHILDREN'S" (NOT "CHILDRENS'")
"Children" is already plural — there's no extra "s" needed. To show possession, add 's:
• One child's toy → the toy of one child.
• Children's toys → the toys of the children.
The only time you'd write an apostrophe AFTER the "s" is with regular plurals like "the students' books."
"YOUR" vs. "YOU'RE"
• Your = possession. "Your clinic." "Your village."
• You're = "you are." "You're sick" = "You are sick."
Quick test: can you swap in "you are"? If yes, use you're.
SHARPER OPENERS
"Somewhere in the world" is fine, but vague openers score lower than openers that drop the reader into a specific scene. Try:
• Concrete location: "In a small rural village far from any city..."
• Sensory detail: "At night, a small rural village glows with electric light — a sight that was impossible just a few years ago."
• Direct statement of change: "Solar panels transformed life in one small village in ways no one expected."
