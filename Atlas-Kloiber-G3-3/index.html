Your #1 Error: What an Appositive Actually Is

From Q3 — you added a detail, but the prompt asked for an appositive

Atlas, on Q3 you were asked to rewrite a sentence and add an appositive. Here is what you wrote:

FROM G3.3 Q3
"Sally swam to the other seals nearby."
Scored 0.5 out of 1. The word "nearby" is a detail, not an appositive — it tells WHERE Sally swam, but it doesn't rename anything.
💡
What an appositive actually does: It renames a noun using different words. The appositive always sits right next to the noun it renames, and it's usually set off by commas. It is not "an extra detail." It is "another name for the same thing."
THE APPOSITIVE FORMULA
Noun + comma + another name for the noun + comma + rest of sentence.

Examples:
• Sally, the baby seal, swam to the other seals.
• Sally, the pup Anna rescued, swam to the other seals.
• Sally, a friendly seal, swam to the other seals.

See how each bold phrase is another way to say "Sally"? That is an appositive. "Nearby" just tells where — it does not rename Sally or the seals.
Which rewrite adds a real appositive?
A Sally swam to the other seals quickly.
B Sally, a rescued pup, swam to the other seals.
C Sally swam to the other seals in the ocean.
THE QUICK CHECK
Before you submit an appositive question, ask yourself: "Could I put my new phrase in place of the original noun and still make sense?"

• "A rescued pup swam to the other seals." → Makes sense. It's an appositive.
• "Nearby swam to the other seals." → Does NOT make sense. "Nearby" is not a name for Sally.

If you can swap it in for the noun, it's an appositive. If not, it's just a detail.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Appositives show up often on writing tests because they are how you add richness to a sentence without starting a new one. Once you know the formula — noun, another name for the noun, rest of sentence — you will catch this question every time. A full point back on Q3 is a chunk of the gap between 89% and 90%.
