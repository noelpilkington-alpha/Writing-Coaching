#1 Focus: Answer the Question the Prompt Asks

Your paragraph answered a different, easier question

Here is the actual Question 11 prompt from “Say What, Horse?”:

Explain what new research reveals about horses' ability to communicate.

The key words are “new research.” The prompt is not asking what horses are like. It is asking what the research — the scientists' study — revealed. Now look at your opening sentence:

YOUR OPENING (G5.1 Q11)
"Horse's facial expressions are kind of like humans'."
This is a clean sentence — but it answers "What are horse facial expressions like?" That is a different, easier question. The research never appears in your paragraph: no study, no experiment, no findings.

The fix: before you write a single word, find the prompt's key noun and underline it in your mind. Here, that noun is the research. Then make your first sentence answer exactly that — the easiest way is to reuse the prompt's own words: "The new research reveals that..."

The prompt asks what the new research reveals. Which opening sentence answers it?
A The new research reveals that horses can use symbols to tell people exactly what they want.
B Horses are smart animals with facial expressions kind of like humans'.
YOUR OPENING, MADE RESPONSIVE
"The new research reveals that horses can do more than make faces. They can deliberately tell humans what they want."
Same clean sentence style you already have — now aimed at the exact question asked.
THE MOVE
Step 1: Read the prompt and find its key noun (here: the research).
Step 2: Make your first sentence answer that noun directly. Borrowing the prompt's own words ("The new research reveals that...") guarantees you cannot drift off-topic.
Step 3: Every sentence after that should support what the research showed.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Your paragraph scored 14 out of 20 with clean sentences. The missing points are not grammar points — they are "did you answer the question?" points. A first sentence that answers the exact prompt is the single highest-value habit on this whole review.

#2 Focus: Study Facts Beat General Statements

The article handed you powerful evidence — and the paragraph used none of it

Asher, “Say What, Horse?” described a real experiment, full of specific facts you could have used. Your paragraph used exactly one detail from the article (the squinting and raised skin) and none of the study. Here is the evidence the article gave you:

The study inside the article
FACT 1 Scientists trained horses to use three symbols on a board: a vertical bar meant "Take my blanket off," a horizontal bar meant "Put my blanket on," and a blank board meant "No change."
FACT 2 The horses learned the symbols within about 14 days.
FACT 3 In the real test — a heavy blanket on a warm day, or no blanket on a cold day — 10 of 12 horses asked for the blanket change they actually needed.

Specific facts like these are what turn a general claim into evidence a grader can credit.

Which sentence gives the grader evidence from the article?
A Horses are smart animals that made up their own way to communicate.
B In the study, 10 of the 12 horses used a symbol to ask for the blanket change that matched the weather.
THE MOVE: COLLECT, THEN PROVE
Before you draft: type the 2–3 most specific facts you plan to use at the top of your response box — a rough fact list. Build your paragraph around them, then delete the list before you submit.

As you write: after every fact, add one sentence saying what that fact proves. Fact: 10 of 12 horses picked the symbol matching the weather. Proves: they were really communicating, not doing a memorized trick.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
A paragraph built on "horses are smart" can only ever earn partial credit, no matter how cleanly it is written — there is nothing for the grader to verify. A paragraph built on the three symbols, the 14 days, and the 10 of 12 shows you read the article and understood the research. That is where the rest of your 20 paragraph points live.

MODEL PARAGRAPH (19–20/20 level) — the moves are in bold

The new research reveals that horses can deliberately tell humans what they want. Scientists trained horses to use three symbols on a board. A vertical bar meant “Take my blanket off,” a horizontal bar meant “Put my blanket on,” and a blank board meant “No change.” The horses learned all three symbols in about 14 days. Then came the real test. When they were given a heavy blanket on a warm day or no blanket on a cold day, 10 of the 12 horses asked for the blanket change. Choosing the exact symbol that matched the weather proves the horses were truly communicating, not just performing a trick. In the end, the research shows that horses can learn a shared set of symbols and use it to say exactly what they need.
