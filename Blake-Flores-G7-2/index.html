The 6-Step Writing Process

G7 essays give you about 90 minutes — here is how to spend them

Blake, your reading is already perfect, so the multiple choice will take care of itself. The whole game on a G7 essay is planning before you write. Use this six-step process every time, and spend real time on Step 4 — that is where the thesis and explanations get built.

1
Read the passage closely
Get the main idea and the key details — this is the part you already master.
2
Answer the multiple choice
Lock in your 10/10. Re-check any answer you were unsure about.
3
Read the essay prompt carefully
Know exactly what claim the prompt is asking you to make before you plan.
4
Plan the essay — spend real time here
Type a quick outline in the response box first: a thesis (your main claim), then for each body paragraph a point + evidence + explanation of what that evidence shows. Then replace the outline with your full essay.
5
Draft the full essay
Intro with thesis, three body paragraphs, conclusion. Follow your plan.
6
Revise and strengthen
Check that every body paragraph explains its evidence and the conclusion echoes the thesis.
WHY STEP 4 IS THE WHOLE GAME FOR YOU
Your essays are losing points on depth, not structure. A one-minute plan that writes the thesis and the "what this shows" for each paragraph means you never have to invent them while drafting. Plan it once, then just expand it.

Open With a Real Thesis

Your intro announces the topic — it should state a claim

Blake, here is how your G7.2 essay opened. It tells the reader what topics are coming, but it does not yet make a claim:

YOUR INTRO (G7.2 Q11)
"Today, I will tell you about how bike lanes improved a town, the disagreements, and the solution."

This is a topic announcement. It lists what you'll discuss but never says what you actually believe about it. A thesis takes a position and previews the three points that prove it.

WHAT A THESIS DOES
A thesis states your main claim about the passage, then previews the three body points that back it up. The reader should be able to predict your whole essay from that one sentence.
A REAL THESIS FOR THIS PASSAGE
"The bike lane project made the town safer, boosted local business, and brought the community together to solve its own problems."

Notice the three previewed points — safety, business, community. Each one becomes a body paragraph. The claim ("made the town better in three real ways") is something you then prove.

Which opening is a real thesis, not just a topic announcement?
A "In this essay I am going to talk about the bike lanes, the problems people had, and how the town fixed them."
B "Adding bike lanes made the town safer, helped local shops earn more, and pulled neighbors together to fix the few problems it caused."
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
The intro is the first thing the essay is scored on. Swapping "Today I will tell you about..." for a real claim that previews your three points is the single fastest upgrade to your essay score — and it makes the whole essay easier to write, because each preview becomes a body paragraph.

Explain What Your Evidence Shows

You list strong facts — now say what they prove

Blake, your body paragraphs pull real details straight from the passage. Look at this one — the facts are great:

YOUR BODY SENTENCE (G7.2 Q11)
"Students could bike to school, shops could stay open later, and more customers stopped by on their way home."

Those are three solid pieces of evidence. But the paragraph stops there — it never says what they show. The reader has to connect the dots. Your job is to connect them for the reader.

THE MOVE: EVIDENCE + EXPLANATION
After your evidence, add a sentence that starts with "This shows..." or "This matters because..." and ties the facts back to your point. Evidence tells what happened; explanation tells why it proves your claim.

UPGRADED BODY PARAGRAPH

The bike lanes also helped local businesses grow. Students could bike to school, shops could stay open later, and more customers stopped by on their way home. This shows that the bike lanes did more than move people safely — they brought more foot traffic right past the stores, which means more sales for the town's businesses. When a project makes a town safer and richer at the same time, it is clearly worth the change.

Which body section earns the higher essay score?
A "Students could bike to school, shops stayed open later, and more customers stopped by. Which is good."
B "Students could bike to school, shops stayed open later, and more customers stopped by. This shows the bike lanes brought more people past the shops, which boosted local sales."
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
You already gather strong evidence — that is the hard half. Adding one "This shows..." sentence to each of your three body paragraphs is worth several points across the essay, because it turns a list of facts into a real argument.

Land a Stronger Conclusion

Your closing is generic — make it echo your thesis

Blake, your essay reached the conclusion with all its parts in place — here is how it ended:

YOUR CONCLUSION (G7.2 Q11)
"The story I just told you shows that with an idea and a plan some things can make another thing better."
Has a conclusion Too vague ("some things... another thing") Doesn't echo a thesis

The shape of a conclusion is there, but it is so general it could end any essay about anything. A strong conclusion does two things: it echoes the thesis and it names the three impacts you proved, so the reader leaves remembering your argument.

Tap to see a stronger conclusion
Echoes the thesis, names the impacts
STRONGER CONCLUSION

"The bike lane project proves that one good idea, planned well, can change a town for the better. It made the streets safer, gave local shops more customers, and brought neighbors together to solve the parking and traffic problems themselves. By listening to everyone and adjusting the plan, the town turned a simple change into a stronger, closer community."

THE CONCLUSION FORMULA
1. Restate your thesis in fresh words.
2. Name the three impacts you proved (safety, business, community).
3. End with one sentence on the bigger takeaway — specific to this passage, not "some things make another thing better."
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
A generic closing leaves points on the table at the exact moment the essay is scored on whether it holds together. Echoing your thesis and naming your three impacts ties the whole essay into one argument — and it costs you only two or three sentences you already planned in Step 4.
