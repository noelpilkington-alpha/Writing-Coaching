Verb Forms in Questions

Q5 — When "Did" starts the question, the verb changes

Q5 asked you to rewrite a statement as a question. You were close, but the verb form needs to change when you add "Did."

YOU WROTE
Did the dance began when shawls replaced the blankets young women once wore?

When a question starts with "Did," the helping verb "did" already carries the past tense. That means the main verb must switch back to its base form (present tense).

began (past tense) → begin (base form)
Did she ran? → Did she run?
Did he wrote? → Did he write?

FIXED
Did the dance begin when shawls replaced the blankets young women once wore?
THE RULE: "DID" + BASE FORM
When you turn a statement into a question using Did, Does, or Do, the main verb goes back to its base form.

Statement: The dance began. → Question: Did the dance begin?
Statement: She sang. → Question: Did she sing?

Quick test: If you see "Did" + a past-tense verb, something is wrong. "Did" already shows it's past tense.
More examples
Did he go home? (not "went")
Did they eat lunch? (not "ate")
Did the team win? (not "won")

Always Capitalize "I"

Q6 — A small fix worth real points

In Q6, you wrote a thoughtful sentence about learning from the Downwind family's traditions. But you wrote "i" in lowercase twice:

YOU WROTE
The Downwind family is very passionate about their tradition and i think i could learn to be more passionate about my family traditions.

The pronoun "I" is always capitalized in English, no matter where it appears in the sentence. This is a rule that applies every single time.

FIXED

EASY RULE TO REMEMBER
The word "I" is always a capital letter. It doesn't matter if it's at the beginning, middle, or end of a sentence. Before you turn in your test, scan for every "i" and ask: Does this "i" mean me? If yes, capitalize it.

Be Specific with Details

Q7 — Replace vague language with precise information

The phrase "long a go" is vague and also has a spelling error (it should be one word: long ago). Also, "a girls life" needs an apostrophe: a girl's life. When you use details from the passage, be as specific as possible.

VAGUE VS. SPECIFIC
long a go → according to an old story
saved a girls life → healed his sick daughter
When you pull information from a passage, include the who, what, and why. Don't just say "something happened long ago" — say what happened and to whom.
Why does this matter on the test?
Vague answers show you sort of understood the passage. Specific answers show you really understood it. The difference between partial and full credit often comes down to one specific detail.

YOUR PARAGRAPH — "Celebrating Native Stories"

Spelling errors that changed meaning:

riding → writing
teal → tell
were kept → were used
where → were

Your paragraph also needs more specific details from the passage and "why it matters" sentences after each detail. Here's the G4 paragraph plan:

Paragraph Plan — TS → D1 → W → D2 → W → D3 → W → CS
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail from the article  •  W = Why it matters  •  CS = Concluding Sentence
TS Native storytelling traditions teach readers about the importance of sharing and keeping culture alive.
D1 Native people wrote stories and drew pictures on animal skins to record important events.
W This shows that they found creative ways to preserve their history so future generations could learn from it.
D2 Elders told stories to children during special gatherings to pass down traditions.
W This shows that sharing stories was not just for fun — it was a way to keep the culture strong.
D3 These traditions continue today as tribes share their stories with people from many backgrounds.
W This shows that storytelling helps connect people and keeps Native culture alive for everyone.
CS Native storytelling traditions remind us that sharing culture through stories is one of the best ways to keep it alive.
TWO THINGS TO FOCUS ON
1. Spell-check key words. When a misspelling changes the meaning (like "riding" instead of "writing"), the reader can't follow your point. Before you submit, re-read each sentence slowly and check any word you're unsure about.

2. Add "why it matters" after each detail. You already have the right structure (TS at the start, CS at the end). Now fill in the middle: give a detail from the article, then explain why it supports your main idea.
