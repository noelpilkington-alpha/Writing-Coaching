The Comma Splice on Q1

The #1 move that turns 85% into 90%+

Connor, Q1 asked you to combine two sentences into one clear and complete sentence. Your combine kept both ideas and the meaning was clear — but you joined two complete thoughts with only a comma. That's called a comma splice, and it's why Q1 came in at 0.5 out of 1.

YOU WROTE (Q1)
"In this event, Science teacher Peter Tabichi received an award, it was the prestigious Global Teacher Prize."
Both halves are full sentences on their own. A comma alone isn't strong enough to hold them together.
THE TWO-THOUGHT TEST
Before you lock in a combine, cover up the comma with your finger and read each side out loud.

Left side: "Science teacher Peter Tabichi received an award." → full sentence
Right side: "It was the prestigious Global Teacher Prize." → full sentence

Two full sentences + one comma = comma splice. You need a stronger join.
THREE FIXES — PICK ONE
1. Use a relative clause (best here): "Science teacher Peter Tabichi received an award that was the prestigious Global Teacher Prize." One idea flows into the other.

2. Use a comma + connector word: "Peter Tabichi received an award, and it was the prestigious Global Teacher Prize." The connector carries the second thought.

3. Use an appositive: "Peter Tabichi received an award, the prestigious Global Teacher Prize." The second half renames the first. Clean and short.
THE CLEANEST COMBINE (YOUR IDEAS, NO SPLICE)
"In this event, Science teacher Peter Tabichi received the prestigious Global Teacher Prize."
Same meaning, same detail — but now it's one clean sentence instead of two thoughts pasted together.
Combine: "I walked into the gym. The gym was empty."
Which combine earns full credit?
A I walked into the gym, it was empty.
B I walked into the gym, which was empty.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q1 was worth 1 point and came in at 0.5. Running the two-thought test on every combine question is a single-second move that recovers that half point — and the same habit protects you on every future combine question for the rest of G4 and G5.

Clean Commas on Long Sentences (Q6)

When an opener runs long, help the reader with a comma

Connor, Q6 asked you to share a school challenge in one sentence. Your idea was clear and specific — but the sentence ran together without a comma to break it up, which made it harder to read. That's why Q6 came in at 0.67.

YOU WROTE (Q6)
"When I was at my old school and showed I mastered all the content_ I couldn't join the higher class until next year."
The opener — “When I was at my old school and showed I mastered all the content” — is long. A comma at the break tells the reader: okay, now the main part is coming.
THE RULE
When a sentence starts with a long "when / if / because / after" opener, put a comma right before the main part of the sentence. The comma is the reader's signal that the setup is done and the main thought is starting.

Setup — Main. That's the shape.
YOUR IDEA, CLEANED UP
"When I was at my old school and showed I mastered all the content, I couldn't join the higher class until next year."
Same idea, same words. One comma that tells the reader where the setup ends.
Which sentence has the comma in the right place?
A After the bell rang, I ran to my next class.
B After the bell rang_ I ran to my next class.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q6 was worth 1 point and came in at 0.67. One comma recovers the third of a point you left on the table — and the same rule shows up on nearly every future "start with a subordinating clause" question.

Your G4.1 Paragraph — Good Start, Needs More

15/20 — the idea is there, the explanation isn't yet

Connor, your paragraph about Cody's opinion change in Surprise Kick has the right idea — you understood that Cody changed his mind about Amanda and Mud. That's the core of the prompt, and that's what got you to 15/20.

The points that stayed on the table came from two spots: the paragraph stopped too early, and the middle needed more specific evidence from the story to explain the change. Let's look.

"Cody had always believed that Amanda and Mud had never been much help until this moment. Ryan passed to Amanda and she showed great skill and then passing to Mud. Mud ran fast and scored a goal. Cody was stunned and knew they were far above of his level."
Topic: opinion change set up Before: Cody underestimated them Middle: needs more evidence No closing / reflection sentence "far above of his level" — grammar

The Q11 rubric rewards paragraphs that open, explain, support with evidence, and close. You have the open and the before. What's missing is the "because" step — one or two sentences that spell out why seeing Amanda and Mud play changed Cody's mind — and a closing sentence that names the new opinion.

YOUR PARAGRAPH — BUILT OUT TO 19–20/20

In Surprise Kick, Cody's opinion of Amanda and Mud changes when he actually sees them play. At the start, Cody had always believed Amanda and Mud had never been much help on the field. Then, during the game, Ryan passed to Amanda and she showed great skill before passing to Mud, who ran fast and scored a goal. This mattered because Cody had been judging them before he ever watched them compete, and the goal proved they could deliver when the team needed it. By the end, Cody was stunned and realized Amanda and Mud were playing far above the level he had assumed — and his opinion of them shifted from doubt to respect.

Notice: every single idea is yours. Same characters, same play, same turning point. What changed: the paragraph now has four clear moves — opening, before, evidence + why it mattered, closing with the new opinion. That structure is worth 4–5 points on Q11.

The 4-Move Paragraph Frame (Memorize This)
OPEN One sentence that names the question — "Cody's opinion changes when…"
BEFORE One sentence about what the character thought at the start
EVIDENCE Two sentences: what happened in the story + why it mattered
CLOSE One sentence that names the new opinion or the lesson
ONE GRAMMAR FIX
You wrote: "far above of his level." Drop the "of" — "far above his level" is the clean version. "Above" already does the work of showing position, so you don't need "of" after it.
