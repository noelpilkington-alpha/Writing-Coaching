Focus #1: What a True Appositive Is

You can spot one (Q5) — now let's build one

Ryke, on Q5 you correctly identified an appositive — nice work. On Q4 the task was to combine two ideas using an appositive, and this is where the points slipped:

YOU WROTE (G3.5 Q4)
“The books are still popular, which is good, they are known for their golden spines.”

Two things happened here. “which is good” is a relative clause, not an appositive, and then “they are known for their golden spines” is joined with only a comma — that is a comma splice. An appositive instead renames or describes the noun with a phrase set off by commas.

Which version combines the ideas with a true appositive?
A The books are still popular, which is good, they are known for their golden spines.
B The books, known for their golden spines, are still popular.
YOUR Q4, REWRITTEN AS A TRUE APPOSITIVE
“The books, known for their golden spines, are still popular.”
THE RULE
An appositive drops a describing phrase right next to the noun and sets it off with commas: “The books, known for their golden spines, are still popular.”

Watch out for two look-alikes that are not appositives:
✗ A “which…” or “that…” relative clause.
✗ A second full sentence added with just a comma (a comma splice).
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
You already recognize appositives when you read them (Q5). Building one yourself is the same skill in reverse: name the noun, then tuck the describing phrase between two commas. That one move earns back the Q4 points.

Focus #2: Clean Sentence Boundaries

Give every idea its own clean space

Ryke, your ideas are good — they just need clean edges. Two spots on G3.5 show it: a couple of “And” sentences on Q11 that cannot stand on their own, and a run-on on Q9 where several facts got fused together.

FROM G3.5 Q11 (the paragraph)
“Lots of divers visit the coral reefs. And accidently hurt them. And if swimmers swim near the statues, they will not hurt the coral reefs.”
“And accidently hurt them.” has no subject — it leans on the sentence before it.
FROM G3.5 Q9
“One reason is: that they did not cost much money they were 25 cents, most other books were $1.50.”
Several facts fused together with a colon and commas — a run-on.
Which version gives each idea a clean boundary?
A Lots of divers visit the coral reefs. And accidentally hurt them.
B Lots of divers visit the coral reefs and accidentally hurt them.
YOUR Q11, WITH CLEAN BOUNDARIES
“Lots of divers visit the coral reefs and accidentally hurt them. If swimmers swim near the statues instead, they will not hurt the coral reefs.”
YOUR Q9, CLEANED UP
“One reason is that they did not cost much money. They were 25 cents, but most other books were $1.50.”
THE RULE
Give every complete idea a clean boundary. This is not about chopping — it is about giving each idea its own clear space.

1. If a sentence cannot stand on its own (like “And accidentally hurt them.”), join it to the idea beside it.
2. If two complete ideas are mashed together, put a period between them or add a joining word: and, but, so, because.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Your content on both answers was right — the reader just could not tell where one idea ended and the next began. Smoothing those boundaries wins back the Q9 and Q11 points without changing a single fact.
