Proofread Before You Submit

Three tiny slips — one read-back catches all of them

Nicholas, your essay was clean and well-built. The clearest two points on the table are three small editing slips that a single read-back would have caught. None of them is a thinking error — they are exactly the kind of thing your eyes skip past while drafting.

FROM YOUR ESSAY (G7.1 Q11)
"...these three factors provide a significant benifit to the community because of solar panels."
"Solar panel installation allow for the businesses to stay open longer."
"...children could now study in the evenings wtihout straining their eyes..."
THE THREE FIXES
1. Spelling: benifit → benefit
2. Subject-verb agreement: the subject is "installation" (singular), so allow → allows
3. Spelling: wtihout → without

The fix is a habit, not a skill you need to build: before you submit, read the essay back one time, slowly, sentence by sentence. Reading slowly forces your eye to land on each word instead of gliding over it — that is how "benifit" and "wtihout" jump out, and how you hear that "installation allows" sounds right while "installation allow" does not.

Which sentence is correct after a careful read-back?
A "Solar panel installation allow the businesses to stay open longer."
B "Solar panel installation allows the businesses to stay open longer."
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Editing is graded directly, and these three fixes are the cheapest points on the whole test — no new writing required, just one slow read-back before you submit. At 18/20, this is likely the single move that closes the gap.

Matching a Topic to Its Benefit

The one multiple-choice question — a fine-tuning, not a weakness

Nicholas, you got 9 of 10 multiple choice correct — real mastery. The single one (Question 7) is worth a closer look because the distinction is subtle and high-level. Here is the question:

THE QUESTION (G7.1 Q7)
Which sentence best matches the topic: "Benefits for local businesses after solar panels were installed"?
YOU CHOSE
"Businesses could stay open for longer."
THE BEST MATCH
"The extra hours gave customers more time to shop."

Here is the fine distinction. The topic is the benefit for local businesses — the good result. "Businesses could stay open for longer" names the change, what mechanically happened. "The extra hours gave customers more time to shop" names the actual benefit that follows from it — the effect that genuinely helps the business.

THE RULE
When a topic is about a benefit or result, pick the sentence that states the effect or outcome — not the one that only restates the cause or the change. Ask: "Is this the thing that happened, or the good that came from it?" The topic wants the good that came from it.
Topic: "Benefits to students after the new library opened." Which sentence matches?
A "The library stayed open later into the evening."
B "Students had more quiet time to finish their homework after school."
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
This is the most advanced reading skill on the test — telling cause from effect inside a topic. Getting it consistently is what separates 9 of 10 from a clean 10 of 10, and the same instinct sharpens your essay topic sentences too.

Watch for Fragments in Your Conclusion

One sentence in your closing is missing its main clause

Nicholas, your conclusion does the right things — it wraps up your three points and ends with energy. One sentence in it, though, is a fragment: it looks like a sentence but cannot stand on its own. Here is your closing as you wrote it:

YOUR CONCLUSION (G7.1 Q11)
"In conclusion, solar panels provide a great boost to any community. Whether that is through increasing business productivity or providing medical service into the night. Even for simple benefits like late night conversations and study, there is a benefit for everyone."

The highlighted part starts with "Whether..." — a subordinate clause. It tells a condition but has no main clause to attach to, so by itself it is not a complete sentence. A complete sentence needs a subject doing something on its own: this one is left hanging.

THE FIX: ATTACH IT TO THE SENTENCE BEFORE
Join the fragment to the previous sentence so the "Whether..." clause has a main clause to lean on:
"In conclusion, solar panels provide a great boost to any community, whether that is through increasing business productivity or providing medical service into the night."
Which option is a complete sentence, not a fragment?
A "Whether that is through increasing business productivity or providing medical service into the night."
B "Solar panels provide a great boost to any community, whether that is through business productivity or medical service into the night."
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Fragments are easy to miss because they sound finished when you read them in your head. The same slow read-back from Card 3 catches them: if a sentence starts with "whether", "because", "which", or "although" and has no main clause, join it to the sentence beside it.
