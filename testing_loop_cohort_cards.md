### Card 5: Transition Words vs Joining Words
*The one rule that fixes all 5 of your Q1 errors*

Rhys, here is the rule that would have saved you points on **every single one of your tests**. It comes down to knowing which words can hold two sentences together with just a comma — and which words can't.

**The Two Types of Linking Words**

| | |
|---|---|
| **JOIN** | **Joining words (FANBOYS):** **for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so**. These CAN hold two complete sentences together with a comma in front. *Rain was falling**,** and the match was cancelled.* ✓ |
| **TRANSITION** | **Transition words (conjunctive adverbs):** **consequently, also, for example, however, moreover, therefore, furthermore, in fact, meanwhile**. These **CANNOT** hold two sentences together with just a comma. They need a **period and a new sentence**. |

> **THE 2 WAYS TO FIX A COMMA SPLICE WITH A TRANSITION WORD**
>
> Take your G4.4 Q1 example: *"A study in Seattle showed that calm music helps people relax, also music can even help people feel less stressed."*
>
> **Fix 1 — New sentence:** "...helps people relax**.** Also, music can even help..."
>
> **Fix 2 — Swap in a joining word:** "...helps people relax**, and** it can even help..."
>
> Both work. Pick whichever feels smoothest.

**Discrimination challenge:** Which sentence is correctly punctuated?

- **A** (✗) I wanted to go to the park, however it started raining hard.
  - *Feedback:* Not quite. “However” is a transition word (a conjunctive adverb). A comma in front of it isn’t strong enough to hold two full sentences together. Use a period and start a new sentence.
- **B** (✓) I wanted to go to the park**.** However, it started raining hard.
  - *Feedback:* Correct! Ending the first idea with a period and starting “However” as a new sentence is the cleanest fix. A comma alone can’t hold two sentences together with a transition word like “however.”

### Card 5: What an Appositive IS (and What It Isn't)
*You reach for relative clauses. Here's how to spot the difference.*

Aksel, on Q3–Q4 when the prompt asks you to **use an appositive**, you often use a **relative clause** or a **compound sentence** instead. Both sound good, both are grammatically correct — but they are not appositives. The grading feedback docks partial credit because the task wasn't met.

**What Is an Appositive?**

| | |
|---|---|
| **DEF** | An appositive is a **noun phrase** that **renames another noun** right next to it, usually **set off by commas**. *My dog**, a golden retriever,** loves the beach.* "A golden retriever" renames "my dog." That's the whole move. |
| **NOT** | **Not an appositive:** a "which..." or "who..." clause. That's a relative clause. *My dog**, who loves the beach,** is a golden retriever.* ← relative clause, NOT appositive. |
| **NOT** | **Not an appositive:** two sentences joined with "and." That's a compound sentence. *My dog is a golden retriever**, and** she loves the beach.* ← compound, NOT appositive. |

*You wrote — YOUR G4.2 Q4 (Mar 3):*
> "…bluebonnet festivals, which have fried food..." ("Which have fried food" is a relative clause, not an appositive. An appositive would be: "bluebonnet festivals, **spring celebrations filled with fried food and music**," — a noun phrase that renames the festival.)

*You wrote — YOUR G4.3 Q3 (Mar 4):*
> "He asked his mom for some lettuce, and she was shocked because he never eats vegetables." (This is a compound sentence with "and." An appositive version would rename one of the nouns: "He asked his mom, **a gardener who grew her own lettuce**, for some.")

*You wrote — YOUR G4.4 Q3 (Mar 5):*
> "Music can help you learn by keeping your attention." (No appositive at all here. An appositive version: "Music, **a natural focus booster**, can help you learn by keeping your attention.")

**Discrimination challenge:** The prompt says: "Combine these sentences using an appositive: The elephant is the largest land animal. The elephant lives in Africa." Which answer is an appositive?

- **A** (✗) The elephant, which is the largest land animal, lives in Africa.
  - *Feedback:* Not quite. “Which is the largest land animal” is a relative clause — it starts with “which” and has its own verb (“is”). An appositive is a noun phrase with no verb of its own: just “the largest land animal,” no “which is.”
- **B** (✓) The elephant**, the largest land animal,** lives in Africa.
  - *Feedback:* Correct! “The largest land animal” is a noun phrase that renames “the elephant.” No “which,” no verb of its own — just a renaming. That’s an appositive.

**What Is an Appositive?**

| | |
|---|---|
| **DEF** | An appositive is a **noun phrase** that **renames another noun** right next to it, usually **set off by commas**. *My dog**, Lucy,** loves the beach.* → "Lucy" renames "my dog" — that's an appositive. |
| **NOT** | **Not an appositive:** adding "and" to join another sentence. That's a compound sentence. |
| **NOT** | **Not an appositive:** adding an adverb like "excitedly" or "quickly." Those describe actions, not nouns. |
| **NOT** | **Not an appositive:** adding adjectives like "bright" or "tall" in front of a noun. Those are just describing words — not a renaming phrase. |

**Discrimination challenge:** The prompt says: "Expand this sentence using an appositive: My brother rides a bike." Which answer is an appositive?

- **B** (✓) My brother**, a fifth grader who loves races,** rides a bike.
  - *Feedback:* Correct! “A fifth grader who loves races” is a noun phrase that renames “my brother.” It’s set off with commas. That’s an appositive.

> **"Rewrite with a new subject"** → Write the full new sentence.

> **THE APPOSITIVE RULE**
>
> An appositive is:
>
> • A **noun phrase** (not a full sentence, not a conjunction)
>
> • It **renames** the noun right before it
>
> • It is **set off by commas** (both sides, like parentheses)
>
> • The sentence should still make sense if you take it out

### Card 4: Appositives — The WHOLE Noun Phrase

> 🔍 *Pattern note:* ⚑ **The appositive rule:** An appositive is a noun phrase that renames the noun right before it, set off by commas. When the phrase has extra describing words, **the appositive is the ENTIRE phrase — not just the head word**.

"Local experts" is a phrase

the appositive, not the whole thing. The appositive is everything between the two commas — the full phrase that renames "workshops." What ARE the workshops?

That is the appositive.

From G8.2 Q4

Identify the appositive in the sentence about market booths at the plaza.

Same issue. You grabbed the first chunk of the noun phrase. The full noun phrase between the commas, including the describing words ("selling postcards and sketches of the towers"), is the appositive.

From G8.3 Q4

Which sentence contains an appositive?

The giveaway is

that renames the subject. A descriptive clause with "that" or "which" is NOT an appositive — appositives do not use those words.

> **3-STEP APPOSITIVE TEST**
>
> **1. Find the two commas.** The appositive sits BETWEEN them.
>
> **2. Select the entire phrase between those commas.** Not just the first word. All of it.
>
> **3. Check: does it rename the noun right before the first comma?** If yes, that is your appositive. If it starts with "that" or "which," it is a clause, not an appositive.

- **C** (✓) a week-long celebration of local music and food trucks
  - *Feedback:* Correct! The full noun phrase between the two commas, renaming "The festival." That is the appositive.
