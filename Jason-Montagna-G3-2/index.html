Question 10 Fix — Use Only the Words the Prompt Gives You

From G3.2 Q10 — Using because, but, or so

You scored 1/3 on this question.

The question gave you two sentences and asked you to combine them using because, but, or so:

"The ice was covered in teddy bears. The players skated among the bears."
(Use because, but, or so to combine these two sentences into one.)
The prompt says to use because, but, or so. Which answer follows the prompt?
A The ice was covered in teddy bears, and the players skated among the bears.
B The ice was covered in teddy bears, so the players skated among the bears.
YOU WROTE (From G3.2 Q10)
"The ice was covered in teddy bears, and the players skated among the bears."

Jason, you combined the two sentences correctly — they flow together, and the meaning is clear. The problem is just one word: you used "and" instead of one of the three words the prompt asked for. The prompt was very specific: because, but, or so.

CORRECT ANSWER
"The ice was covered in teddy bears, so the players skated among the bears."
THE BIG LESSON
When a prompt gives you a list of words you can use, pick from that list ONLY.

Your brain might jump to the first connecting word you think of (like "and") — but if that word is not on the list, it is wrong.

Read the prompt, underline the allowed words, then pick one of those.
WHICH ONE FITS? because / but / so
• because → gives a REASON. "The players skated among the bears because the ice was covered in them."
• but → shows a CONTRAST. (Does not fit here — there is no contrast.)
• so → shows a RESULT. "The ice was covered in teddy bears, so the players skated among the bears."

Two out of three choices worked here (because and so). Either would have earned full credit.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
This one slip cost you 2 full points on a question you really did know how to answer. Learning to underline the allowed words before you write is a 10-second habit that protects real points. Do this every time you see a list in a prompt.

Question 1 Fix — Concise Means Shorter, Not Longer

From G3.2 Q1 — Rewriting to be clear and concise

You scored 1/2 on this question.

The question said:

"Rewrite this sentence to make it clear and concise:"
"That day, the Hershey Bears broke their own record by collecting 45,650 stuffed animals that day."

The original has "that day" written twice — that is the problem. The task was to remove the repeated words.

Which version is more concise (shorter and cleaner)?
A That day, the Hershey Bears broke their own record by collecting 45,650 stuffed animals.
B The Hershey Bears host a Teddy Bear Toss once every year, One year at the Teddy Bear Toss, the bears scored a few minutes into the game and the fans broke the teddy bear collecting record with collecting 45,650 stuffed animals.
YOU WROTE (From G3.2 Q1)
"The Hershey Bears host a Teddy Bear Toss once every year, One year at the Teddy Bear Toss, the bears scored a few minutes into the game and the fans broke the teddy bear collecting record with collecting 45,650 stuffed animals."

Jason, your answer actually went the opposite direction — instead of making the sentence shorter, you made it much longer by adding new information about the Teddy Bear Toss. You also joined two sentences with just a comma (a comma splice) — "year, One year" needed a period, not a comma.

CONCISE ANSWER
"That day, the Hershey Bears broke their own record by collecting 45,650 stuffed animals."

All you had to do was delete the second "that day" at the end. That is it!

WHAT DOES "CONCISE" MEAN?
Concise = SHORTER. Fewer words. Same meaning.

When you are asked to make something concise, look for:
• Repeated words (like "that day... that day") — cut one
• Extra filler words (like "very really super") — cut them
• Saying the same thing twice — pick one version, delete the other

You should never add new information to a concise rewrite.
QUICK CHECK
After you rewrite, count the words. If your version has more words than the original, stop and try again — concise means fewer, not more.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
This question cost you 1 point. Knowing exactly what "concise" means unlocks an easy category of questions. If you see the word "concise" on a future test, your first move is to look for repeated or extra words — then cut them.

Question 3 Fix — Making Real Questions

From G3.2 Q3 — Rewriting a statement as a question

You scored 1/2 on this question.

The question said:

"Rewrite this as a question:"
"This fun event sells out every year."
Which rewrite is an actual question?
A The Teddy Bear Toss event sells out every year.
B Does this fun event sell out every year?
YOU WROTE (From G3.2 Q3)
"The Teddy Bear Toss event sells out every year."

Jason, you changed "This" to "The Teddy Bear Toss event" — but the sentence is still a statement, not a question. It ends in a period, and it is telling the reader something. A question has to ask something.

CORRECT ANSWER
"Does this fun event sell out every year?"
HOW TO TURN A STATEMENT INTO A QUESTION
Every question needs two things:
• A question word at the start
• A question mark at the end (?)

Common question starters:
• Does / Do — "Does this event sell out?"
• Is / Are — "Is this event popular?"
• Was / Were — "Was the event sold out?"
• Can / Could — "Can you get tickets?"
• Did — "Did the event sell out?"
• Who / What / When / Where / Why / How
THE 2-STEP TRICK
Step 1: Add a question word to the start (Does, Is, Did, etc.).
Step 2: Change the period at the end to a question mark (?).

Example:
Statement: "This event sells out."
→ Add "Does" and flip "sells" to "sell": "Does this event sell out?"
READ-IT-ALOUD TEST
Read your sentence out loud. Does your voice go UP at the end?
• If YES → it is a question. Good!
• If NO → it is still a statement. Add a question word and a question mark.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
This cost you 1 point. Questions are one of the four main sentence types — if you get the difference locked in now, you will get every "change this to a question" item right from here on.

Question 11 — Paragraph Development

One Book, One Camel — 13/20 points
Which paragraph better explains how the Camel Mobile Library affected the people of Garissa?
A The Camel Mobile Library has affected the people of Garissa who are constantly moving. The Camels provide books to children and adults so they can enjoy them.
B The Camel Mobile Library has affected the people of Garissa in big ways by bringing books to families who are constantly moving. Since 1996, camels have carried boxes of books across the desert so that nomadic children can learn to read even when there is no school building nearby. Both kids and adults borrow books from the camels, and the program helps people who would otherwise have no way to get books. Because of the Camel Mobile Library, reading is possible for families far from any town.
Clear Central Idea Only 2 Sentences Missing Specific Details No Closing

Jason, you got the central idea right: the Camel Mobile Library affects people who are constantly moving, and it brings books to children and adults. That is a solid start — you understood the main point of the article.

What held you back was length and detail. A paragraph needs at least 4 sentences: a topic sentence, 2-3 details with evidence from the article, and a closing. Your answer had just 2.

YOU WROTE (From G3.2 Q11)
"The Camel Mobile Library has affected the people of Garissa who are constantly moving. The Camels provide books to children and adults so they can enjoy them."
WHAT YOU DID WELL
• Clear topic sentence that matches the prompt
• You understood WHO is affected (nomadic families) and HOW (they get books)
• Your writing is grammatically clean
WHAT WAS MISSING
Specific details from the article would have made your paragraph much stronger:
• The Camel Mobile Library started in 1996
• Camels carry boxes of books across the desert
• It serves nomadic families who move often
• It reaches areas with no school buildings or regular libraries
• Both children and adults borrow the books

Evidence from the article makes your paragraph more convincing and shows graders that you read carefully.
The 4-Part Paragraph
TOPIC State your central idea (you did this!)
DETAIL 1 Give a specific detail from the article that supports your idea
DETAIL 2 Give another specific detail from the article
CLOSING Wrap up by connecting back to your central idea
