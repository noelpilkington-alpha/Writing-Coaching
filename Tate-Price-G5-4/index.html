Question 8 Fix — Connecting to the Bigger Picture

Writing a sentence about Colvin's court case and the Civil Rights Movement
📈 Writing sentences from a prompt is your biggest growth area across all 4 tests (83% average). The pattern: your ideas are on the right track, but the sentence doesn't fully develop or explain the connection. This skill is exactly what makes essay body paragraphs strong — each sentence needs to carry a complete thought.

You were asked: "Write one sentence that explains how Colvin's court case connected to the larger Civil Rights Movement."

Which sentence fully explains the connection between Colvin's case and the Civil Rights Movement?
A Colvin's court case connected to the larger Civil Rights Movement by proving how bias judges are.
B Colvin's court case connected to the larger Civil Rights Movement because it became one of four cases used to challenge bus segregation laws in court, helping lead to real change.
YOUR ANSWER
"Colvin's court case connected to the larger Civil Rights Movement by proving how bias judges are."

Two issues here:

  • Grammar: "bias" is a noun — you needed the adjective form: "biased"
  • Incomplete idea: Even with correct grammar, "proving how biased judges are" doesn't explain the actual connection. How did the case contribute to the movement?
Tap to see a stronger version
Think about what the case actually led to
STRONGER VERSION
"Colvin's court case connected to the larger Civil Rights Movement because it became one of four cases used to challenge bus segregation laws, helping prove that those laws were unconstitutional."
SO WHAT? — WHY THIS MATTERS FOR YOUR WRITING
When a prompt asks you to "explain how" something connects, you need to name the specific link. "Proving judges are biased" is a vague claim. Ask yourself: "What did this actually lead to? What happened because of it?" That answer IS your connection. In your essays, this is the difference between a sentence that earns full credit and one that falls short.
See the "bias" vs. "biased" grammar rule
Bias = a noun (a thing): "The judge showed bias."
Biased = an adjective (describes someone): "The judge was biased."

Quick test: if you can replace the word with "unfair," use biased. "How biased judges are" = "how unfair judges are."

Question 9 Fix — How the Community Treated Colvin

Your idea was right — the grammar tripped you up

You were asked: "Write one sentence that describes how Claudette Colvin's community treated her after her arrest."

Which sentence correctly describes how Colvin was treated?
A After Claudette Colvin's arrest she was treated like a criminal because she standed up for what she believed in.
B After Claudette Colvin's arrest, her community shunned her and treated her like a troublemaker, even though she had stood up for her rights.
YOUR ANSWER
"After Claudette Colvin's arrest she was treated like a criminal because she standed up for what she believed in."

Your thinking was solid — Colvin WAS treated poorly. Two things to fix:

standed → stood

"Stand" is an irregular verb — its past tense is "stood," not "standed." Irregular verbs don't follow the normal "-ed" rule.

SO WHAT? — WHY THIS MATTERS FOR YOUR WRITING
Irregular verb errors can cost you points even when your idea is correct. A few common ones to memorize:
• stand → stood (not "standed")
• run → ran (not "runned")
• speak → spoke (not "speaked")
• write → wrote (not "writed")

Also: when you start a sentence with a phrase like "After Claudette Colvin's arrest," always add a comma before the main part of the sentence.

Question 10 Fix — What Colvin's Story Teaches About History

Good sentiment, but the sentence needs to go deeper

You were asked: "Write one sentence that explains how Colvin's story reminds us about history."

Which sentence gives a specific, fully developed answer about what Colvin's story teaches us?
A Colvin's story reminds us that sometimes the unspoken heros are the bravest.
B Colvin's story reminds us that history is shaped by ordinary people whose contributions are often overlooked, and that learning about them helps us understand the full picture of the Civil Rights Movement.
YOUR ANSWER
"Colvin's story reminds us that sometimes the unspoken heros are the bravest."

Two things to fix:

  • Spelling: "heros" → "heroes" (most words ending in "-o" add "-es" for the plural)
  • Incomplete idea: "Unspoken heroes are the bravest" is a nice thought, but the prompt asked about history. What does her story teach us about how we remember history? You need to make that connection specific.
Tap to see a stronger version
Think: what does her story teach about how history works?
STRONGER VERSION
"Colvin's story reminds us that history is often shaped by ordinary people whose bravery goes unrecognized, and that learning their stories gives us a more complete understanding of movements like the fight for civil rights."
SO WHAT? — ANSWERING THE ACTUAL QUESTION
When a prompt says "explains how X reminds us about history," your answer needs to be about history, not just about bravery. A good strategy: after you write your sentence, re-read the prompt and check — did I actually answer what they asked? If the prompt mentions "history," your sentence should too.
The "re-read the prompt" trick
Before submitting any sentence-from-a-prompt answer, do this 5-second check:
1. Re-read the prompt's key words (in this case: "reminds us about history")
2. Check if your answer uses or directly addresses those key words
3. If not, revise to include them

This one habit can recover 2–3 points per test.

Question 11 — Your Essay Paragraph

You scored 17/20 — strong, but a few points left on the table
📈 This is part of a pattern across your last 4 tests. The structure of your writing is the consistent difference-maker. You scored 18/20 on G5.1 and G5.3 — when your paragraph has strong structure, you earn top marks.

Your essay about high-speed trains earned 17 out of 20 — that's solid. But you left 3 points on the table. Here's what could push you to 19 or 20:

Which paragraph opening is more effective for this essay prompt?
A For three main reasons all U.S. cities should have high-speed trains. My first reason is that high-speed trains make about five times less carbon dioxide than cars.
B The author organizes the argument for high-speed trains by presenting three key benefits: they are better for the environment, safer than cars, and faster for long-distance travel.

Here's what your essay did well and what could improve:

Your essay: "For three main reasons all U.S. cities should have high-speed trains. My first reason is that high-speed trains make about five times less carbon dioxide than cars resulting in cleaner air. My second reason is that high-speed trains are less likely to get in an accident meaning less injuries on the road. Finally, I would like to point out how much time it takes to travel by car because of traffic, crashes, and lights. In conclusion, high-speed trains are cleaner, faster, and more efficient making them the best way to travel from city to city."

What you did well:

  • Three clear reasons with evidence (environment, safety, speed)
  • A conclusion that ties the reasons together
  • Good use of transition words ("Finally," "In conclusion")

What to improve for full marks:

  • Answer the prompt directly: The question asked how the author organizes and supports the argument. Your paragraph states your own opinion ("all U.S. cities should have...") instead of analyzing the author's structure.
  • Use "the author" language: "The author argues..." / "The author supports this by..." / "The author uses evidence such as..."
  • Add "explain why" after each piece of evidence: You listed the reasons but didn't explain WHY each one matters. After each fact, ask yourself: "So what? Why does this matter?"
Tap to see a stronger version of the opening
Notice how it answers the prompt directly
STRONGER OPENING + FIRST REASON
"The author organizes the argument for high-speed trains by presenting three key benefits: they are better for the environment, safer than cars, and faster for long-distance travel. First, the author explains that high-speed trains produce about five times less carbon dioxide than cars. This matters because reducing pollution is important for keeping the air clean and fighting climate change..."
SO WHAT? — THE "AUTHOR LANGUAGE" RULE
When a prompt asks "How does the author organize/support the argument," you must write about the author's choices, not your own opinion. Start with: "The author argues/organizes/supports..." Then after each piece of evidence, add your "So what?" explanation.
Quick formula for "how does the author" prompts
Opening sentence: "The author organizes the argument by [naming the strategy]."
Body: "First, the author explains that [evidence]. This is important because [explain why]."
Repeat for each reason/piece of evidence.
Closing sentence: "By presenting these reasons, the author makes a convincing case that [main idea]."
