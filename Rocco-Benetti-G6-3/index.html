Q4: Appositives Don't Need Extra Pronouns

MCQ — Combining sentences with appositives

You chose this version:

YOUR ANSWER
"Carter, a boy who helped butterflies, he created a way station."

The problem is the extra "he" after the appositive. An appositive is a phrase set off by commas that renames the noun. When you close the commas, the original subject is still doing the job:

CORRECT VERSION
"Carter, a boy who helped butterflies, created a way station."

Here's the pattern:

Structure Example
Subject + appositive + verb Carter, a boy who helped butterflies, created a way station.
Subject + appositive + pronoun + verb Carter, a boy who helped butterflies, he created a way station.
QUICK TEST
Remove the appositive (the part between commas). Does the sentence still work?
• "Carter created a way station." → Yes.
• "Carter he created a way station." → No — the "he" is a leftover. Remove it.

Q9: Transition Words — However vs. Unless

MCQ — Connecting contrasting ideas

The two ideas being connected:

Sentence 1
Fewer butterflies are surviving their trips.
Sentence 2
People are taking action to protect them.

You chose "Unless" — but these aren't conditional. The second sentence doesn't depend on the first. Both things are happening at the same time. The correct word is "However" — it shows contrast between a bad situation and a positive response.

Transition What it means Fits here?
However Shows contrast: "This is true, but on the other hand..." Yes — bad news contrasted with good news
Unless Shows a condition: "This will happen if not..." No — no condition is being set
Therefore Shows cause-and-effect: "Because of this..." No — the action isn't caused by fewer surviving
TIP
When two sentences show opposite situations (bad news then good news, or a problem then a solution), the connecting word is almost always "However" or "But." "Unless" sets up a condition — it means "if not," which changes the meaning entirely.

Write an Introduction First

Your essay jumped straight into Carter's story

Your essay's first paragraph was about Carter — his birthday, his way station, planting milkweed. That's a body paragraph, not an introduction. An introduction needs two things:

  • A hook — something interesting to pull the reader in
  • A thesis — one sentence that previews your main points

Here's what your essay looks like now vs. what it should look like:

YOUR P1 (NO INTRO)
Starts immediately with Carter's birthday, way station, milkweed planting. No hook, no thesis, no preview of what the essay will explain.
WHAT AN INTRO SHOULD LOOK LIKE
[Hook] Every year, millions of monarch butterflies make an incredible journey from Canada to Mexico — but that journey is becoming more dangerous.
[Thesis] People are helping monarch butterflies survive by creating way stations, studying their migration as citizen scientists, and spreading awareness about the threats they face.

Notice how the thesis previews three topics — one for each body paragraph. This gives your essay a roadmap so the reader (and you) know exactly where it's going.

G6 ESSAY PLAN
INTRO (hook + thesis) → BP1 (TS + E1 + X1 + E2 + X2 + CS) → BP2 → BP3 → CONC

Always start with the introduction. Don't jump into your first example — set up the essay first.

Reorganize Your Essay

Your structure is backwards — solutions before problems

Here's the order your essay actually follows:

P1: Carter (solution) P2: Migration (background) P3: Threats (problems) P4: Citizen scientists (solution) P5: Carter again (conclusion)

The prompt asks "How people are helping monarch butterflies survive." P2 (migration background) and P3 (threats) don't directly answer that question — they explain the problem, not the solution. And you start with a solution (Carter) before explaining why monarchs need help.

Here's a better structure using the same information you already had:

Reorganized Essay Plan
INTRO Hook about monarchs' journey → Thesis: People help monarchs by creating way stations, volunteering as citizen scientists, and protecting milkweed habitat.
BP1 Way stations — E1: Carter's way station with milkweed → X1: explains why milkweed matters (monarchs need it to rest/breed) → E2: other people planting milkweed → X2: how this creates a network of rest stops
BP2 Citizen scientists — E1: tagging butterflies to track migration → X1: explains how data helps researchers → E2: counting caterpillars, posting sightings → X2: shows how ordinary people contribute
BP3 Raising awareness of threats — E1: farms/cities destroy milkweed → X1: explains why this is the biggest danger → E2: storms destroying forests in Mexico → X2: people spreading the word leads to conservation efforts
CONC Restate 3 points → Carter's quote about small actions → broader message
KEY DIFFERENCE
Every body paragraph now answers the prompt: "How are people helping?" Background and threats get woven into body paragraphs as context — they don't get their own standalone paragraphs. This keeps every paragraph focused on the question.
