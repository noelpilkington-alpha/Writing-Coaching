The #1 Fix: Answer the Prompt with Facts from the Passage

Question 11 — where 12 points are waiting

Cooper, the prompt asked one exact question: “Why can learning to cook help children?” and it told you to use facts from the passage. Here is how your paragraph started:

YOU WROTE (Q11, the opening)
“Hi, my name is Cooper Baldwin and I’m here to talk about baking. Baking is important to everyone.”

That is a warm, confident opening — but it is a spoken speech about baking in general, not an answer to the question. It never says why cooking helps children, and it does not use a single fact from the passage. The grader can only give points for answering the actual prompt, so a speech, no matter how friendly, cannot score.

A TOPIC SENTENCE THAT ANSWERS THE PROMPT
Learning to cook helps children because it teaches them to follow steps in order and to measure ingredients carefully.

See the difference? The strong version states your answer in the first sentence, and it points straight at facts from the passage. No “Hi, my name is…” — just your central idea, right away.

🔍 The habit: read the prompt twice, underline the exact question in your mind, and make your first sentence answer THAT. Then reach into the passage for facts to back it up — not your own opinion about what you like.

One Clear Idea per Sentence

Give each idea a clean boundary

Cooper, several of your short answers had good ideas inside them, but the ideas ran together into one long sentence with no stopping points. When two or three complete ideas fuse, the grader can lose track of each one. Here is Question 7:

YOU WROTE (Q7)
“These are very helpful because they are what starts the pumpkin to grow by grabbing pollen from one flower to another tiny pumpkins begin to grow.”

That is really two ideas fused into one (bees carry pollen, AND tiny pumpkins begin to grow), with no boundary between them. The fix is to give each idea its own clean space: end one thought before the next, or join them with a connecting word like because, and, or so.

SAME IDEA, ONE CLEAR SENTENCE
Bees carry pollen from flower to flower, and that is how tiny pumpkins begin to grow.

The same thing happened on Question 2, where two sentences got joined with only a comma:

YOU WROTE (Q2)
“People decorate their homes with them, could you imagine stores filled with big boxes with bright orange pumpkins in them?”
TWO IDEAS, JOINED CLEANLY
People decorate their homes with pumpkins, and stores fill big boxes with bright orange ones.

A Bees carry pollen from flower to flower, and that is how tiny pumpkins begin to grow.
B These start the pumpkin to grow by grabbing pollen from one flower to another tiny pumpkins begin to grow.
THE RULE
After each complete idea, ask: “Is this a whole thought?” If yes, end it with a period or join it to the next idea with a connecting word like because, and, or so. Never let one complete idea slam straight into the next with nothing between them — and cut any word you accidentally repeated.

Two Quick Sentence Fixes

Appositives & matching subject to verb

Cooper, two small fixes will pick up points on the editing questions. First, appositives. On Question 4 the task was to rewrite the sentence about bees to add an appositive — a noun phrase that renames the noun. Here is what you wrote:

YOU WROTE (Q4)
“These buzz from flower to flower collecting all the pollen.”

There is no appositive here, and it also changed “Bees” into the vague word “These.” An appositive renames the noun right after it, tucked between two commas:

WITH AN APPOSITIVE
Bees, busy pollinators, buzz from flower to flower.

The phrase “busy pollinators” renames “Bees” — that is the appositive. Second fix: match the subject to the verb. On Question 9 you wrote “Some foods… is…”; because “foods” is plural, it needs are:

YOU WROTE (Q9)
“Some foods that people can make with pumpkins is pumpkin pie, pumpkin cake, and many more things.”
SUBJECT MATCHES VERB
Some foods that people can make with pumpkins are pumpkin pie, pumpkin cake, and many more.
The question asks you to add an appositive that renames “Bees.” Which answer includes an appositive?
A Bees, busy pollinators, buzz from flower to flower.
B These buzz from flower to flower collecting all the pollen.
REMEMBER
An appositive renames a noun with a noun phrase, set off by commas (“Bees, busy pollinators, buzz…”). And check that a plural subject like “foods” takes a plural verb: foods are, not “foods is.”
