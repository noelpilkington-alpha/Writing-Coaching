Change the VERB, Not Add a Word

Q1 — Make the action stronger

The question asked you to rewrite the sentence by changing the verb to make the action stronger. The verb in the sentence is looked. You needed to replace it with a stronger verb.

YOU WROTE
"Layla curiously looked at the flower."

You added the adverb "curiously" — that describes how she looked, but the verb is still "looked." An adverb is not a verb. The question asks you to swap the verb itself.

Tap to see the fixed version
Think: what vivid verb could replace "looked"?
FIXED
"Layla examined the flower."
"Layla studied the flower."
RULE: VERB vs. ADVERB
A verb is the action word (looked, ran, ate). An adverb describes how the action happens (curiously, quickly, loudly). When the question says "change the verb," find the action word and swap it for a more vivid one. Don't add extra words around it.
See more strong-verb swaps
looked → examined, studied, inspected, peered
said → exclaimed, whispered, announced
walked → hiked, trudged, strolled

"Since" Shows the Reason

Q2 — Cause and effect go in the right order

The word "since" introduces the cause — the reason something happened. The effect (what happened as a result) goes in the other part of the sentence.

YOU WROTE
"Layla saw a butterfly, since she took out her camera."

This says Layla saw the butterfly because she took out her camera — that's backwards! She took out her camera because she saw the butterfly. The cause and effect are reversed.

Tap to see the fixed version
Which event happened first?
FIXED
"Since Layla saw a butterfly, she took out her camera."
RULE: SINCE + CAUSE, EFFECT
The word "since" always goes in front of the reason. Ask yourself: What happened first? What happened as a result?

Pattern: Since + CAUSE, EFFECT.
Example: Since it was raining, we stayed inside.
Quick test to check your order
Replace "since" with "because." If the sentence still makes sense, your order is correct.
"Because Layla saw a butterfly, she took out her camera." — makes sense!

Questions Need Question Marks

Q3 — Rewrite as an interrogative sentence

An interrogative sentence is a question. Every question must end with a question mark (?). You wrote a good question but used the wrong punctuation at the end.

YOU WROTE
"Why did the geode feel like a perfect souvenir."

The sentence starts with "Why did" — that's a question! But it ends with a period. A period makes it look like a statement. Simple fix, but it cost you points.

Tap to see the fixed version
Just one character to change!
FIXED
"Why did the geode feel like a perfect souvenir?"
RULE: MATCH YOUR PUNCTUATION TO THE SENTENCE TYPE
Declarative (statement) → period .
Interrogative (question) → question mark ?
Exclamatory (strong feeling) → exclamation mark !

Quick check: Does the sentence ask something? If yes, it must end with ? — always.

THE "BECAUSE" RULE
Whenever a question asks for your opinion or analysis, follow this pattern:

I think [your answer] because [your reason with a detail from the story].

If your sentence doesn't have "because" (or a similar word like "since" or "which shows"), you probably haven't explained why.

PROOFREADING TIP
After you finish writing, go back and read each word one at a time. Point to each word with your finger or pencil. This slows your brain down so you can spot letters that are out of order or missing. Most of these errors are small — you know the words, your hand just moved too fast.
Try this trick: read backwards
Start with the last word of your paragraph and read each word moving backward to the first. This forces you to look at each word by itself instead of reading for meaning. It's one of the best ways to catch typos.

Modern sports developed through rules, leagues, and new ways of life. First, people created rules to make games fair, which allowed teams from different places to compete against each other. When you have many facts, group them into 2-3 clusters of related ideas. Use transition words (First, Next, Finally, Also) to move between groups. This makes your paragraph flow instead of feeling like a list.

The G3 Paragraph Plan

TS → D1 → W → D2 → W → D3 → W → CS

Every expository paragraph follows this plan. Each detail gets a "Why it matters" sentence right after it. Here's how your Sports and Society paragraph should be organized:

Paragraph Plan — Sports and Society
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail  •  W = Why it matters  •  CS = Concluding Sentence
TS Modern sports developed through rules, leagues, and new ways of life.
D1 People created rules to make games fair.
W This let teams from different places compete against each other.
D2 Leagues organized teams into regular competitions.
W Records and scores pushed people to improve.
D3 The Industrial Revolution made people want fun and exercise.
W Schools also helped by training students in sports.
CS That is how sports grew from simple games into the competitions we know today.
HOW TO USE THIS PLAN ON TEST DAY
Before you start writing, jot down your TS and CS first — those are your opening and closing. Then pick 3 details from the passage and write them as D1, D2, D3. After each detail, write one sentence explaining why it matters to your main point. That's 8 sentences total: a complete, organized paragraph.
