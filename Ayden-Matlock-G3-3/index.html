Question 9 Fix — Run-On Sentences

This is your #1 area to improve.

You scored 0.33/1 on this question — the lowest score on the test.

The question asked:

"If you were Anna or Jim, what would you have done differently in the story? Write your answer in one sentence."

Which answer is a clear, complete sentence?
A If I was Anna I would have put the seal in a bigger box so it could move I would also have been more careful I would have hurried.
B If I were Anna, I would have put the seal in a bigger fish box so it could move around more easily.
YOU WROTE
"if is was Anna or Jim what i would have did differentley is i would if put it a big fish box so it a move."
WHAT HAPPENED HERE
Your answer has three problems that all come from writing too fast:
• Missing capital letter at the start ("if" should be "If")
• Words got jumbled ("if is was" and "i would if put it a")
• Missing words ("so it a move" → "so it could move")
THE FIX: READ IT BACK OUT LOUD
Before you move on, whisper your sentence to yourself. If any part sounds jumbled or missing a word, fix it. On a one-sentence question worth 1 point, a clean sentence is almost always the difference between full credit and partial credit.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
You answered this same type of question correctly for Q1–Q5 (combining sentences, adding appositives) — you already know the rules. Slowing down on Q6–Q10 to reread each answer would move you from 63% to 90%+ on these questions.

Proofreading — Spelling & Capitalization

Small fixes that lift your score

Your paragraph on Question 11 has a few spelling and capitalization slips that cost points. These are the easiest fixes because you already know the right versions — you just need to catch them when you reread.

"Tajiri childhood influenced him to make pokemon because when he was a boy he loved catching bugs... Tajiri said every new but was a mystery... creatures calld pokemon."
FOUR THINGS TO CATCH WHEN YOU PROOFREAD
1. Apostrophes for possession: "Tajiri childhood" → "Tajiri's childhood" (the childhood belongs to him).
2. Proper nouns: "pokemon" → "Pokémon" (capital P — it's a name).
3. Typos that look like real words: "every new but" should be "every new bug" — spell-check won't catch it because "but" is a real word.
4. Common misspellings: "calld" → "called".
PROOFREADING TRICK
After you finish writing, read your paragraph one sentence at a time, backwards, starting from the last sentence. Your eyes are less likely to skip over errors when the order is unfamiliar. Aim to spend the last 5 minutes of your 60-minute test doing this.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Spelling, capitalization, and punctuation are graded on Question 11. Four small fixes like these can be the difference between 15/20 and 17/20 — and they are the easiest points on the whole test.

Question 11 — Your Paragraph

From Collecting to Designing: The Story of Pokémon — 15/20 points
Which version is easier for a reader to follow?
A When he got older he liked video games he studied to make games he thought most games were not so good he wanted to make games better.
B When he got older, he liked video games. He studied how to make games. He thought most games were not so good, so he wanted to make them better.
15/20 points 3 sentences (should be 5–6) 95 words

Ayden, you have all the right ideas here. You told Satoshi's whole story — bugs, nickname, growing up, video games, creating Pokémon. The problem is that your last "sentence" is actually six ideas packed into one.

TOPIC Tajiri's childhood influenced him to make Pokémon because when he was a boy he loved catching bugs.

DETAIL 1 (two ideas — needs a period) His friends called him Dr. Bug. Tajiri said every new bug was a mystery and collecting bugs made him happy.

RUN-ON (six ideas — needs 4 or 5 periods) When he got older he liked video games he studied to make games he thought most games were not so good he wanted to make games better he remembered his bug collecting and used that idea in his game players looked for and catch creatures calld pokemon.
WHAT YOU DID WELL
Your ideas are in the right order: bugs → nickname → growing up → video games → studying → creating Pokémon. That is strong storytelling. You also used "because" to connect a cause and effect in your first sentence.
THE BIG FIX: BREAK UP YOUR RUN-ON
Look at your last "sentence." Every time you can pause to breathe while reading it, that is usually where a period belongs. Here is how it breaks apart:

• "When he got older, he liked video games."
• "He studied to make games."
• "He thought most games were not so good, so he wanted to make games better."
• "He remembered his bug collecting and used that idea in his game."
• "Players look for and catch creatures called Pokémon."

Same ideas. Same order. Just periods added. That alone would push this paragraph to 18/20 or higher.
