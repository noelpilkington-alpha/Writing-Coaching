Connector Logic: Comma + "because"

The one multiple choice point — a small, learnable rule
🔍 Pattern alert: Your multiple choice section was 9 of 10 — nearly perfect. The single miss was a punctuation rule about "because." Lock this one rule in and your multiple choice is essentially flawless.

Cole, on Q3 you chose this version:

YOU CHOSE (G6.3 Q3)
"Carter planted milkweed, because it attracts monarch butterflies."

The idea is right — but the comma before "because" is the error. When "because" joins a result to its reason in a single sentence like this, no comma goes in front of it.

Which sentence is punctuated correctly?
A Carter planted milkweed, because it attracts monarch butterflies.
B Carter planted milkweed because it attracts monarch butterflies.
THE RULE
When "because" comes in the middle of a sentence to give the reason for something — the "X because Y" shape — you do not put a comma before it.

✗ "Carter planted milkweed, because it attracts monarchs."
✓ "Carter planted milkweed because it attracts monarchs."

(The one time "because" does take a comma is when the reason comes first: "Because it attracts monarchs, Carter planted milkweed." Different shape, different rule.)
QUICK CHECK
When you see a comma sitting right in front of "because" in the middle of a sentence, pause. Nine times out of ten that comma should not be there. Read it without the comma — if the sentence still flows in one breath, delete the comma.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Your multiple choice section is your anchor — it is what keeps your overall score strong even on a tough essay day. Locking in this one rule turns a 9/10 into a 10/10 and protects the points you already earn well.

Explain Your Evidence

The biggest lever — from naming facts to showing what they mean
🔍 Pattern alert: Your essay earned 15/20 — strong structure, real evidence. The points left on the table are in the same spot each body paragraph: you state the evidence but stop before explaining what it shows.

Cole, here is a real body paragraph from your monarchs essay. The facts are accurate and pulled straight from the passage:

"Recently fewer and fewer butterflies have been succesfully completing the long journey. Much of the land with the milkweed that monarchs rely heavily on has been cleared for residential and agricultural land."

That is solid evidence — but notice it stops at the fact. The reader is left to figure out why it matters. The grader is looking for you to connect the dots.

Which version of this body paragraph earns a higher essay score?
A Fewer butterflies are completing the journey. The land with milkweed has been cleared for houses and farms.
B Fewer butterflies are completing the journey because the land with milkweed has been cleared for houses and farms. This shows that destroying milkweed removes the food and shelter monarchs depend on, which is why their migration is breaking down.

SAME EVIDENCE + ONE EXPLANATION SENTENCE

"Recently fewer and fewer butterflies have been completing the long journey. Much of the land with the milkweed that monarchs rely heavily on has been cleared for residential and agricultural land. This shows that when the milkweed disappears, monarchs lose the food and shelter they need to survive the trip — which is exactly why their migration to Mexico is starting to fail."

THE MOVE: ADD A "WHAT THIS SHOWS" SENTENCE
After every piece of evidence in a body paragraph, add one sentence that answers "So what? Why does this matter?" Start it with a phrase like:

• "This shows that..."
• "This matters because..."
• "As a result..."

That single sentence is the difference between a fact sitting on the page and an argument that earns the point.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Your essay is worth 20 of the 30 points on a G6 test — it is two-thirds of your score. You already do the hard part: finding real evidence. Adding one explanation sentence per body paragraph is the single fastest way to push a 15/20 essay toward 18/20 and your overall score back into the 90s.
