Same skill you missed on G7.1 — let's lock it in

YOU CHOSE

THE TRICK: MATCH EVERY WORD OF THE TOPIC
When a question asks which sentence "best supports" a topic, mentally break the topic into parts and check every part:

Topic: "The town helped drivers and riders share the road."
• Drivers? — must be mentioned
• Riders? — must be mentioned
• Share the road? — must connect them

"Students could bike to school" only hits "riders," so it cannot be the best match. "Signs about the rules for bikes and cars" hits bikes + cars + rules that govern how they share — a three-for-three match.
WHY THIS KEEPS TRIPPING YOU UP
On G7.1 the topic was "benefits for local shops." You picked the sentence about stores staying open longer (the cause) instead of the one about customers shopping more (the benefit). Same move here: you picked a sentence that matched part of the topic but missed the full picture. The fix is the same both times — read the topic word-by-word before you pick.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
This is exactly how graders read your essay too. When they check whether your body paragraph supports your thesis, they look at every part of the topic sentence. If your thesis promises "safety, business, and family travel," each body paragraph needs to actually deliver on its promise — not just one word of it.

Essay Focus — Thesis + One Idea per Paragraph

The two structural moves that lift 15/20 to 18+/20
📊 Pattern alert: Your G7.1 essay and your G7.2 essay both scored 15/20. Two structural moves are holding you at 15: (1) no thesis in your intro, and (2) body paragraphs that mix two topics. This card fixes both.

For an excessive period, people did not feel safe when they were walking are riding on the main roads. WHAT IS MISSING
Your intro has four sentences of setup but never states a thesis. The grader is looking for a sentence that names those three ways upfront. You didn't give them one.

STRONGER INTRO (SAME OPENING, ADDS A THESIS)
"In a small town with too many minor car accidents, residents wanted safer streets. To fix this, the town added bike lanes to its main roads — and the change did more than anyone expected. The bike lane project improved life in the town by making streets safer, boosting local businesses, and bringing families and neighbors closer together."

The last sentence is the thesis. It names the three things your body paragraphs will prove. Now a grader reading just this paragraph knows exactly what is coming.
THE 30-SECOND THESIS TRICK
Before you write anything, look at the prompt and fill in this sentence:

• "____ did/improved ____ by ____, ____, and ____."

For this prompt: "The bike lane project improved life in the town by [safety], [business], and [family travel]."

That is your thesis. The three blanks are your three body paragraphs. The 30 seconds you spend on this save you 5 points on the essay.
WHY A THESIS MATTERS
Graders read your intro first and form an opinion in seconds. A thesis tells them: "this student knows what they are arguing and is organized." No thesis tells them: "this student is still figuring it out." Same content, stronger essay.

Move 2 — One topic per body paragraph

Your G7.2 essay has 5 paragraphs, but Body 2 and Body 3 each mix two unrelated ideas. That is why the ideas feel scattered — the content is there, but the packaging is off.

Which version of Body 2 stays on one topic?
A "Parents mentioned that the streets felt calmer in the evenings. When the bike lanes were first approved, not everybody wanted them. Residents worried that adding lanes would reduce parking..."
B "The bike lanes also made the streets feel calmer. Parents noticed drivers moving more slowly in the evening, and people crossed without rushing. A few months after the lanes went in, the town reported fewer local car crashes."
YOUR BODY 2 (MIXES TWO TOPICS)
"Parents around the towned mentioned that that streets felt safer and clamer in the evenings. When the bike lanes were first getting approved, not everybody wanted them. Residents worried thats adding lanes would reduce the amount of available parking spots. The local delivery drivers added that turing on narrow blocks would become more difficult."
WHAT WENT WRONG
Body 2 starts with a benefit (streets felt safer and calmer) but then pivots hard into a concern (parking, narrow blocks). These are two different topics. The reader has to re-orient halfway through.

Body 3 has the same problem: it starts with "how these problems were solved" and ends with "families can ride together on nice days" — two different ideas glued together.
THE FIX: ONE TOPIC PER PARAGRAPH
Every body paragraph should answer one question:
• Body 1: How did the lanes help safety?
• Body 2: How did the lanes help local businesses?
• Body 3: How did the lanes bring the community together?

If you are writing a sentence that doesn't answer the paragraph's question, stop — either start a new paragraph or cut the sentence.

BODY 2 REWRITTEN (ONE TOPIC: SAFETY)
"The bike lanes also made the streets feel safer. Parents noticed that drivers moved more slowly and people crossed without rushing in the evenings. SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
You already have strong ideas — parking concerns, family riding, fewer crashes, neighbors talking. They are all good. The issue is that they are in the wrong rooms. One idea per paragraph is the fastest way to move from 15/20 to 18+/20 without writing a single new sentence — just rearranging the ones you have.
