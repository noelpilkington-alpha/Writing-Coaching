THE EVIDENCE SANDWICH
Layer 1 — TOPIC SENTENCE: Your main idea (you already nail this).
Layer 2 — CLAIM + SPECIFIC DETAIL from the passage: Your point, plus one concrete thing from the article — quoted OR paraphrased.
Layer 3 — EXPLAIN: One sentence that says what the detail shows or why it matters.

Two sandwiches (two body sentences with details) is enough for a 3rd grade paragraph.
How to QUOTE so you earn points (not lose them)
DO Pull a short, exact phrase from the passage and put it inside "double quotation marks" so the grader can see it's from the article.
The article says golf and cricket were "only for the rich" because they had "more free time."
DO Introduce the quote with a phrase like "The article says…" or "The passage explains…" so it's clear the words are the author's, not yours.
DO NOT Copy a whole sentence or the whole paragraph. Long quotes don't show you understood — they just fill space. Pick 2–8 words that prove your point, then add your own words around them.
DO NOT Quote without explaining. A bare quote earns fewer points. After the quote, always add a sentence that says what it shows.
How to PARAPHRASE so you earn points (not lose them)
DO Put the passage's idea in your own words, keeping the specific facts — names, places, dates, numbers, what someone did.
The article says — rewritten as — By the late 1800s, schools in Britain began holding organized matches with written rulebooks.
DO Keep the detail specific when you paraphrase. "People played sports" is too general. "British schools wrote down cricket rules in the 1800s" has the specific facts.
DO NOT Copy the passage word-for-word without quotation marks — that's not paraphrasing, and it can lose points. Change at least most of the words; keep the key facts.
DO NOT Leave out the specific fact. "People played sports" could be about any time or place. Every paraphrase needs at least one specific detail — a name, a year, a place, or a number.

Answer BOTH Parts of Two-Part Prompts

Question 10 lost you 2 of 3 points — same issue, easy fix
🔍
Pattern: On Question 10, when the prompt has TWO parts (like "What is the rule? Why is it important?"), you've been answering only one part. On G3.4 that earned 1 out of 3 instead of the full 3 points — a small habit that costs 2 points.

Here's what happened on Question 10 of your G3.4 test. The prompt asked for one rule AND why it's important. You nailed the rule. But you didn't say why it was important.

YOUR G3.4 QUESTION 10 ANSWER (1 of 3)
"To take only pictures and leave only footprints."
(You nailed the rule — that's part 1 of the prompt. But the prompt also asked why it's important, and you didn't answer that part. Missing one of two parts = partial credit.)

Here's the fix: when you read a prompt, look for the word "and" or a second question mark in the middle — those are your signals that the prompt has two parts. Every time you see two parts, your one sentence answer has to cover both parts — usually by joining them with the word "because".

Prompt: "What is one rule from the passage? Why is it important? Write your answer in one sentence." Which answer earns full credit?
A To take only pictures and leave only footprints.
B One rule is to take only pictures and leave only footprints because it keeps the park’s plants and rocks safe for other visitors to enjoy.
YOUR 2-STEP MOVE FOR TWO-PART PROMPTS
1. Read the prompt twice. Slowly. Look for the word "and," or a second question mark, or a second "Why" / "How" / "What".

2. Write one sentence that covers both parts. Use "because…" to link part 1 to part 2, pulling in something from the passage (quoted or paraphrased).
Prompts That Often Have TWO Parts
1 "What… AND why?" → name the thing + give the reason (joined with "because")
2 "Would you…? Why or why not?" → yes/no + reason from passage (joined with "because")
3 "Name one thing. How is it connected to…?" → name the thing + explain the connection
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Questions 6–10 are each worth 3 points. Going from 1 of 3 up to 3 of 3 on Question 10 alone is 2 raw points right there. Across future tests, this one habit (read twice, answer with "because") could be worth a full point or more every time. Quick fix, steady points.

What Is an Appositive?
DEF An appositive is a noun phrase that renames another noun right next to it, set off by commas on BOTH sides.
My dog, Lucy, loves the beach. → "Lucy" renames "my dog."
NOT Not an appositive: breaking it into two or three separate sentences.
NOT Not an appositive: missing the second comma ("My dog, Lucy loves the beach" — needs a comma after "Lucy" too).
Which sentence is a correct appositive?
A "My family has two dogs. The best two dogs. Their names are Lucy and Rocket."
B "My family has two dogs, Lucy and Rocket, and they are the best."
