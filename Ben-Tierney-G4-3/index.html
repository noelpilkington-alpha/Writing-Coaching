YOUR READ-BACK CHECKLIST (last 3 minutes)
Check 1 — Every comma. Before each comma, ask: is this already a complete sentence? After the comma, is that also a complete sentence? If BOTH are complete, you have a comma splice. Change the comma to a period or add "and/but/so/because."

Check 2 — Articles. Before a word that starts with a vowel sound (a, e, i, o, u), use "an". "An award," "an apple," "an idea." Not "a award."

Check 3 — Spelling of longer words. impact (not imapct), nervous (not nervouse), separated (not seperated), example (not exsample).

Your #1 Repeated Error: Comma Splices on Q1

When combining sentences, a comma alone is not enough

Ben, Q1 asks you to combine sentences in a clear and effective way. You have the right idea every time — you are trying to make one smooth sentence. The problem is that you often use a comma to glue two full sentences together, which creates a comma splice. This has shown up in all 3 tests:

"One unique flower I have seen is the bluebonnet, it is a wildflower with bright blue petals."

G4.3 Q1 — still there
EXAMPLE 3 OF 3 — FROM G4.3 Q1
"They swished their tiny tails as if waving hello, it made me so happy!"
Same pattern. "They swished their tiny tails as if waving hello" is a complete sentence. "It made me so happy" is also a complete sentence. A comma alone cannot join them.
THE RULE
If two parts of a sentence could each stand alone as a complete sentence, a comma alone is not enough. Your choices:

1. Use a period: "They swished their tails as if waving hello. It made me so happy!"
2. Add a joining word after the comma: "They swished their tails as if waving hello, and it made me so happy!"
3. Turn one part into an appositive (a describing phrase): "The bluebonnet, a wildflower with bright blue petals, is unique."

Joining words: and, but, so, because, or.
Q1 asks you to combine: "Roadsides and hills take on a whole new look." + "Meadows do too."

Appositives: Describe the Right Noun

An appositive renames the noun it sits next to

Ben, Q3 usually asks you to add an appositive. An appositive is a short describing phrase that renames or gives more information about a noun. The rule: the appositive sits right next to the noun it describes. Here is where this has been tricky across your tests:

FROM G4.1 Q3 (full credit)
"Peter Tabichi, the person who won the award, is from Kenya, Africa, and teaches high school science."
Perfect — the appositive "the person who won the award" sits right next to "Peter Tabichi" and describes him.
FROM G4.2 Q3 (0/2) — prompt asked for an appositive about the geode
"Sally carefully swam to the other seals."
This added an adverb ("carefully"), not an appositive. An appositive would be a describing noun phrase, like: "Sally, a young seal with brown fur, swam to the other seals."
FROM G4.3 Q3 (1/2) — prompt: rewrite with appositive about Noah's mom
"He asked his mom for some lettuce, his favorite food."
The appositive "his favorite food" is next to "lettuce," so it describes lettuce — not Noah's mom. The prompt asked for an appositive about mom. Try: "He asked his mom, a kind gardener, for some lettuce."
Q3 asks you to add an appositive about Noah. Which version works?
A Noah fed the worms, his new pets, some lettuce.
B Noah, a kind and curious boy, fed the worms some lettuce.
THE APPOSITIVE CHECK
Before you write your answer, read the prompt again. It will say "add an appositive about X" — find that X. Your appositive has to sit right next to X in the sentence, with commas around it.

✗ "He asked his mom for some lettuce, his favorite food." (describes lettuce)
✓ "He asked his mom, a kind gardener, for some lettuce." (describes mom)
