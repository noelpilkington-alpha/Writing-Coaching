Q5 Fix — Using Because, But, So

Can you spot the right way to join these sentences? Then we will break it down.
📈 You have missed "using because, but, so" questions on 3 of 5 tests — but you got it right on G3.4 and G3.5, which proves you CAN do this. The pattern each time: not finishing the combined sentence.

The question asked you to combine these two sentences using because, but, or so:

22. Inside a pumpkin is stringy pulp and many seeds.
23. The seeds can be cleaned and baked for a snack.

YOUR ANSWER
"Inside a pumpkin is stringy pulp and many seeds," (stopped here — the sentence was not finished)
Tap the sentence that correctly combines both ideas:
A Inside a pumpkin is stringy pulp and many seeds, but the pulp is messy.
B Inside a pumpkin is stringy pulp and many seeds, so the seeds can be cleaned and baked for a snack.
Word What It Does When to Use It
Because Tells the reason why something happens "I wore a coat because it was cold."
But Shows something different or surprising "Pumpkins look tough, but they are soft inside."
So Shows what happens next as a result "The seeds are inside, so you can clean and bake them."
THE KEY RULE
When combining sentences, you must include both ideas — the part before the connecting word AND the part after it. Always re-read your answer and check: Did I finish the whole sentence?
See another example
Sentence 1: "The garden was full of weeds."
Sentence 2: "Mom pulled them all out."

Combined: "The garden was full of weeds, so Mom pulled them all out."
Both ideas are there — the weeds AND what Mom did about them.

Q4 Fix — Writing Appositives

An appositive gives extra information right next to a word. Can you spot the right one?
📈 You have missed writing appositives on 3 of 5 tests. But you can always find appositives when you read them (100% on that skill!) — the gap is in writing your own.

The question asked you to rewrite this sentence with an appositive giving more information about the bees:

16. Bees buzz from flower to flower.

YOUR ANSWER
"Bees buzz from flower to flower, geting pollen."

Two problems: the spelling (getting, not "geting") and this is not an appositive — it describes what bees do, not what bees are.

Which version has a real appositive about the bees?
A Bees buzz from flower to flower, getting pollen along the way.
B Bees, important helpers in the garden, buzz from flower to flower.
A GOOD ANSWER
"Bees, important helpers in the garden, buzz from flower to flower."
WHAT IS AN APPOSITIVE?
An appositive is a group of words right next to a noun that renames or describes that noun. It is set off by commas.

Test: Can you replace the noun with the appositive and it still makes sense?
• "Bees, important helpers in the garden, buzz..." → "Important helpers in the garden buzz..." — yes, that works!
• "Bees, getting pollen, buzz..." → "Getting pollen buzz..." — no, that does not work. Not an appositive.
See another example
Original: "My dog runs fast."
Not an appositive: "My dog runs fast, chasing a ball." (tells what the dog does)
Appositive: "My dog, a speedy golden retriever, runs fast." (tells what the dog is)

Q7 Fix — Writing Sentences from Prompts

Your idea was right, but the explanation needs to be clearer.
📈 Writing sentences from prompts is your most-tested skill — you have seen it on every test. Your answers usually have the right idea but lose points on unclear explanations.

The question asked: Write one sentence explaining why bees help pumpkins grow.

YOUR ANSWER
"I think that bees help pumpkins, because bees help flowers and flowers are the same plant as the pumpkins."

Your idea is on the right track — bees do help the flowers! But "flowers are the same plant as the pumpkins" is confusing. The passage says bees carry pollen from flower to flower, and that is what helps pumpkins grow fruit.

Which sentence gives a clear explanation?
A Bees help pumpkins grow because they help the flowers on the plant.
B Bees help pumpkins grow because they carry pollen from flower to flower, which lets the plant make fruit.
THE "SO WHAT?" TEST
After you write your explanation, ask yourself: "Did I say HOW or WHY?"

• "Bees help flowers" — how? Why does that matter?
• "Bees carry pollen from flower to flower, which lets the plant make fruit" — now we know the HOW!

Always push one step deeper. Do not stop at "they help" — say what they actually do.

Another "explain why" question. Same pattern as the last card.

YOUR ANSWER

The spelling grow (not "gro") is a small fix. The bigger issue: "because the pumpkins and the vines are the same plant" does not explain why the vines grow longer. The passage talks about the pumpkin getting heavier and the vine spreading.

B The pumpkin changes color because it is growing.
THE SAME RULE AGAIN: GO ONE STEP DEEPER
When a question says "explain why," your answer needs to give a real reason from the passage — not just restate the question in different words.

• "Vines grow because they are the same plant" — that is a restatement, not a reason
• "The color changes from green to orange because the sun and nutrients help it ripen" — that is a real reason from the passage

Q9 Fix — Uses for Pumpkins

Almost full points! One small fix to clean up your sentence.

The question asked: Write one sentence describing what people can use pumpkins for after they are picked. Include an example.

YOUR ANSWER
"I think that one thing that people can use after a pumpkin picked, is they can be cleaned and baked as a snack."

Your example (cleaned and baked as a snack) is great — that comes right from the passage! The issue is that the sentence is hard to follow because of how it is put together. Notice: "after a pumpkin picked" is missing the word "is", and the sentence switches from "one thing" to "they."

Which version is clear and easy to read?
A One thing people can use after a pumpkin picked is that they can be cleaned and baked for a snack.
B After a pumpkin is picked, people can clean and bake the seeds for a tasty snack.
KEEP SENTENCES SIMPLE AND CLEAR
When you write a sentence, read it back to yourself: Does it sound like something you would say out loud?

If it sounds tangled, try starting with the simplest version: WHO + DID WHAT + EXAMPLE.
• Who: People
• Did what: can clean and bake the seeds
• Example: for a tasty snack

THERE vs. THEIR vs. THEY'RE
These three words sound the same but mean different things:
• There = a place or "there is/there are" — "There is math involved."
• Their = belongs to them — "Their cookies were delicious."
• They're = they are — "They're learning to cook."
PROOFREADING TIP
After you finish writing, read your work one more time slowly. Touch each word with your finger or pencil as you read. This helps you catch missing letters and mixed-up words.
