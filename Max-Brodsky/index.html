TWO RULES FOR COMPLETE SENTENCES
1. No fragments: If your sentence starts with "when," "because," or "since," make sure there's a main idea too. "When she found the geode" is a fragment. "The most important moment was when she found the geode" is complete.

2. Check for missing words: After you write a sentence, read it out loud slowly. If something sounds off, you probably skipped a word. "Thought was" doesn't make sense — "thought it was" does.
See more examples
Fragment: "Because it was raining outside." → Complete: "We stayed inside because it was raining outside."
Missing word: "She wanted go home." → Fixed: "She wanted to go home."

Stop the Comma Splice

Q11 — Your paragraph has comma splices

A comma splice happens when you join two complete sentences with just a comma. Your paragraph has several of these:

"Sports began in Britain" is a complete sentence. "They used to be only for the upper class" is also complete. A comma alone can't join them.

THE COMMA SPLICE FIX
A comma alone can never join two complete sentences. You have three choices:

1. Add a conjunction: , and / , but / , so
2. Use a period and start a new sentence.
3. Use a semicolon (;) if the ideas are closely related.

Quick test: Cover the comma. Can each side stand alone as a sentence? If yes, you need more than a comma.

Develop Your Paragraph

Q11 — Add a topic sentence, transitions, and a conclusion

Your paragraph scored 16/20. You wrote 6 sentences with good details about sports history, but your paragraph is missing a clear topic sentence, transition words, and a concluding sentence.

Your Paragraph
Sports began in Britain, they used to be only for the upper class. The industrial revalution came along and the middle class had more free time. Rules were set in place to stop arguments on how the sport was played. Once rules were set in place sport leagues were made and consisted of many different teams. People started playing with points to have winners and losers, this got recorded and now you can compare players. You have great details! But you jump right into facts without telling the reader what your paragraph is about. Also notice: "revalution" should be revolution, and "there" should be their.

Here's the G3 paragraph plan you should follow:

Paragraph Plan — TS → D1 → W → D2 → W → D3 → W → CS
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail from the article  •  W = Why it matters  •  CS = Concluding Sentence
TS Modern sports developed over time and became popular because of new rules, leagues, and fans.
D1 Sports began in Britain and were originally only for the upper class.
W This shows that sports were not available to everyone at first.
D2 After the Industrial Revolution, the middle class had more free time and started playing sports too.
W This helped sports grow because more people could participate.
D3 Rules were created, leagues were formed, and people started keeping score.
W This made sports more organized and exciting, which attracted fans.
CS Overall, modern sports became popular because they went from a small activity for the wealthy to an organized competition that everyone could enjoy.
THREE THINGS YOUR PARAGRAPH NEEDS
1. A topic sentence: Tell the reader what your paragraph is about FIRST. Answer the prompt in your opening sentence.

2. Transition words: Use words like "First," "Next," "In addition," "As a result" to connect your ideas smoothly.

3. A concluding sentence: Wrap up your main point at the end. Don't introduce new details — restate your central idea.
