Stop the Comma Splice

Q2 & Q4 — Two sentences need more than a comma

A comma splice happens when you join two complete sentences with just a comma. You did this twice:

Q2 — Expand the sentence with details:

YOU WROTE
Dad found the pup early in the morning, he freed is because it got stuck in a fishing net.

Good details! But two complete thoughts are smashed together with just a comma. Also, "freed is" should be "freed it."

FIXED
Dad found the pup early in the morning, and he freed it because it got stuck in a fishing net.

Q4 — Combine two sentences into one:

YOU WROTE
Anna and Jim ran to the beach, they found a baby seal in a fishnet!

Two complete sentences joined by just a comma. You need a conjunction after the comma.

FIXED
Anna and Jim ran to the beach, and they found a baby seal in a fishnet!
THE COMMA SPLICE FIX
A comma alone can never join two complete sentences. You have three choices:

1. Add a conjunction: , and / , but / , so
2. Use a period and start a new sentence.
3. Use a semicolon (;) if the ideas are closely related.

Quick test: Cover the comma. Can each side stand alone as a sentence? If yes, you need more than a comma.

Q5: "But" Is Not a Subordinating Conjunction

Learn the difference between two types of conjunctions

Q5 asked you to combine sentences using a subordinating conjunction. You used "But" — but "but" is a coordinating conjunction, not a subordinating one.

YOU WROTE
But ...

Here's the difference:

Coordinating (FANBOYS) → for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so
These go BETWEEN two sentences → I was tired, but I kept going.

Subordinating conjunctions go at the beginning and show a relationship (cause, time, contrast):

Because, Since, Although, When, Before, After, If, While, Until
EXAMPLES WITH SUBORDINATING CONJUNCTIONS
Since the seal was trapped, Dad carefully cut the net.
Because Anna loved animals, she wanted to help the baby seal.
Although the seal was scared, it let Anna get close.
HOW TO REMEMBER
Subordinating conjunctions start the sentence and make one part depend on the other. Think of the word "sub" — it means "under." The subordinating conjunction makes one idea support (go under) the other.

Common ones to memorize: Because, Since, Although, When, Before, After, If, While

Develop Your Paragraph

Q11 — Great start, but you need more sentences

Your paragraph scored 17/20. You had a strong topic sentence, but you only wrote 2 sentences total. A G3 paragraph needs more development.

YOUR TOPIC SENTENCE
Satoshi Tajiri's childhood was a good influence for the creation of Pokemon because when he was a kid, he loved to catch bugs.

This is a great start! You answered the prompt right away. But the paragraph needs details from the article and explanations of why they matter.

Here's the G3 paragraph plan you should follow:

Paragraph Plan — TS → D1 → W → D2 → W → D3 → W → CS
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail from the article  •  W = Why it matters  •  CS = Concluding Sentence
TS Satoshi Tajiri's childhood love of catching bugs led him to create Pokémon.
D1 As a child, Tajiri spent hours catching bugs — his friends called him "Dr. Bug."
W This shows how much he loved collecting creatures, which became the main idea behind Pokémon.
D2 When he grew up, he remembered his bug-catching joy and used it as the idea for a video game.
W This shows that without that childhood memory, the game might never have been created.
D3 Pokémon became huge — over 150 characters, TV shows, movies, apps, and more.
W This shows that one kid's hobby turned into something millions of people enjoy.
CS Tajiri's childhood hobby of catching bugs turned into one of the most popular games in the world.
THE KEY: ADD "WHY IT MATTERS" AFTER EACH DETAIL
You already know how to find details in the article. The next step is to explain why each detail matters to the main point. After each detail, ask yourself: "So what? Why does this show that his childhood influenced Pokémon?" That answer is your "W" sentence. Also, always end with a concluding sentence that wraps up your main point.
