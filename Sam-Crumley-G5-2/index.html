THE "NAME ONE THING" RULE
Before you submit any written answer, scan it for vague phrases like "better at it," "certain things," "that stuff," or "in many ways." When you spot one, ask yourself: "Can I name one specific example from the passage?"

Vague: "Practicing makes you better at it."
Specific: "Practicing helped Grandma Addie get the filling just right after dozens of tries."

Vague: "Small details can add attention to detail on certain things."
Specific: "The curve of the letters in a logo can make people feel trust."

One concrete example turns a 2/3 answer into a 3/3 answer.

Graders are checking whether you understood the passage and can explain your thinking. When you write "being more consistent and better at it," the grader cannot tell whether you actually understood the passage or you are just echoing the question. A specific example proves comprehension. That is why it earns full credit.

Watch: Sentences That Trail Off

G5.2 Q7 — "which gets you..." loses the reader
🔍
What happened on Q7: Your sentence started strong but then added "which gets you..." and kept going without a period. When a sentence runs past its natural ending, it loses clarity — the grader cannot tell where your idea ends and the next one begins.
G5.2 Q7 — TRAILS OFF INTO A RUN-ON
You wrote: "Practicing over time can lead to success by being more consistent and better at it which gets you..."
After "better at it," the sentence should end or transition clearly. "Which gets you..." adds a dangling clause that goes nowhere.

The fix is simple: end the sentence when the thought is complete. If you want to add a second idea, start a new sentence.

CLEAN VERSION — ONE IDEA PER SENTENCE
✓ "Practicing over time can lead to success because each try helps you notice what needs fixing. For example, Grandma Addie adjusted her pie filling many times before it tasted exactly right."

First sentence: states the claim. Second sentence: gives the specific example. No trailing "which gets you..." — each sentence has a clear start and stop.

THE "PERIOD TEST"
After you write a sentence, ask: "Does this sentence have exactly one main idea?" If you find yourself adding "which..." or "that gets..." or "because then..." without a clear endpoint, put a period and start fresh.

✗ "Practicing makes you better which gets you to the point where..."
✓ "Practicing makes you better. Each try helps you notice what to fix next."

Two short, clear sentences always beat one long, trailing one.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
A trailing run-on signals to the grader that you lost control of the sentence. Even if your idea is right, the run-on makes it harder to follow — and harder to follow means lower score. Ending cleanly proves you know where your thought stops.

D+E pattern (Detail + Explanation): A supporting detail names the fact. The explanation tells the reader why it matters or what it shows. At G5, the rubric expects both. A detail without explanation reads as a list, not an argument.

S.D. 2 Second detail from the passage + explanation.
