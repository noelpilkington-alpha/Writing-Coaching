Comma After Subordinating Clauses

G5.1 Q4 — 1 out of 2
🔍
The rule: When a sentence starts with a subordinating clause (while, after, because, when, if, although...), you need a comma before the main clause begins. The comma separates the setup from the main action.

Zara, on Q4 you wrote a sentence that starts with "while the rest of the class filed in" — that is a subordinating clause. It sets up the scene. But then you jumped straight into the main clause ("Crista felt even more guilty") without a comma to separate them. The reader needs that comma to know the setup is over and the main action is starting.

G5.1 Q4 — MISSING COMMA (SCORED 1 OUT OF 2)
You wrote: "Finally, while the rest of the class filed in Crista felt even more guilty."
The subordinating clause "while the rest of the class filed in" needs a comma after it — right after "in" — before "Crista felt..." begins.
FIXED VERSION
✓ "Finally, while the rest of the class filed in, Crista felt even more guilty."

The comma after "in" tells the reader: the setup is done, here comes the main clause.
HOW TO SPOT THIS
Look for these words at the start of a sentence (or after a comma):
while, when, after, before, because, if, although, since, unless

If one of those words kicks off a clause that is NOT the main point of the sentence, put a comma at the end of that clause, right before the main action starts.

✓ "While everyone was eating lunch, she slipped out."
✓ "After the bell rang, Crista walked slowly to her seat."
✓ "When the class filed in, Crista felt guilty."
Which sentence uses the comma correctly?
A Although she was nervous she raised her hand and told the truth.
B Although she was nervous, she raised her hand and told the truth.
THE QUICK TEST
Read your sentence aloud. If you naturally pause between the setup and the main clause, that pause needs a comma. "While the class filed in... [pause] ...Crista felt guilty." That pause = comma.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q4 was worth 2 points and you earned 1 — that is 1 point lost from a single missing comma. On a 45-point test, 1 point is about 2 percentage points. A comma after subordinating clauses is one of the cheapest points to win back because you already know how to write the sentence — you just need to add the punctuation.

Fix Run-On Sentences

G5.1 Q6 — 2 out of 3
🔍
What is a run-on? When two complete sentences are crammed together with no punctuation between them. The fix: put a period between them and start a new sentence.

Zara, on Q6 you wrote one long sentence that is actually two separate ideas jammed together with no punctuation. You can hear it when you read it aloud — there is a place where one thought ends and the next begins, but there is nothing on the page to separate them.

G5.1 Q6 — RUN-ON SENTENCE (SCORED 2 OUT OF 3)
You wrote: "One time I had to admit that I broke a vase that belonged to my mom after I told her what happened she was not mad at me"
Two complete ideas: (1) "One time I had to admit that I broke a vase that belonged to my mom." (2) "After I told her what happened, she was not mad at me." They need a period between them.
FIXED VERSION
✓ "One time I had to admit that I broke a vase that belonged to my mom. After I told her what happened, she was not mad at me."

Period after "mom" ends the first thought. "After I told her what happened" is a subordinating clause — note the comma before the main clause, just like we covered on the previous card.

Also notice that the original answer does not clearly describe what happened. It says "I broke a vase" but then "after I told her what happened" is vague. A stronger answer would name the specific action: "After I told her I was the one who broke it, she was not mad at me."

Which version correctly splits the run-on into two clear sentences?
A One time I had to admit that I broke a vase that belonged to my mom after I told her she was not mad at me.
B One time I had to admit that I broke a vase that belonged to my mom. After I told her I was the one who broke it, she was not mad at me.
THE RUN-ON CHECK
Read your sentence aloud. If you run out of breath or lose track of the idea, there are probably two sentences crammed into one. Find the spot where the first idea ends and the second begins. Put a period there. Start the next word with a capital letter.

Signal words that often start a second idea: after, she, he, they, then, it, when, because

If you see one of those words in the middle of a long sentence, check whether everything before it is already a complete thought.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q6 was worth 3 points and you earned 2 — that is 1 point lost. The grading also noted that the answer does not clearly describe what happened, which ties into the "be specific" skill on the next card. Fixing run-ons is about clarity: when each idea gets its own sentence, the reader (and grader) can follow your thinking.

THE "WHICH STORY?" TEST

Paragraph: Detail + Explanation

G5.1 Q11 — 15 out of 20 — "Say What, Horse?"

Zara, your paragraph scored 15 out of 20 (75%). That is where the biggest chunk of points is sitting. The opening has the right idea, but there are a few issues holding it back: awkward phrasing, a spelling error, and not enough development.

YOUR Q11 OPENING
You wrote: "There are several reasons why what new research reavels about horses' ability to communicate..."
"reavels" should be "reveals". Also, the phrasing "several reasons why what new research reveals" is tangled — there are too many clause layers stacked before the reader gets to the point.
CLEANER OPENING OPTIONS
✓ "New research reveals surprising facts about how horses communicate."
✓ "According to the article 'Say What, Horse?' new research shows that horses communicate in ways scientists did not expect."

Shorter, clearer, and correctly spelled. Get to the point in one clean clause.
Awkward Phrasing Spelling Error Needs More Development

To move from 15/20 toward a higher score, your paragraph needs:

  • A clear, clean topic sentence — get to the point without stacking multiple clauses
  • Two or three specific pieces of evidence from the passage (numbers, details, examples)
  • Explanation after each piece of evidence — tell the reader WHY it matters
  • A closing sentence that wraps up the main idea
  • Proofread for spelling — "reveals" not "reavels"

Clean opener + evidence + explanation + closing
MODEL PARAGRAPH (full-credit level)

New research reveals surprising facts about how horses communicate with their faces. According to the article "Say What, Horse?" scientists have identified 17 different facial expressions that horses use. For example, horses raise the skin above their eyes when they experience negative emotions like fear or pain. This matters because it shows that horses have a rich emotional life that humans can learn to read. Understanding these expressions can help people take better care of their horses by noticing when something is wrong. Overall, this research proves that horses communicate more than most people realize.

Clean topic sentence. Two pieces of evidence with explanation. Closing sentence that wraps up the idea. Six sentences total.

SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
The paragraph is worth 20 points — nearly half the test. Moving from 15/20 to 18/20 would push your overall score from 82% to about 89%. The recipe: clean topic sentence + two evidence details with explanation + a closing line. That structure alone is worth several points.
