What Is an Appositive?

Q3 — Learn this important grammar tool

An appositive is a noun phrase that renames or describes the noun right next to it. It is set off by commas.

EXAMPLE
"My dog, a golden retriever, loves to swim."
The phrase "a golden retriever" is the appositive — it renames "my dog."

Q3 asked you to add an appositive about what kind of music helps with learning.

YOU WROTE
"Music can help you by calming you down and seting the mood and you can learn aswell."

This describes what music does, but it is not an appositive. An appositive would rename "music" with a noun phrase set off by commas.

Read the explanation above first
FIXED
"Music, a powerful learning tool, can help you learn too."

The phrase "a powerful learning tool" is an appositive — it renames "music" and is set off by commas.

RULE: APPOSITIVE = NOUN PHRASE + COMMAS
An appositive renames the noun beside it. It is always a noun phrase (not a verb phrase), and it is set off by commas.

Pattern: Noun, [appositive], rest of sentence.
Example: "Texas, the Lone Star State, is known for bluebonnets."

Read the Directions Carefully

YOU WROTE

FIXED

This uses "so" — one of the three choices the question gave.

ALSO: YOUR vs. YOU'RE
Your = something that belongs to you ("your book").
You're = "you are" ("you're studying" = "you are studying").

Quick test: Replace the word with "you are." If it makes sense, use you're. If not, use your.
RULE: ALWAYS RE-READ THE QUESTION
Before you write your answer, underline or circle the key words in the question. If it says "use because, but, or so," make sure your answer uses one of those three — not a different word.

Run-On Sentences

Q2 & Q7 — Keep one complete thought per sentence

A run-on jams two or more complete thoughts together without proper punctuation or conjunctions.

Q2 — Your answer also used "more happier," which is a double comparative:

YOU WROTE (EXCERPT)
"...they listened to calm music and slept better and they were more happier."

"More happier" should be just "happier." When a word already ends in "-er," don't add "more" in front of it.

Q7 — Two issues: "like listen" and a run-on after "running":

YOU WROTE
"I like listen to music when it's studying time, or when im running it gets me in the right mindset."

"like listen" should be "like to listen" or "like listening." And "im" should be "I'm." After "running," a new thought begins without any punctuation — that's the run-on.

Tap to see the fixed version
Read the explanation above first
FIXED (Q7)
"I like to listen to music when it's studying time, or when I'm running because it gets me in the right mindset."
RULES TO REMEMBER
1. No double comparatives: "happier" is already comparative. Don't write "more happier" — just "happier."

2. Fix run-ons: If your sentence has two complete thoughts, connect them with a conjunction (and, but, because, so) or split them with a period.

3. "I" is always capitalized: "im" → "I'm"

Answer the Question First

Q11 — Your paragraph retold the story but didn't state the lesson

The prompt asked: "What LESSON do readers learn from Ferdinand Cheval's story?"

Your paragraph retold what happened in the story, but it didn't name the lesson. The topic sentence should directly answer the question.

YOUR PARAGRAPH

Ferdinand Cheval was an ordnary man, he was a mail man carrier in a small town in France, but he bulit a stone palace all by himself, it took 33 years! On his walks, he would pick up rocks. He did this for 20 years, he glued them together and used his imagination, and at the end of the day he made a palace with all the rocks!"

You retold the events well, but you never stated what lesson readers learn. Your topic sentence should start by naming that lesson.

Tap to see a better topic sentence
Think about what LESSON the story teaches
BETTER TOPIC SENTENCE
"Ferdinand Cheval's story teaches readers that hard work and imagination can turn an ordinary life into something extraordinary."

This names the lesson right away. Then you use details from the passage to explain why that lesson is true.

RULE: ANSWER THE PROMPT IN YOUR TOPIC SENTENCE
Before you start writing, re-read the prompt. If it asks "What lesson...?" your topic sentence should say: "[Character]'s story teaches that [lesson]." If it asks "How did...?" start with: "[Character] did this by [answer]." Don't retell the story — answer the question.

Paragraph Plan

TS → D1 → W → D2 → W → D3 → W → CS

Here is how your paragraph about Ferdinand Cheval could follow the G4 paragraph plan:

Paragraph Plan — "A Legacy of Stone"
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail from the article  •  W = Why it matters  •  CS = Closing Sentence
TS Cheval's story teaches that patience and creativity can create something amazing.
D1 He was just a mail carrier in a small town in France.
W This shows that you don't need to be special to start something great.
D2 He collected rocks on his walks for 20 years.
W This shows incredible patience — he didn't give up even after years of work.
D3 He built an entire palace by himself using those rocks and his imagination.
W His imagination turned simple rocks into art, which shows how powerful creativity is.
CS Cheval shows that anyone with a dream and determination can leave a lasting mark on the world.
THE KEY: DETAIL + WHY IT MATTERS
After each detail from the article, ask yourself: "So what? Why does this support the lesson?" That answer is your "W" sentence. This is what turns a retelling into an explanation. Also, always end with a closing sentence that restates your main point without adding new details.
