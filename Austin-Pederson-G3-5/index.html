Were vs Where

Q1 — 1 out of 2 weighted points

Austin, on Q1 you used the wrong word. These two words look similar but mean completely different things.

Q1 — YOUR ANSWER (1 out of 2)
"Where the books sold in department stores?"

The problem: You wrote "Where" but the sentence needs "Were." These are two different words:

THE RULE
Were = past tense of "are." It asks or tells about something that happened.
• "Were the books sold in stores?" (Yes or no question about the past.)

Where = asks about a place or location.
• "Where were the books sold?" (Asking which place.)
CORRECTED
"Were the books sold in department stores?"

Quick test: If you can replace the word with "are" and the sentence still makes sense as a question about yes/no, then you need were. If the sentence is asking about a location, you need where.

Which sentence uses the correct word?
A Where the children playing outside yesterday?
B Were the Little Golden Books popular with children?
REMEMBER
Were = was it happening? (past tense of "are")
Where = what place?

Next time you write one of these words, pause and ask: "Am I asking about a place, or am I asking whether something happened?"

Write an Appositive

Q4 — 1 out of 2 weighted points

Austin, your Q4 answer tried to pack three ideas into one sentence, but they ended up joined with commas in a way that creates comma splices (two complete sentences joined by just a comma). There is a better structure called an appositive that fixes this.

Q4 — YOUR ANSWER (1 out of 2)
"The books are still popular, more that two billion copys have been printed, they are known for they golden spines."

The problems:

  • Comma splices: Three complete sentences joined by commas only.
  • "copys" should be "copies" (when a word ends in -y, change the y to -ies).
  • "they" should be "their" (showing ownership).
WHAT IS AN APPOSITIVE?
An appositive is a naming phrase placed between commas that adds extra information about a noun. It lets you combine ideas without creating a comma splice.

Pattern: Subject, naming phrase between commas, rest of sentence.

Example: "Little Golden Books, a series with more than two billion copies printed, are known for their golden spines."
REWRITTEN WITH AN APPOSITIVE
"Little Golden Books, a series with more than two billion copies printed, are still popular and known for their golden spines."

Notice how the appositive tucks the extra fact neatly inside the sentence instead of creating three separate clauses joined by commas.

Which sentence correctly uses an appositive to combine these ideas?
A The Poky Little Puppy was very popular, it sold millions of copies.
B The Poky Little Puppy, one of the best-selling children's books of all time, sold millions of copies.
SPELLING REMINDER
Words ending in a consonant + y: change the y to -ies for plural.
• copy → copies
• story → stories
• puppy → puppies

And remember: their = belonging to them. They = the people themselves.

Remove Extra Commas

Q2 and Q3 — 1 out of 2 weighted points each

Austin, on Q2 and Q3 you added commas that do not belong. Both errors follow the same pattern: you put a comma where the sentence does not need a pause.

Q2 — YOUR ANSWER (1 out of 2)
"The Poky Little Puppy, was very popular."

The rule: Never put a comma between a subject and its verb. "The Poky Little Puppy" is the subject and "was" is the verb. They go together with no comma between them.

CORRECTED
"The Poky Little Puppy was very popular."
Q3 — YOUR ANSWER (1 out of 2)
"The books had golden spines, and colorful pages."

The rule: When two things share the same subject and the same verb, do not put a comma before "and." Here, "the books" had both golden spines and colorful pages. One subject, one verb, two objects joined by "and" = no comma.

CORRECTED
"The books had golden spines and colorful pages."
WHEN DO YOU NEED A COMMA BEFORE "AND"?
You only need a comma before "and" when you are joining two complete sentences (each with its own subject and verb).

• "The books had golden spines and colorful pages." → No comma. Same subject.
• "The books had golden spines, and children loved the colorful covers." → Comma. Two different subjects ("books" and "children").
Which sentence is punctuated correctly?
A The sunken museum has ancient artifacts and colorful exhibits.
B The sunken museum has ancient artifacts, and colorful exhibits.
THE QUICK CHECK
Before you put a comma before "and," ask: Does the part after "and" have its own subject?

• If yes → comma is fine.
• If no (same subject) → remove the comma.

Your G3.5 Paragraph

Q11 — 16 out of 20 — needs more development

Austin, your paragraph on Q11 earned 16 out of 20. That is a solid score. Your ideas come through and your structure is clear. The 4 points that slipped away were about developing your ideas more fully.

What "more development" means: After you give a detail or example, add one more sentence that explains why it matters or what it shows. This turns a quick point into a fully developed point.

THE PATTERN: DETAIL + "THIS SHOWS..."
Step 1: Give a detail or example from the passage.
Step 2: Write one more sentence starting with "This shows..." or "This matters because..." that explains the detail.

Without development: "Divers can see statues and artwork underwater."

With development: "Divers can see statues and artwork underwater. This shows that the sunken museum gives people a way to experience history that they could not find in a regular building."
