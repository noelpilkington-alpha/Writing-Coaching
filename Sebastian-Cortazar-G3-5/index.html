#1: What a True Appositive Is

An appositive is a naming phrase — not “and,” and not a verb
🔍 Pattern alert: Two questions this test asked about appositives, and both point to the same idea. On Q4 you combined the sentences with and instead of an appositive, and on Q5 you named became as the appositive. Let's lock in exactly what an appositive is.
WHAT AN APPOSITIVE IS
An appositive is a noun phrase that renames the noun right beside it, set off by commas. It says the same thing in a new way. It is never joined with and, and it is never a verb.

Example: The books, known for their golden spines, are still popular. The phrase known for their golden spines renames the books.

Here is your real Q4. The task was to combine the two sentences using an appositive:

YOU WROTE (G3.5, Q4)
"The books are still popular, and they are known for their golden spines."
AS A TRUE APPOSITIVE
"The books, known for their golden spines, are still popular."

Your sentence is correct English — but joining with and makes it a compound, not an appositive. The appositive tucks the naming phrase between commas instead.

And here is your real Q5. The task was to identify the appositive in: The Poky Little Puppy, a best-selling story, became a favorite.

YOU WROTE (G3.5, Q5)
"became is the appositive."
CORRECT
"a best-selling story is the appositive."

became is a verb — it tells what happened. The appositive is the naming phrase between the commas, a best-selling story, because it renames The Poky Little Puppy.

Which sentence uses a true appositive?
A The books are still popular, and they are known for their golden spines.
B The books, known for their golden spines, are still popular.
THE TEST TO USE
To find or write an appositive, ask two things:

• Is it a naming phrase (a noun idea), not a verb like became?
• Is it set off by commas right next to the noun it renames, with no and?

If both are yes, it is a true appositive.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Appositives showed up twice this test, so knowing this one rule cleanly protects two questions at once. Rename the noun with a comma-phrase, and never reach for and or point at a verb.

🔍 Pattern alert: On Q7 your idea was right, but the sentence was a little loose — it started with a vague It, had a comma in an odd spot, and did not quite finish as one clear thought. Tightening it into a single complete sentence earns the full point.
