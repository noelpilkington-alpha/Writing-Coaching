Q4: Add Specific Details

Expand your kernel sentence with clear, precise words

This question asked you to expand a kernel sentence — take a short sentence and add details that make it more interesting and informative.

YOU WROTE
She had a constitutional right to stay seatened.

You had the right idea — Claudette Colvin believed the Constitution protected her. But two things cost you points:

  • "Seatened" should be "seated." This is a spelling error that changes how the sentence reads.
  • "Stay seated" is vague. The expansion doesn't add enough detail. Where was she seated? Why did she stay? A strong expansion paints a clearer picture.
EXPANDED WITH DETAILS
Claudette Colvin believed she had a constitutional right to stay seated in her bus seat, so she refused to give it up.
THE EXPANSION TRICK
When you expand a kernel sentence, ask yourself three questions:

Who? (Name the person.)
Where / What? (Add a specific place or thing.)
Why? (Give the reason.)

The more of these you answer, the stronger your expansion will be.

Q9: Adverbs + Run-on Sentences

Two small fixes that make a big difference

Your answer on Q9 had two issues. Let's look at each one:

1. "Unfair" should be "unfairly."

YOU WROTE
People treated her unfair...
CORRECTED
People treated her unfairly...

"Treated" is a verb, so you need an adverb (unfairly) to describe how she was treated — not an adjective (unfair). Adverbs usually end in -ly.

treated her unfair → treated her unfairly
she spoke quiet → she spoke quietly
he ran quick → he ran quickly

2. Run-on sentence.

Your answer also had two complete thoughts joined without proper punctuation. When two ideas are jammed together, that's a run-on. Fix it by splitting them into two sentences or by adding a comma and a connecting word.

ADVERB QUICK CHECK
If a word describes how someone does something, it's an adverb. Most adverbs end in -ly.

Ask yourself: How? Treated how? → unfairly. Spoke how? → quietly.

For run-ons: read the sentence out loud. If you hear two separate ideas, you need a period or a comma + connecting word between them.

Your Paragraph: Use Evidence from the Article

This is your biggest opportunity to improve — 13/20

Your paragraph answered the prompt about "The Need for High-Speed Trains?" but it was very short (only 4 sentences) and didn't include any specific evidence from the article. Let's look at what you wrote:

Your Full Paragraph
High speed trains are benificial and neccessary. They are neccessary because they make travel time quicker. High speed trains take less resources to power. This is why they are better than cars and are extremely neccessary.

You made a clear claim — high-speed trains are beneficial — but every sentence is a general statement. There are no facts, numbers, or details from the article. On a writing test, the paragraph section is asking you to show what you learned from the passage.

What was missing:

  • No evidence from the article. How much quicker? How many fewer resources? The article had these details — the paragraph needs them.
  • Only 4 sentences. A strong expository paragraph needs 7–9 sentences: a topic sentence, 3 details with explanations, and a concluding sentence.
  • No explanation of why each detail matters. After each fact, tell the reader what it shows.

Here's what a paragraph plan for this prompt could look like:

Paragraph Plan — "The Need for High-Speed Trains?"
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail from the article  •  W = Why it matters  •  CS = Concluding Sentence
TS High-speed trains are beneficial and necessary because they save time, use fewer resources, and help the environment.
D1 [Detail from the article about travel time — use a specific fact or number]
W This shows that high-speed trains can make long trips much shorter, which saves people hours of travel.
D2 [Detail from the article about resources or energy — use a specific fact]
W This matters because using fewer resources means the trains are cheaper and better for the planet.
D3 [Detail from the article comparing trains to cars or planes — use a specific fact]
W This shows that high-speed trains are a smarter choice than driving or flying for many trips.
CS For all of these reasons, high-speed trains are an important part of the future of transportation.
THE EVIDENCE RULE
Every detail sentence should include something specific from the article — a fact, a number, a name, or an example. If you can't point to where it came from in the passage, it's a general claim, not evidence.

General: "They make travel time quicker."
With evidence: "According to the article, a high-speed train can travel from City A to City B in just two hours, while driving takes over five."

The second version shows the reader you actually read and understood the article.
