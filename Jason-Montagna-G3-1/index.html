Question 1 — Writing an Appositive

What an appositive actually is — and what it is not

From G3.1 Q1.

The question asked you to combine two sentences into one using an appositive. The original sentences were about a family with two dogs named Lucy and Rocket.

Which sentence uses an appositive?
A My family has two dogs, and their names are Lucy and Rocket.
B My family has two dogs, Lucy and Rocket.
FROM G3.1 Q1 — YOU WROTE
"My family has two dogs, and their names are Lucy and Rocket."

Your sentence works as a sentence — but it is not an appositive. You joined two thoughts with "and," which makes it a compound sentence. The question specifically asked for an appositive.

APPOSITIVE VERSION
"My family has two dogs, Lucy and Rocket."
WHAT IS AN APPOSITIVE?
An appositive is a noun or short phrase that renames another noun right next to it. It tells the reader more about the noun — like giving it a nickname or a label.

The formula is simple:
• Noun, appositive that renames the noun.

Examples:
• "My dog, Rocket, loves to run." — "Rocket" renames "dog"
• "My guide, Mr. Ore, helps me learn." — "Mr. Ore" renames "guide"
• "We live in Miami, a city in Florida." — the phrase renames "Miami"
MEMORY TRICK
Ask yourself: "Can I put the second part right next to the noun with just a comma — no extra words like 'and,' 'their names are,' or 'he is'?"
If yes → that is an appositive.
If you had to add extra connecting words → you wrote a compound sentence instead.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Appositives make your writing sound smoother and more grown-up. Instead of stacking two short sentences, you can slip in extra information in one clean phrase. Once you see the pattern — noun, renaming noun — you will catch it every time.

Question 2 — Turning a Statement into a Question

A question mark is not enough — the words have to move

From G3.1 Q2.

The question asked you to rewrite sentence 4 ("There is one place we don't take the dogs.") as a question.

Which one is actually a question?
A There is one place we don't take the dogs?
B Is there one place we don't take the dogs?
FROM G3.1 Q2 — YOU WROTE
"There is one place we don't take the dogs?"

You added a question mark at the end, but the sentence still starts like a statement. A question mark by itself does not transform a sentence into a question — the words themselves have to move.

REAL QUESTION
"Is there one place we don't take the dogs?"
HOW REAL QUESTIONS START
Most real questions start with a helping verb that flips to the front of the sentence:

• Is / Are / Was / Were → "Is the dog hungry?"
• Do / Does / Did → "Does the dog bark?"
• Can / Could / Will / Would → "Can the dog come inside?"
• Or a question word: Who, What, Where, When, Why, How → "Where does the dog sleep?"
QUICK TRICK
Take the statement: "There is one place..."
Find the little verb (is, are, was, were, do, does, can, will).
Move it to the front: "Is there one place...?"

That swap is the whole move. The question mark just confirms it.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Questions are one of the most common things writers are asked to make. Once you have the helping-verb flip memorized, you will get these right instantly. The fix is tiny — one word moves — but it changes everything about how the sentence reads.

Questions 6 & 8 — Answering the Exact Question

Read the prompt carefully — what is it actually asking?

From G3.1 Q6 and Q8.

This is a big one. Two questions on this test asked specific things, and the answers drifted off topic. The fix is one small habit: underline the key word in the prompt before you write.

The prompt asks: "How would you feel if a dog walked up to you?" Which answer actually answers it?
A If a dog walked up to me I would let it smell my hand then slowly try to pet it.
B If a dog walked up to me, I would feel a little nervous but also excited, because I love dogs but I would not know if this one was friendly.
FROM G3.1 Q6 — YOU WROTE
"If a dog walked up to me I would let it smell my hand then slowly try to pet it."

Good instinct — that is a smart thing to do around a new dog! But the prompt asked how you would feel, not what you would do. The answer is about feelings (nervous, excited, happy, scared), not actions.

ANSWERING THE FEELING QUESTION
"If a dog walked up to me, I would feel a little nervous but also excited, because I love dogs but I would not know if this one was friendly."

Now here is the other one:

FROM G3.1 Q8 — YOU WROTE
The prompt: "What is one place where dogs should be allowed?"
Your answer: "I would either ignore it, or go ask the owner of the dog if i could pet it."

This one looks like the Q7 answer got pasted into Q8 by mistake — it talks about a barking dog, not about a place where dogs should be allowed. The prompt asked for a place: a park, a beach, a backyard, a pet-friendly store, etc.

ANSWERING THE PLACE QUESTION
"One place where dogs should be allowed is the park, because dogs need open space to run around and get exercise."
THE UNDERLINE TRICK
Before you write, find the one key word in the prompt that tells you what kind of answer to give. Underline it in your head.

• "How would you feel..." → key word: FEEL → name an emotion
• "What is one place..." → key word: PLACE → name a location
• "What would you do..." → key word: DO → name an action
• "Why..." → key word: WHY → give a reason
• "How..." → key word: HOW → explain the way it happens
TWO-STEP SELF-CHECK
After you write your answer, go back and read the prompt one more time. Ask: "Did I answer the exact key word?"

If the prompt says FEEL and your answer only says what you would DO — you missed the target. Add a feeling sentence. Easy fix.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
A perfect answer to the wrong question scores the same as no answer at all. This one habit — pausing to find the key word in the prompt — protects more points than almost anything else. It is the same skill you will use on reading tests and math word problems for years.

Question 4 — Fixing a Run-On Sentence

When two complete thoughts get smushed together

From G3.1 Q4.

The question asked you to combine two sentences using because, but, or so. You used "but" and "because" — good instinct — but the sentence ended up as a run-on.

Which version is not a run-on?
A Secondly dogs might growl or bite, but there's a reason for this it's because they might be nervous or tired.
B Dogs might growl or bite because they are nervous or tired.
FROM G3.1 Q4 — YOU WROTE
"Secondly dogs might growl or bite, but there's a reason for this it's because they might be nervous or tired."

Look closely at the middle: "there's a reason for this it's because..." Two complete thoughts are crammed together with no punctuation between "this" and "it's." That is a fused sentence — one type of run-on.

WHAT IS A RUN-ON SENTENCE?
A run-on sentence happens when two complete thoughts are joined without proper punctuation or a connecting word.

Two common types:
• Fused sentence — no punctuation at all: "there's a reason for this it's because..."
• Comma splice — just a comma between two thoughts: "Dogs bark, this scares people."
FIX 1 — KEEP IT SHORT AND CLEAN
"Dogs might growl or bite because they are nervous or tired."
FIX 2 — SPLIT WITH A PERIOD
"Dogs might growl or bite. There is a reason for this — they might be nervous or tired."
THREE WAYS TO FIX A RUN-ON
1. Split into two sentences. Add a period and start a new sentence.
2. Add a comma and a joining word (and, but, so, because).
3. Tighten it down. If the sentence has extra words like "there's a reason for this it's because," just use because once and cut the rest.
HOW TO CATCH RUN-ONS
Read the sentence out loud. If you hear two separate ideas — two things that could each be their own sentence — check whether you connected them properly. If there is nothing (or just a comma) between them, you have a run-on.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Run-on habits travel with you into every essay, every short answer, every email. By G3.3 you were already catching most of these. The trick now is to keep watching for them, especially when you are trying to pack a lot of ideas into one sentence.

Question 11 — Writing a Full Paragraph

One sentence is not a paragraph — here is the recipe

From G3.1 Q11. The prompt was: "Explain the ways that soil is important to Earth."

Which response is a full paragraph?
A Soil is important to the earth because it helps plants grow and is home to animals like bugs, worms, and burrowing groundhogs.
B Soil is important to Earth in many ways. First, soil gives plants a place to grow, and plants provide food and oxygen for people and animals. Second, soil is home to small creatures like bugs, worms, and burrowing groundhogs, which help keep the ground healthy. Without soil, Earth would not be able to support the life that lives on it.
FROM G3.1 Q11 — YOU WROTE
"Soil is important to the earth because it helps plants grow and is home to animals like bugs, worms, and burrowing groundhogs."
Good Central Idea Two Real Reasons Only One Sentence No Topic / Closing

Jason, here is the cool part: the ideas in your one sentence are correct. Soil does help plants grow, and it is home to bugs, worms, and groundhogs. You had the right answer — you just did not stretch it into a paragraph.

The 4-Part Paragraph Recipe
TOPIC One sentence that states your main idea (answers the prompt directly)
DETAIL 1 A specific reason or example that supports your main idea
DETAIL 2 Another specific reason or example
CLOSING One sentence that wraps up and connects back to the main idea
Tap to see your one sentence stretched into a full paragraph
Try writing your own version first, then compare
YOUR IDEAS, STRETCHED INTO A PARAGRAPH

Topic: "Soil is important to Earth in many ways."
Detail 1: "First, soil gives plants a place to grow, and plants provide food and oxygen for people and animals."
Detail 2: "Second, soil is home to small creatures like bugs, worms, and burrowing groundhogs, which help keep the ground healthy."
Closing: "Without soil, Earth would not be able to support the life that lives on it."

THE STRETCH MOVE
When a prompt says "explain" or "describe," your brain should whisper: at least 4 sentences. Even if your first thought is one sentence, force yourself to break it into smaller pieces and expand each one.

Your G3.1 answer is really two ideas smushed together:
• "it helps plants grow" → this is Detail 1
• "it is home to bugs, worms, and groundhogs" → this is Detail 2
You already had the bones. You just needed to put each one in its own sentence and wrap with a closing.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Question 11 is worth 20 out of 45 points on a G3 test — almost half your score. A single-sentence answer leaves a lot of points on the table, even when the ideas are right. By G3.3 you were writing strong paragraphs with a topic sentence and details. That jump is the biggest single reason your score went from 58% to 87%.
