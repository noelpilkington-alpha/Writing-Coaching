Fix Comma Splices (Questions 6–10)

The one habit costing you a point on every short answer
🔍 Pattern alert: This exact error showed up in all five short answers — Q6, Q7, Q8, Q9, and Q10. Each one joined two complete sentences with only a comma, and each one earned 2 of 3 points instead of 3. That is one point five times over.

Brooklyn, here is the habit. A comma splice happens when you take two complete sentences and glue them together with just a comma. A comma is not strong enough to do that job. Here are two of your real answers:

YOU WROTE — Q6 (earned 2 of 3 points)
"In spring time my favorite thing to do is paint flowers, I like this because it helps me realize the nature around me."
YOU WROTE — Q9 (earned 2 of 3 points)
"One place that feels special to me and my family is Ephriam Utah, this is specail because we were all born and rasied there until we moved to Texas."

In each one, the part before the comma is a complete sentence, and the part after the comma is also a complete sentence. The comma alone cannot hold them together.

Which version is correct?
A In spring my favorite thing to do is paint flowers, I like it because it helps me notice nature.
B In spring my favorite thing to do is paint flowers because it helps me notice the nature around me.
THE RULE — TWO WAYS TO FIX A COMMA SPLICE
A comma cannot join two complete sentences. You have two clean fixes:

1. Keep it to ONE complete sentence. Join the idea to its reason with because or so. This is the best fit for Questions 6–10, because each one asks for one sentence with a reason.

2. Use a period and start a new sentence. If both parts are full sentences and you want to keep them both, end the first with a period and start the next with a capital letter.

✗ "I like spring, it makes me happy." (comma splice)
✓ "I like spring because it makes me happy." (one sentence with a reason)
✓ "I like spring. It makes me happy." (period + new sentence)

YOUR Q9, AS ONE CLEAN SENTENCE (full 3 points)

SPOT IT ON YOUR PROOFREAD PASS
On your second read of Questions 6–10, find every comma. Ask: "Is the part before the comma a complete sentence, AND is the part after it a complete sentence too?" If the answer is yes to both, it is a comma splice — rebuild it as one sentence with "because"/"so," or split it with a period.

Expand the Fragment Correctly (Question 2)

Turn the given fragment into a complete sentence
🔍 Pattern alert: On Q2 you were asked to expand a fragment into a complete sentence. Instead you wrote a personal opinion about it, so the fragment was never actually completed — that is why it earned partial credit.

Brooklyn, Question 2 gives you a fragment — a piece that is not yet a full sentence — and asks you to grow it into a complete, descriptive sentence. The fragment was something like "a wildflower with bright blue petals." That names a thing, but it has no action and no complete thought yet.

YOU WROTE — Q2 (earned partial credit)
"I like the wildflower with bright blue peddals."

The task was not "tell me your opinion about it." The task was to take that fragment and finish it into a sentence that describes the thing — what it does or what it looks like in action.

Which one correctly expands the fragment "a wildflower with bright blue petals" into a complete sentence?
A I think the wildflower with bright blue petals is my favorite one.
B The wildflower with bright blue petals sways in the spring wind and opens toward the sun.
HOW TO EXPAND A FRAGMENT
Take the fragment and add the missing pieces so it becomes a complete thought:

1. Find the thing the fragment names ("a wildflower with bright blue petals").
2. Give it an action or a complete description — what does it do, or what is it like? (sways in the wind, grows along the roadside, opens toward the sun).
3. Read it back and make sure it can stand on its own as a full sentence.
