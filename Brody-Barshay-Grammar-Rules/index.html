Grammar Rules Reference

The general rules, each one a check you can run. Keep this page open when you write

Nineteen rules, a quick check, and a rules list you can print.

1. A SENTENCE
Find the action word. Find the doer. Is the thought finished? All three there: a sentence. Any one missing: a fragment. Long does not mean finished.
SENTENCEKites fly.
FRAGMENTthe red kite in the tall tree
No action word. The red kite is stuck in the tall tree.
FRAGMENTWhen the wind picked up
When leaves it hanging. When the wind picked up, the kite rose.
2. TWO THOUGHTS
Two thoughts, two sentences, or one joining word between them (and, but, so, because). A comma on its own cannot hold two sentences together.
RIGHTThe rain stopped. We went outside.
RIGHTThe rain stopped, so we went outside.
RUN-ONThe rain stopped we went outside.
COMMA ALONEThe rain stopped, we went outside.
3. FOUR KINDS OF SENTENCE, FOUR END MARKS
The end mark matches. A statement (declarative) tells and ends with a period. A question (interrogative) starts with a question word or a helper such as do, did, can, is, and ends with a question mark. A command (imperative) starts with the action word and ends with a period. An exclamation (exclamatory) tells with strong feeling and ends with an exclamation mark. It never asks.
STATEMENTSwimmers rinse off after the pool.
QUESTIONDo swimmers rinse off after the pool?
COMMANDRinse off after the pool.
EXCLAMATIONThat pool is freezing!
4. GROWING A SENTENCE
Grow a bare sentence: when, where, why, how. Start with the doer and the action word, then add the answers. The dog barked. becomes Last night, the dog barked at the gate because a fox ran past.
GROWNAfter school, the team practiced free throws in the gym because the finals were on Friday.
STILL BAREThe team practiced a lot.
A lot answers nothing. When, where, why, how.

Capitals

Three places a capital goes, and where it does not
5. CAPITAL LETTERS
A capital goes on: the first word of a sentence; the word I; and names, months and titles: one particular person, place, pet, brand, day, month, holiday, and the main words of a book or film title. The check: is it the name of one particular one? Capital. Is it a kind of thing (a dog, a city, a season)? Small letter.
6. AFTER A COMMA
After a comma, a small letter, unless it is a name or I.
RIGHTOn Friday, Maya and I visited Denver.
RIGHTWe read The Hobbit in March.
RIGHTA quick learner, Max mastered the sit command in just one day.
Yours. Max keeps its capital after the comma because it is a name.
WRONGon friday, maya and i visited denver.
Six capitals missing.
WRONGIn the Summer we swim.
A season is a kind of time, not a name. summer.

Punctuation

Commas, apostrophes, quotation marks
7. COMMAS
A comma goes: between items in a list (eggs, milk, and bread); before and, but or so when they join two whole sentences (I ran, but I missed the bus.); after an opening phrase (After lunch, we read.); and on both sides of a naming phrase (Leo, my cousin, plays drums.). A comma hugs the word before it, then one space. A comma never holds two sentences together on its own.
LISTWe packed hats, snacks, and water.
TWO SENTENCES JOINEDThe bus was late, so we walked.
OPENING PHRASEAfter the game, the team ate pizza.
WRONGSam ,my cousin ,plays drums.
Spaces on the wrong side. Sam, my cousin, plays drums.
8. APOSTROPHES
An apostrophe marks a missing letter or an owner. can’t, it’s: letters are missing. Sam’s bike: Sam owns it. A plural noun, more than one of something, gets none: dogs, knives. The owner words its, his, hers never take one.
MISSING LETTERWe didn’t hear the bell.
OWNERThe coach’s whistle was loud.
WRONGThe dog’s ran across the yard.
A plural noun, nothing owned. The dogs ran.
9. QUOTATION MARKS
Quotation marks go around exact words: what someone said, word for word, or a short exact phrase copied from a passage. Your own words about the passage get none.
EXACT WORDSThe article says the reefs “can rest.”
YOUR OWN WORDSThe article explains that the reefs get a break from swimmers.

Words That Agree

Subject and verb, tense, pronouns, sound-alikes
10. SUBJECT AND VERB
One thing: is. More than one: are. After did: the plain verb. One thing takes is, was, has, does, runs. More than one takes are, were, have, do, run. After did, does or do, the verb goes back to its plain form: did win, not did won. The check: point at the doer and count it.
RIGHTUnder the porch are three puppies.
RIGHTDid the bus leave on time?
WRONGUnder the porch is three puppies.
11. TENSE
Pick a tense and stay in it. If the sentence starts in the past, the other verbs stay in the past.
RIGHTThe air was freezing, so we sweated less.
WRONGThe air was freezing, so we sweat less.
12. I AND ME
I does the action. Me receives it. Sam and I went. The coach spoke to Sam and me. The check: take the other person out. I went, not me went. Spoke to me, not spoke to I.
13. SOUND-ALIKE PAIRS
These sound the same and mean different things. its (belongs to it) / it’s (it is). their (belongs to them) / there (a place) / they’re (they are). your (belongs to you) / you’re (you are). to (toward) / too (also, or extremely) / two (2). where (a place) / wear (clothes). then (next) / than (comparing). The check: say the long form back. If it is fits, write it’s.

Joining and Naming

Joining words, and the naming phrase
14. JOINING WORDS
Each joining word has a job. and adds. but contrasts. so gives the result. because gives the reason. since gives the reason or the time. or gives a choice. When and, but or so join two whole sentences, a comma goes before them. because and since take no comma when they come second in the sentence. Use the joining word the instruction names, not a different one.
RESULTThe gym was closed, so we ran outside.
REASONWe ran outside because the gym was closed.
CONTRASTWe wanted the gym, but it was closed.
WRONG JOBWe ran outside, and the gym was closed.
And only adds. The second half is the reason, so because.
15. JOINING WORDS THAT LEAN
Some joining words start a part that cannot stand alone: because, since, when, if, after, before, while, although, until. Comma when the joining part comes first. When it comes second, no comma.
FIRST, COMMAWhen the bell rang, we lined up.
SECOND, NO COMMAWe lined up when the bell rang.
WRONGWe lined up, when the bell rang.
16. THE NAMING PHRASE (APPOSITIVE)
An appositive is a naming phrase. It gives the thing another name, starts with a, an, the or my plus a naming word, and has no verb inside. The check: cover the noun. Can your phrase stand in its place? In the middle, commas on both sides. First in the sentence: naming word first, small letter after the comma.
MIDDLEKoalas, fuzzy Australian animals, eat eucalyptus leaves all day.
Yours, full marks.
FIRSTA skilled night hunter, the owl catches mice in the dark.
Yours, full marks.
NOT A NAMING PHRASEThe drum, that was out of shape, was made from a can.
That was has a verb and cannot stand in for the drum. The drum, a loud instrument, was made from a can.

Paragraphs and Outlines

The shape that earns the twenty points
17. THE PARAGRAPH
The topic sentence answers, it does not ask. It is a statement that answers the question in the prompt. Then two or more supporting details, each one a fact from the passage followed by a This shows sentence that says what the fact proves. Then the concluding sentence: one sentence, no new fact. Six sentences at least.
THE SHAPE, ON A MADE-UP PASSAGE ABOUT BEES
TOPIC SENTENCEBees matter to farmers because they pollinate the crops. DETAIL 1 · SHOWThe passage says one hive can visit a million flowers in a day. DETAIL 1 · SO WHATThis shows that a single hive does work no farmer could do by hand. DETAIL 2 · SHOWIt also says apple trees without bees grow fewer than half as many apples. DETAIL 2 · SO WHATThis shows that the harvest depends on the bees showing up. CONCLUDING SENTENCEBees are small, but a farm cannot work without them.
18. TRANSITIONS, AND STAYING ON TOPIC
Transition words show the reader the order: First, Next, Then, Finally. For example brings a detail, However makes a turn, In conclusion ends. A comma follows a transition word that starts a sentence. Every sentence serves the topic sentence. The check: cover the topic sentence, read the detail, ask whether it proves it. If not, cut it.
TRANSITIONFirst, the hive wakes before dawn.
OFF TOPICBees matter to farmers. My cousin is scared of bees.
The second sentence is about the cousin, not the farmers. Cut it.
19. THE OUTLINE
Before the paragraph, the outline: the topic sentence as a full statement, then notes, and notes are key words, not sentences. If a note could stand on its own with a period, it is a sentence, not a note. Then the concluding sentence. On the test, type the outline at the top of the box, write the paragraph under it, then delete the outline before you submit.
NOTEmillion flowers a day, hive
NOTEapple trees, fewer than half
A SENTENCE, NOT A NOTEOne hive can visit a million flowers in a day.
