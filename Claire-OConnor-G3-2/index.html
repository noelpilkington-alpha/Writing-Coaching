The 4 Sentence Types

Question 4 — match the sentence to its job

Claire, on Question 4 you were asked to name the type of this sentence:

QUESTION 4 SENTENCE
"Workers spent over 30 minutes clearing the ice."

You chose imperative. But an imperative sentence gives a command — this sentence simply tells you a fact. That makes it declarative (a statement). Here are the four types and the job each one does:

The 4 Sentence Types
STATE Declarative — tells you something. Ends with a period. "Workers cleared the ice."
ASK Interrogative — asks a question. Ends with a question mark. "Did the workers clear the ice?"
EXCITE Exclamatory — shows strong feeling. Ends with an exclamation point. "The ice is finally clear!"
TELL Imperative — gives a command. Ends with a period. "Clear the ice now."
Which of these is a declarative sentence (a statement that tells you a fact)?
A Clear the ice before the next game.
B Workers spent over 30 minutes clearing the ice.
THE TRICK
Ask one question: what is this sentence doing?

• Telling you a fact → declarative (.)
• Asking something → interrogative (?)
• Showing strong feeling → exclamatory (!)
• Giving a command → imperative (.)

"Workers spent over 30 minutes clearing the ice" is telling you a fact, so it is declarative.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Naming the sentence type was the one short question you missed on this test. Learn the four jobs above and that point comes straight back — it is a quick, reliable win.

Pick the Right Connector

Question 10 — the joining word and where the comma goes

Claire, on Question 10 you combined two ideas. Here is what you wrote:

YOU WROTE (G3.2 Q10)
"The ice was covered in teddy bears but, the playyers skated among the bears."

Two things to fix here. First, "but" shows a contrast, but these two ideas have a result relationship — the ice was covered, so the players skated among the bears. Second, the comma belongs before the joining word, not after it. Each joining word has its own job:

Pick the Connector by Its Meaning
REASON because — tells why. "The players skated carefully because the ice was covered."
CONTRAST but — shows a difference. "The ice was covered, but the game still started."
RESULT so — shows what happened next. "The ice was covered, so the players skated among the bears."
Which version combines the two ideas correctly?
A The ice was covered in teddy bears so, the players skated among the bears.
B The ice was covered in teddy bears, so the players skated among the bears.
YOUR SENTENCE, FIXED
"The ice was covered in teddy bears, so the players skated among the bears."
Changed "but" to "so" (result), moved the comma before "so," and fixed the spelling of "players."
THE RULE
Before you join two ideas, ask what is the relationship?

• Telling why → because
• Showing a difference → but
• Showing a result → so

Then put the comma before the joining word.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Choosing the right connector and placing the comma correctly is a skill the test checks more than once. Get the relationship right and the comma in the right spot, and you lock in points you are currently leaving on the table.
