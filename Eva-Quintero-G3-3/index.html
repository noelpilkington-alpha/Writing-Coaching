Your Paragraph: Answer the Prompt

The most important rule — your paragraph must answer the question

Your paragraph scored 2/20. Here's why: the prompt asked a specific question about the article, but your paragraph didn't answer that question.

YOU WROTE

I know the Pokémon article might not have been your favorite topic, but on a test, you need to show what you learned from the article even if you didn't love it. The prompt asks a specific question, and your paragraph must answer that question.

Here's what the paragraph plan should look like:

Paragraph Plan — "How Tajiri's childhood influenced Pokémon"
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail from the article  •  W = Why it matters  •  CS = Concluding Sentence
TS Satoshi Tajiri's love of catching bugs as a kid led him to create Pokémon.
D1 As a child, Tajiri spent hours catching bugs — friends called him "Dr. Bug."
W This shows how much he loved collecting creatures, which became the main idea behind Pokémon.
D2 When he grew up, he remembered his bug-catching joy and turned it into a game idea.
W This matters because without that childhood memory, the game might never have been created.
D3 Pokémon became huge — 150+ characters, TV shows, movies, apps.
W This shows that one kid's hobby turned into something millions of people enjoy.
CS Tajiri's childhood hobby turned into one of the most popular games in the world.
THE TOPIC SENTENCE TRICK
Read the prompt, then turn it into your first sentence:

Prompt: "Explain how Satoshi Tajiri's childhood influenced the creation of Pokémon."
Topic sentence: "Satoshi Tajiri's childhood love of catching bugs influenced him to create Pokémon."

You flip the prompt into a statement. Then your details come after. Even if you don't love the topic, this trick gets you started.

Proofreading Basics

Small fixes that make a big difference

Several of your answers lost points because of small errors that are easy to fix once you know to look for them. Let's build a quick checklist:

0 checked
Capital letter at the start of every sentence.
In Q9 you wrote "let the seal find his parents..." — that needs a capital L to start.
Period (or question mark) at the end of every sentence.
Several of your answers didn't have ending punctuation. Every sentence needs a full stop.
Check spelling of names and common words.
thay → they
Shally → Sally
his (for Sally) → her (Sally is a girl seal)
Read each sentence out loud.
Does it sound finished? If it sounds like half a thought, add more. If it sounds like two thoughts smashed together (a run-on), split it into two sentences with a period.
Check: did you answer what the question actually asked?
If the question asks for one word, write one word. If it asks for a paragraph about the article, write about the article. Read the question one more time before you finish.
