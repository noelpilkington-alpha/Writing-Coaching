What IS a Complete Sentence?

Q2 — 0 out of 2 — Fragment fix

Armin, Q2 asked you to fix a fragment. Here is what you wrote:

YOUR ANSWER
"His dedication to education and his impact on his students' lives health and knowlage."

You added more words ("health and knowlage"), but the sentence is still a fragment. Why? Because there is no verb. Look at it: "His dedication... and his impact..." — what about them? The sentence names things but never says what those things do.

THE COMPLETE-SENTENCE TEST
Every complete sentence needs two things:

1. A subject — who or what the sentence is about
2. A verb — what the subject does or is

If you can point to both, it is a sentence. If either is missing, it is a fragment.
Which of these is a complete sentence?
A His dedication to education and his impact on his students' lives health and knowledge.
B His dedication to education and his impact on his students' lives inspired people around the world.
A CORRECT FIX FOR Q2
"His dedication to education and his impact on his students' lives inspired people around the world."

The word "inspired" is the verb that completes the thought. Without it, the sentence just hangs there naming things. Next time you fix a fragment, ask: "Where is the verb? What does the subject DO?"

How Appositives Work

Q3 — 0 out of 2 — Appositive comma placement

Q3 asked you to add an appositive (a renaming phrase) to a sentence about Peter Tabichi. Here is what you wrote:

YOUR ANSWER
"Peter Tabichi, is from Kenya, Africa, teaches high school science."

The problem: you put commas around the main clause ("is from Kenya, Africa") instead of around the naming phrase. That broke the sentence apart. The main subject-verb ("Peter Tabichi... teaches") got disconnected.

WHAT IS AN APPOSITIVE?
An appositive is a naming phrase that renames or describes the noun right next to it. It sits between two commas, like a parenthetical insert:

Pattern: [Subject], [appositive phrase], [verb]...

The rule: if you lift the appositive out, the sentence underneath must still be complete.
Which sentence correctly uses an appositive to add information about Peter Tabichi?
A Peter Tabichi, is from Kenya, Africa, teaches high school science.
B Peter Tabichi, a science educator from Kenya, teaches high school science.
THE LIFT-OUT TEST
After you write an appositive, cover the words between the commas with your hand. Does the sentence still make sense?

Peter Tabichi, [a science educator from Kenya], teaches high school science.
Cover it: "Peter Tabichi teaches high school science." — still complete.

If covering the middle breaks the sentence, the commas are in the wrong place.

Combining Sentences Correctly

Q5 — 1 out of 2 — Comma splice

Q5 asked you to combine two sentences using a conjunction. You connected the ideas, but you used a comma where you needed a comma plus a conjunction word.

THE COMMA SPLICE RULE
Two complete sentences cannot be joined by a comma alone. That is called a comma splice.

Wrong: Peter Tabichi won the award, he donated most of his salary.
Right: Peter Tabichi won the award, and he donated most of his salary.

The fix: add a conjunction after the comma (and, but, so, yet).
Which version correctly combines two sentences?
A Peter Tabichi gave away most of his salary, he wanted to help his students succeed.
B Peter Tabichi gave away most of his salary, because he wanted to help his students succeed.
⚙ Connection to Q2 and Q3: All three errors come from the same root habit. You make changes to a sentence without re-reading it to check: does it still have a subject, a verb, and proper connections? The "point to the subject, point to the verb" habit fixes all three.
