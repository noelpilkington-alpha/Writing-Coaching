Your 6-Step Test Process

A reliable routine for the G6 writing test (90 minutes)

Cole, you already work fast and accurately on the multiple choice. The biggest gains come from how you approach the essay. This six-step routine puts your planning energy where the points are. You have a full 90 minutes — there is plenty of room to plan before you draft.

The 6 Steps
STEP 1 Read the passage closely. Note the main idea and the specific examples the author actually uses.
STEP 2 Answer the multiple choice carefully. This is your strength — keep checking the relationship a question is testing (cause? contrast? addition?).
STEP 3 Read the essay prompt twice. Decide your thesis — the one clear claim your whole essay will prove.
STEP 4 Type a quick outline first. For each body paragraph, jot: POINT + a SPECIFIC detail FROM the passage + what it EXPLAINS. Then build the paragraph from that.
STEP 5 Draft the five paragraphs: intro with thesis, three body paragraphs, conclusion. You already do this well.
STEP 6 Revise. Check every connector word, run one spelling sweep, and confirm each body paragraph names a real passage detail.
WHY STEP 4 IS THE GAME-CHANGER
Most of your lost essay points come from evidence that is invented or vague. If you plan each body paragraph as POINT + SPECIFIC EVIDENCE FROM THE PASSAGE + EXPLANATION before you draft, the specific evidence is already locked in. You will never reach a body paragraph and have to make something up.

Connector Logic

The one multiple choice point that got away — Q3

Cole, you got 9 of 10 multiple choice questions right. The single miss was Q3, where you had to combine two ideas with the right connecting word. This is a quick fix once you see the logic.

YOU CHOSE (Q3)
"Animated movies are fun because they include valuable lessons."

The word because says one thing causes the other — that the movies are fun because of the lessons. But that is not the relationship. The movies being fun and the movies teaching lessons are two separate true things that happen together. The correct connector shows contrast or addition, not cause:

CORRECT
"Animated movies are fun but they include valuable lessons." — but signals two things are true at once (fun, AND also educational), which is exactly the relationship here.
The Connector Framework
BECAUSE Cause. The second idea is the reason for the first. ("I stayed in because it was raining.")
BUT Contrast. The two ideas are both true but pull in different directions. ("It was raining but I went out.")
AND Addition. The second idea simply adds to the first. ("It was raining and it was cold.")
SO Result. The second idea is the outcome of the first. ("It was raining so I stayed in.")
Which connector correctly joins: "The movie is exciting ___ it also teaches honesty"?
A The movie is exciting so it also teaches honesty.
B The movie is exciting and it also teaches honesty.
THE TEST FOR YOURSELF
Before you pick a connector, ask: does the second idea CAUSE the first, RESULT from it, CONTRAST with it, or just ADD to it? Match the word to the relationship. On Q3, both ideas were simply true together — that is addition or contrast, never "because."
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
This is the only multiple choice point you left behind. Naming the relationship before you choose the word would have given you a clean 10 of 10. Same habit pays off in your essay, where connectors hold your sentences together.

REBUILT BODY PARAGRAPH (full-marks level)

Animated movies also teach kids honesty and kindness through the characters they follow. For example, the passage points to films like Brave, where Merida learns that courage means facing her fears instead of running from them. This shows that an animated story can model a real life lesson by letting kids watch a character struggle with a choice and make the right one. When children see a character they love act bravely or honestly, they remember the lesson long after the movie ends. That is how a fun film quietly teaches.

The Evidence Formula for Every Body Paragraph
POINT State the lesson this paragraph is about (kindness, bravery, honesty).
EVIDENCE Name a specific detail from the passage (a real film or character it mentions).
EXPLAIN "This shows..." — say what that detail proves about your point.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Your essay scored 15 of 20. The single biggest lever is swapping each invented example for a real passage detail and explaining it. That alone is worth several points, and you already write the "This shows..." sentence — just point it at real evidence.
