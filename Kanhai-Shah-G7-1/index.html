Q2: The Four Sentence Types

Declarative, Interrogative, Imperative, Exclamatory — know all four cold

On Q2 you were asked to identify the type of this sentence: "Some residents worried that the panels would not work during cloudy days."

YOU PICKED
Imperative.

That sentence is not giving a command — it is stating a fact about what some residents worried about. The correct answer was declarative. The test is that simple, but it relies on having the four sentence types locked in. Here they are side by side:

Declarative
States a fact or idea. Ends in a period.
The solar panels powered every home in the village.
Interrogative
Asks a question. Ends in a question mark.
Did the solar panels work on cloudy days?
Imperative
Commands or requests. Often starts with a verb, subject is "you."
Turn off the lights before you leave.
Exclamatory
Shows strong feeling. Ends in an exclamation point.
What an incredible change this brought!
🔍
The fast test. Ask yourself: Is there a subject and a verb stating something? If yes, and it ends in a period — it's declarative. Imperative always tells someone to do something. "Some residents worried..." has a clear subject ("residents") and is telling you a fact, so it's declarative.
Which sentence is imperative?
A The villagers gathered to watch the panels being installed.
B Read the instructions before turning on the panel.
C What a bright idea the engineers had!
KANHAI'S RULE FOR Q2-STYLE QUESTIONS
When a sentence starts with a subject + verb describing what someone thought, felt, said, or did — it is almost always declarative. Imperative sentences drop the subject because the subject is always "you." If you can put "You," in front of the sentence and it still makes sense, it is imperative. If not, it isn't.

"You, some residents worried..." — doesn't work. Declarative.
"You, read the instructions..." — works. Imperative.

Q3 & Q7: Reading the Prompt's Logic

Two misses, one root cause — matching the answer to the exact ask

Kanhai, Q3 and Q7 are different questions with the same underlying mistake: picking an option that is technically correct instead of the one that best matches what the prompt is asking for. Walk through both.

Q3 — Combine the two sentences logically
PROMPT "Children could study in the evenings." + "They could see better."
YOUR PICK "Children could study in the evenings; they could see better."
CORRECT "Because they could see better, children could study in the evenings."

A semicolon is grammatically fine — but the prompt asked for the most logical combination. The two ideas have a cause-and-effect relationship: being able to see better is why they could study. A semicolon just glues the ideas together in the order you read them. "Because..." names the relationship and puts cause before effect — which is what a logical combination does.

🔗
The "why are these two ideas next to each other?" test. When a prompt asks you to combine two sentences, first ask: is there a cause, a contrast, a time order, or a condition linking them? If yes, use the conjunction that names it (because, although, when, if). Only default to "and" or a semicolon when the ideas are truly parallel.
Q7 — Pick the supporting detail for "Benefits for local businesses"
YOUR PICK "Businesses could stay open for longer."
CORRECT "The extra hours gave customers more time to shop."

This one is a topic-sentence-vs-supporting-detail trap. "Businesses could stay open for longer" is a topic sentence itself — it is the general claim the paragraph makes. "The extra hours gave customers more time to shop" is the specific consequence that proves why longer hours mattered. That is what a supporting detail does: it explains or proves the topic sentence.

Topic Sentence vs. Supporting Detail
GENERAL Topic sentence — the main claim ("Businesses benefited.")
SPECIFIC Supporting detail — the why/how ("More shopping hours meant more sales.")
A paragraph's topic sentence is "Solar panels improved village safety at night." Which is the strongest supporting detail?
A The village became much safer after the panels were installed.
B Street lights powered by the panels cut nighttime road accidents nearly in half.
C Solar panels produce clean energy without burning fuel.
THE "DOES IT SAY HOW?" TEST
When a question asks for a supporting detail, ask: does this sentence tell me HOW or WHY the claim is true?

✗ A restatement of the claim in different words = still a topic sentence.
✓ A specific outcome, number, example, or cause = supporting detail.

The supporting detail always adds information that the topic sentence doesn't already have on its own.

Second — and this is the bigger point lever — each of your body paragraphs names the benefit and gives a detail, but doesn't push into what that benefit reveals about the village. That is the "layer-2" analysis sentence. Here is what adding one per body paragraph looks like:

Reveal: polished body paragraph (19-20/20 version)
See one layer-2 analysis sentence added — same evidence, more credit
BEFORE — YOUR BODY 1 (paraphrased)
"One benefit was that businesses could stay open for longer. The extra hours gave customers more time to shop. This helped the shop owners a lot."
AFTER — ONE LAYER-2 SENTENCE ADDED
"One benefit was that businesses could stay open for longer. The extra hours gave customers more time to shop, which meant shop owners saw more sales each evening. This shows how reliable electricity didn't just light up the village — it created a real economic ripple effect, giving families a steadier income and pulling money into the local economy."

What changed? One sentence — "This shows how..." — that tells the reader what the detail means for the bigger thesis. Do that in each of your three body paragraphs and the evidence/analysis row of the rubric goes from 4/5 to 5/5. That is the difference between 18/20 and 19-20/20.

LAYER-2 SENTENCE STARTERS
After your evidence sentence in each body paragraph, use one of these openers:

• "This shows how..."
• "This matters because..."
• "What this reveals is..."
• "In other words, the village gained..."

One of these per body paragraph — three sentences total — turns an 18/20 into a 19-20/20.
