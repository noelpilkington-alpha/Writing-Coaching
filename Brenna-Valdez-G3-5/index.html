Appositives — A Naming Phrase, Not "That"

Q4 — 1 out of 2
📝
What happened on Q4: You needed to add an appositive (a renaming phrase) but instead wrote a "that are..." clause. An appositive is a noun phrase set off by commas that renames the noun before it.
WHAT YOU WROTE (Q4)
"The books, that are known for their golden spines, are still popoular."

The problem: "that are known for their golden spines" is a relative clause (it starts with "that are"), not an appositive. An appositive is a short naming phrase that renames or describes the noun right next to it.

Relative Clause vs. Appositive
CLAUSE "The books, that are known for their golden spines, are still popular." (uses "that are")
APPOSITIVE "The books, a series known for their golden spines, are still popular." (a naming phrase)
THE RULE
An appositive is a noun phrase placed right next to another noun to rename or describe it. It does NOT start with "that," "which," or "who." It reads like a quick label you could drop in: "My dog, a golden retriever, loves to swim." The phrase "a golden retriever" is a noun phrase, not a clause.
Which sentence uses a true appositive (a naming phrase, not a clause)?
A Little Golden Books, that are loved by children, have been around for decades.
B Little Golden Books, a beloved children's series, have been around for decades.
C Little Golden Books, which are popular with children, have been around for decades.
QUICK TEST
When you write an appositive, check: "Does my inserted phrase start with 'that,' 'which,' or 'who'?" If yes, it's a clause, not an appositive. Rewrite it as a short noun phrase that renames the word before it.

Brenna, your paragraph has the right idea. You picked up on the key topic (Jason's underwater statues). To move from 16/20 to 18 or 19/20, each detail needs two parts: what the passage says (a specific fact) and what it shows or why it matters (the explanation).

The Detail + Explanation Pattern
DETAIL A specific fact from the passage (names, numbers, what happened, what something looks like).
EXPLAIN Why it matters or what it shows about the main idea. This is the sentence most students leave out.
EXAMPLE USING THE PASSAGE
Detail: "Jason deCaires Taylor placed life-sized cement statues on the ocean floor near Cancun, Mexico."
Explanation: "This created an underwater museum where divers can swim through art, and the statues also help coral grow on them over time."

See how the explanation connects the detail back to why the statues matter? Without it, the reader just has a fact but doesn't understand why it's important.
Which version of this paragraph detail is more developed?
A Jason built two very well done statues in the Caribbean sea. They are really cool and interesting to see.
B Jason deCaires Taylor placed life-sized cement statues on the ocean floor near Cancun. Over time, coral grows on the statues, which means his art actually helps the ocean environment while giving divers a one-of-a-kind museum to explore.
YOUR DEVELOPMENT CHECK
After writing each detail sentence, ask: "Did I explain what this shows or why it matters?" If your sentence just states a fact without connecting it to the main idea, add one more sentence that explains. The pattern is always: passage detail → explanation of why it matters.
