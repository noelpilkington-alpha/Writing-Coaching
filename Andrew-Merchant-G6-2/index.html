Error #1 — Conjunction Logic

Q3 — 0 out of 1 — picking the word that matches the relationship between ideas

Andrew, you got 9 out of 10 multiple choice questions right. The one miss was Q3 — combining two sentences with a conjunction. This one is worth studying because the pattern comes up in essays too.

The prompt asked you to combine these two sentences: "Animated movies are fun. They also include valuable lessons."

You chose because. The correct answer was but.

Which conjunction correctly combines the two sentences?
A Animated movies are fun because they include valuable lessons.
B Animated movies are fun but they include valuable lessons.
THE CONJUNCTION DECISION TREE
Before you pick a conjunction, read the two sentences and ask: what is the actual relationship between them?

• Cause and effect? → because, so, therefore, as a result
• Contrast or surprise addition? → but, yet, however, although
• Both true together (no surprise)? → and, also, plus, in addition
• Condition? → if, when, unless, until

The grammar can be correct and the conjunction still wrong. Always name the relationship first, then pick the word that matches.
WHY THE Q3 TRAP WORKS
The wrong answer used "because," which sounds smart and grown-up. That is the trap. "Because" means one thing causes the other. Animated movies aren't fun BECAUSE they have lessons — they are fun AND they have lessons. Two separate good things, where the second is a bit unexpected.

Whenever you see "because" as an option, stop and ask: is one of these actually causing the other? If you can't finish the sentence "this happens because of that," then "because" is wrong.

Error #2 — Develop with Passage Evidence

Your body paragraphs need specific movie examples + explanation

Andrew, your essay opens strong — your thesis is clear and you set up the argument well. But in the body paragraphs, you make claims without always backing them up with a specific detail from the passage and then explaining what that detail shows. The rubric rewards essays that go beyond naming an idea and actually prove it with evidence.

WHAT A THIN BODY PARAGRAPH LOOKS LIKE
"Animated movies teach kids about problem-solving. Many animated movies show characters solving problems in creative ways. This helps kids learn to think creatively too."

This paragraph makes a claim (animated movies teach problem-solving) and restates it — but never names a specific movie, a specific character, or a specific moment. The grader reads it and thinks: "What movie? What happened? How does it teach that?"

THE DETAIL + EXPLANATION PATTERN
Every body paragraph needs this sequence:

1. Claim: State the skill or lesson animated movies teach.
2. Detail: Name a specific movie and a specific moment from the passage.
3. Explanation: Explain what that moment shows or why it matters.

The detail is the what. The explanation is the so what. You need both.
SAME IDEA, WITH DETAIL + EXPLANATION
"Animated movies teach kids about problem-solving. [Detail] In Finding Nemo, Marlin has to figure out how to cross an entire ocean to rescue his son, navigating jellyfish fields and escaping a shark. [Explanation] This shows kids that big problems can be broken into smaller steps, and that persistence matters even when the situation feels impossible."

The passage "The Power of Animated Movies for Children" names specific films and specific lessons. Your essay body paragraphs should pull those details in and then add your own sentence explaining what each detail proves.

Which body paragraph uses the Detail + Explanation pattern correctly?
A Animated movies teach kids to be brave. In Brave, the main character has to face a scary situation. This teaches kids to be brave in their own lives too.
B Animated movies teach kids to be brave. In Brave, Merida must break the spell on her mother before sunrise, which means facing a bear and admitting that her own stubbornness caused the problem. This shows kids that bravery is not just about fighting — it is about owning your mistakes and fixing them, even when it is scary.
A QUICK CHECK BEFORE YOU MOVE ON
After writing each body paragraph, ask yourself two questions:

• Did I name a specific movie and a specific moment? (Detail)
• Did I write a sentence that starts with "This shows..." or "This means..."? (Explanation)

If either answer is no, add a sentence before moving to the next paragraph. One detail + one explanation is worth more than three vague claims.

Proofread Pass — Catch the Spelling Slips

Small errors add up on the mechanics row of the rubric

Andrew, your essay had some spelling errors that cost points on the mechanics row. These are not ideas problems — they are fingers-moved-faster-than-eyes problems. A short proofread pass at the end catches most of them.

SPELLING SLIPS FROM YOUR G6.2 ESSAY
"Animated movies are often viewed as simple entertainment for children, filled with exting themes and catchy songs" (should be: exciting)

When your brain is focused on getting ideas down, it skips over letter-level errors. That is normal. The fix is a separate pass at the end — one where you are only looking at spelling, not ideas.

THE BACKWARDS-SENTENCE TRICK
When you proofread forward, your brain auto-fills what it expected to see, not what is actually on the page. That is why "exting" slipped through.

Read each sentence starting from the last sentence and working backward. Not word-by-word backward — sentence-by-sentence. Your brain has to process each one on its own, without the story flow carrying you past errors. Spelling catches jump from about 50% to about 90% with this one trick.
Which version has caught ALL the spelling errors?
A Animated movies are often viewed as simple entertainment for children, filled with exting themes and catchy songs. However, as the passage explains...
B Animated movies are often viewed as simple entertainment for children, filled with exciting themes and catchy songs. However, as the passage explains...
THE COST OF SKIPPED PROOFREADING
Every 2–3 spelling errors on a G6 essay costs 1 point on the mechanics row of the rubric. A 4-minute backward-read catches most of them. Build the habit: finish drafting, then switch your brain into "spelling only" mode and read backward through the essay sentence by sentence.
