Comma Splices — The Cross-Grade Pattern

Same issue on G3.2, G3.3, and G4.1 — let's end it here
🔍 Beau, this exact error has appeared on three tests in a row — G3.2, G3.3, and now twice on G4.1 (Question 1 and Question 10). It's quietly costing you 2–3 points per test. Nailing this one rule closes that gap for good.

A comma splice is when you take two complete sentences and join them with just a comma. A comma isn't strong enough to do that job. Here's what it looked like on G4.1:

FROM QUESTION 1 — COMBINE SENTENCES (1 of 2 points)
"In this event, Science teacher Peter Tabichi received an award, This award was the prestigous Global Prize."
("In this event, Science teacher Peter Tabichi received an award" is a complete sentence. "This award was the prestigious Global Prize" is also a complete sentence. A comma can't hold them together.)
FROM QUESTION 10 — SENTENCE FROM PROMPT (2 of 3 points)
"A skill I have that can help others is that I can make art, I can sell this art and then give them some of my money."
(Same exact pattern. Two full sentences stuck together with only a comma.)
FROM G3.2 AND G3.3 (EARLIER TESTS)
"Fans tosses bears onto the ice, After the Bears scored a goal."
"Anna and Jim ran to the beach,when they got there..."
(Same bug, different tests. This is a habit — and habits fix with one clear rule.)
The One-Rule Fix
YES Use a period to split them into two sentences.
"...received an award. This award was the Global Prize."
YES Use a conjunction after the comma (and, but, so, because).
"I can make art, and I can sell it to help them."
YES Use a semicolon if the two sentences are closely connected.
"He won an award; it was the Global Prize."
NO A bare comma between two complete sentences. This is the comma splice — the exact error to avoid.
Which option fixes the comma splice: "I finished my homework, I went outside to play."
A I finished my homework, then I went outside to play.
B I finished my homework, and I went outside to play.

SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
You've had a comma splice on three tests in a row. One simple rule closes it: between two complete sentences, never use just a comma. Use a period, a conjunction, or a semicolon. That's 2–3 points per test you've been leaving on the table.

Your Question 11 is already strong — here's the Evidence Sandwich

The good news: every one of those is a proofreading fix, not a writing-skill fix. Your analysis is already at 19/20 level. Let's also sharpen how you weave evidence in — that's the other point.

The Evidence Sandwich (for every body paragraph)
TOP Set up the evidence — one sentence introducing what the passage shows.
MEAT Use the evidence — quote a short phrase OR paraphrase a moment from the text.
BOTTOM Explain the evidence — one sentence saying what it tells us about the character or idea.
Quoting — DO and DON'T
DO Quote a short phrase (2–6 words) inside your own sentence. The passage says Cody was "doging difenders," which shows his quickness.
DON'T Quote a whole sentence on its own. That's a drop-quote — it doesn't let you show your thinking.
DON'T Quote every piece of evidence — mix paraphrase and quote. Overquoting feels like copying.
Paraphrasing — DO and DON'T
DO Retell a moment in your own words and name what happened specifically. When Cody dodged the last defender, he scored the winning goal.
DON'T Be vague ("something happened," "it was cool"). Paraphrase must be specific to count as evidence.
DON'T Just copy the passage with one word changed. That's not paraphrasing — that's dressing up a quote.
You're writing the supporting detail on "Cody was a clutch player." Which sentence is the stronger Evidence Sandwich?
A Cody was really clutch. He always came through when his team needed him the most.
B Cody showed his clutch side late in the game. After dodging defenders and getting past the keeper with seconds left, he scored the winning goal — proof he could stay calm when it mattered most.

Appositive Grammar — 'A' vs 'An'

Question 3 on G4.1 — one sound-based rule
💡 Beau, your appositive on Question 3 was 1.5 out of 2 — structurally correct, just tripped up by 'a' vs 'an'. This is a one-sound rule you can lock in today.
FROM QUESTION 3 — WRITE AN APPOSITIVE (1.5 of 2 points)
"Peter Tabichi, a award wining teacher, is from Kenya, Africa, and teaches high school science."
(The appositive structure is right — noun phrase set off by commas that renames Peter Tabichi. Just two polish issues: 'a' should be 'an', and 'wining' should be 'winning'.)
The 'A' vs 'An' Rule (it's about SOUND, not letters)
AN Use an before a word that starts with a vowel sound: an award, an apple, an hour (even though 'hour' starts with h, it sounds like 'our').
A Use a before a word that starts with a consonant sound: a teacher, a book, a university (even though 'university' starts with u, it sounds like 'yoo').
