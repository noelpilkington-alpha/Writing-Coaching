Run-On Sentences

Q8 — Three ideas jammed together

On Q8, you had three complete ideas but no punctuation to separate them:

YOU WROTE
"One of the unique plants I saw had many leaves it kind of looked like a fern leaf it interested me."

This is a run-on sentence. Three separate thoughts are mashed together with no periods, commas, or conjunctions to show where one ends and the next begins.

Tap to see the fixed version
Read the explanation above first
FIXED
"One of the unique plants I saw had many leaves. It kind of looked like a fern leaf, and it interested me."
RULE: ONE COMPLETE THOUGHT AT A TIME
If a sentence has more than one complete thought, use one of these:

1. A period to split them into two sentences.
2. A comma + conjunction (and, but, so) to connect them.
3. A semicolon (;) if the ideas are closely related.

Quick test: Count the verbs. If your sentence has two or three main verbs with separate subjects, you probably need to break it up.

Commas in Lists & After Transitions

Q11 — Small fixes that make your paragraph shine

Your paragraph about robots was solid — good structure and real details from the passage. But a couple of comma rules would make it even stronger.

Rule 1: Commas in a list of three or more

YOU WROTE
"...simple things including pencils markers and many other things"
Tap to see the fixed version
Read the rule above first
FIXED

Rule 2: Comma after transition words

YOU WROTE
"First they can help..."  /  "Secondly robots can help..."  /  "Lastly an exoskeleton..."
Tap to see the fixed versions
Transition words need a comma right after them
FIXED
"First, they can help..."
"Secondly, robots can help..."
"Lastly, an exoskeleton..."
TWO COMMA RULES TO REMEMBER
Lists: When you write three or more items in a row, put a comma between each one: "apples, oranges, and bananas."

Transitions: When a sentence starts with a transition word (First, Next, Also, Finally, However), put a comma right after it: "First, ..."
