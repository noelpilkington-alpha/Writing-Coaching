THE READ-ALOUD TEST
Before you submit any written answer, read the sentence aloud in your head slowly — word by word. Your ear is trained to hear natural English. When a small word like "a," "the," or "to" is missing, the sentence sounds off. Trust that instinct.

2. If something sounds choppy or incomplete, look for a missing small word.
3. Check names against the passage — "Kessler" not "Keller."
4. Scan for doubled or jumbled words ("its was" → "it was").

Keep Your Sentences Tight

Q6 — 2 out of 3 — good storytelling, but one sentence does too much
🔍
What happened on Q6: You told a vivid personal story — the grader could picture it. But you chained so many ideas together with "and" and "and then" that one sentence tried to carry three separate events. When a sentence holds too many clauses, it becomes a run-on.
G5.1 Q6 — RUN-ON (SCORED 2 OUT OF 3)
You wrote: "Once I was walking with a glass cup of water, and then I dropped the water and instead of picking it up, I ran away. Later, I felt guilty so I went to..."
Count the events chained into one sentence: (1) walking with a glass, (2) dropped the water, (3) instead of picking it up, (4) ran away. That is four ideas joined by "and" and commas. Each event deserves its own sentence or at most one clean pair.
CLEANER VERSION — ONE IDEA PER SENTENCE
✓ "Once I was walking with a glass cup of water. I dropped it, and instead of picking it up, I ran away. Later, I felt guilty, so I went back to clean it up."

Same story, same voice. The difference: a period after the first event breaks the chain. Now each sentence carries one or two ideas — not four.
Which version keeps the story clear without chaining too many ideas?
A Once I was walking with a glass cup of water and then I dropped it and instead of picking it up I ran away.
B Once I was walking with a glass cup of water. I dropped it, and instead of picking it up, I ran away.
THE "AND" COUNT RULE
If your sentence has two or more "and" connectors (or "and then"), it is probably doing too much. Find the natural break point and put a period there.

Too many: "I was walking and I dropped it and I ran away."
Just right: "I was walking with a glass of water. I dropped it and ran away."

One "and" per sentence is usually the sweet spot at G5 level.
YOUR STORYTELLING IS A STRENGTH
Emilio, the content of your Q6 answer is genuinely good. The grader could picture the scene — the glass cup, the panic, the guilt. That kind of vivid detail is hard to teach. All you need is the structural habit of breaking long chains into shorter sentences so the story breathes.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q6 was worth 3 points and you earned 2. That single run-on cost you 1 point. On a 25-point section, 1 point is 4 percentage points. Break the chain, keep the story — free point.
