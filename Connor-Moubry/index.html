1 Answer the Question

Your first sentence answers the question. Don’t describe the passage—go straight to the answer.

Question Flip: Take the question, flip it around, add "because," then finish the sentence from your head. Don’t look at the passage.

Your Sam’s Breakfast question: Why is breakfast important for Sam?

✗ Too vague: "Because it is a healthy meal Sam almost always has oatmeal."
(This was your opening sentence — it doesn’t clearly answer why breakfast is important.)
✓ Answers the question: "Breakfast is important for Sam because it gives him energy for school."
Quick check: Read your first sentence. Does it answer the question with "because"? If not, rewrite it.
2 Pick 2–3 DIFFERENT Details

Each detail sentence gives a new fact. If two sentences say the same thing, that only counts as one.

Here is what happened in your Sam’s Breakfast paragraph:

✗ Your sentence 1: "...so he can have an amazing day."
✗ Your sentence 2: "...so you can have better grades."
✗ Your sentence 3: "...so he can have an astonishing day."

"Amazing day," "better grades," and "astonishing day" all say the same thing: breakfast makes your day better. That is one idea, not three.

Find a fact about three different things:
A thing → "Sam eats oatmeal with blueberries."
A person → "His mom adds fruit or toast with peanut butter."
A feeling or reason → "It gives Sam energy so he can focus at school."
After each sentence, ask: "Did I already say this?" If yes → cross it out and find a new fact.
3 Use Your Own Words

Swapping one or two words is still copying. The grader calls it "synonym swapping" and it does not count.

From the Sam’s Breakfast passage and your paragraph:

✗ Passage: "light and fluffy instead of flat and dense"
✗ You wrote: "fluffy and light instead of dense and dry"
(You rearranged the words, but the sentence still has the same shape.)
Close and Tell:
1. Read the fact in the passage.
2. Cover the passage with your hand.
3. Say it out loud like you’re telling a friend. Write that down.
✓ Your own words: "Eating breakfast makes Sam feel full and ready to go."

It doesn’t have to sound fancy. It just has to sound like you.

Then, in a second sentence, add "because" or "this shows" to explain why it matters:

✓ Add why: "This is important because without breakfast Sam would be tired and hungry all morning."
4 Write a Closing Sentence

Your last sentence wraps everything up. It should NOT just repeat your first sentence with different words.

From your Sam’s Breakfast paragraph:

✗ Just repeating: "Sam always has oatmeal so he can have an astonishing day."
(This says the same thing as your other sentences — it doesn’t wrap up.)
Start your closing with: "This is why..." or "That is what makes..."
✓ Simple closing: "This is why Sam’s mom makes sure he eats a good breakfast every morning."
✓ Even better: "This is why breakfast is so important for Sam. It gives him the energy he needs to do his best."
Tip: Look at your 2–3 details. Can you put them together? If you talked about the food, his mom, and energy, your closing could mention all three.

💡 What If I’m Stuck?

If you don’t know how to start:

Use the Question Flip. Write the question words, add "because," and finish with the first thing that comes to mind. You can fix it later.

If the grader keeps saying "copied":

Do not look at the passage while you write. Read it first. Then cover it up. Write your whole paragraph from memory. It does not have to be perfect—it just has to be yours.

If you tried once and it did not pass:

That is normal! Here is what to do:

1. Read the feedback notes. Find the one or two things the grader marked.

2. Fix just those things. Don’t rewrite everything.

3. Check for repeats one more time.

4. Submit again.

Remember: Your Sam’s Breakfast paragraph scored 9 out of 15. You are close to the 12 you need. The grader said to find different details and fix your commas. Fix those two things and you could pass on your next try.
