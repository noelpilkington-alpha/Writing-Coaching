Read the Connector Requirement (Q5)

When the prompt names the words, use THOSE words

Cristobal, Q5 on this test asked you to combine two sentences using because, but, or so. Your combine kept all the meaning and all the detail — but you used and instead of one of the three required connector words. That is why Q5 came in at 0.5 out of 1.

YOU WROTE (Q5)
"His dedication has tramsformed the school, and due to his efforts, enrollment has doubled, and more studentts are now attending college."
Three things to notice: the connector word (“and”) wasn't on the menu the prompt gave you, and two spelling slips (“transformed” and “students”) slipped through.
THE RULE
When a Q5 prompt says "combine these sentences using because, but, or so," those are the only three connector words that earn credit. Think of the prompt as giving you a menu — you have to pick from the menu.

because — shows a reason (He won the award because his dedication transformed the school.)
but — shows a contrast (The school had problems but Tabichi changed that.)
so — shows a result (His dedication transformed the school, so enrollment doubled.)
A BETTER COMBINE (CRISTOBAL'S IDEAS, CORRECT CONNECTOR)
"His dedication has transformed the school, so enrollment has doubled and more students are now attending college."
Same meaning, same detail. “So” is from the menu (because/but/so) and it correctly shows the result — his dedication caused the enrollment jump.
The Q5 prompt says: Combine these sentences using because.
Which combine earns full credit?
A Peter Tabichi won the Global Teacher Prize, and his dedication has transformed the school.
B Peter Tabichi won the Global Teacher Prize because his dedication has transformed the school.
THE PROMPT-READING HABIT
Before you write a Q5 combine, find the connector words in the prompt first. If the prompt says because, but, or so, write one of those three words into your combine. If the prompt says only because, use because. Matching the prompt word is half the grade — and it's a one-second move.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q5 was worth 1 point and came in at 0.5. Reading the connector word carefully is a single-second habit that recovers that half point — and the same habit protects you on every future combine question, on every test.

Pick a Specific Lesson (Q7)

When the prompt asks for a lesson, name one from the story

Cristobal, Q7 asked: What lesson from Peter Tabichi's story applies to your own life? You gave a one-line response — but the phrase you picked doesn't quite land. That is why Q7 came in at 0.33 out of 1.

YOU WROTE (Q7)
"It shows how we can work hard to overcome our dreams."
Two issues: “overcome our dreams” doesn't work — you don't overcome a dream, you pursue it or achieve it. And the sentence doesn't name a specific lesson from Tabichi's story.
THE TWO-PART RULE
When a prompt asks about a lesson from the story, do two things:

1. Pick a real lesson from the passage. Peter Tabichi's story has several: dedication pays off, teachers can change a whole community, hard work helps students from poor backgrounds, giving to others matters more than winning the prize.

2. Use precise words. You achieve a dream, reach a goal, overcome an obstacle. Picking the right verb for the noun is a G4 polish move.
A BETTER RESPONSE (CRISTOBAL'S STYLE, SPECIFIC LESSON)
"Peter Tabichi's story shows that hard work and dedication can help us achieve our dreams, even when things are hard."
Same idea, better verb (“achieve” fits with “dreams”), and it names a specific lesson from the story (hard work + dedication).
ANOTHER STRONG VERSION
"The lesson I learned is that dedication can change lives — Peter Tabichi's effort doubled his school's enrollment, and I can use that same dedication in my studies."
This one pulls a specific detail from the passage (enrollment doubled) and connects it to the student's own life. That's exactly what Q7 is asking for.

Before you move on from a "lesson" or "opinion" question, read your answer and ask:

2. Do my verbs and nouns match? You achieve dreams, reach goals, overcome challenges. If the pair sounds off, swap the verb.
