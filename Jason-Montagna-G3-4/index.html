Original: "Layla looked at the flower."

The key word is changing the verb. You needed to swap out "looked" for a stronger verb.

Which rewrite actually changes the verb?
A Layla, a curious girl, looked at the beautiful flower.
B Layla gazed at the flower.
YOU WROTE From G3.4 Q1

You added nice descriptive details ("a curious girl," "beautiful"), but the verb "looked" did not change. The instruction was specifically "change the verb," so the graders could not give full credit even though your sentence sounds better.

ONE WAY TO CHANGE THE VERB

THE RULE: REPLACE, DON'T ADD
When a prompt says "change the verb", your job is to swap the verb for a better one. Find the verb in the original, cross it out, and put a stronger verb in its place. Adding adjectives ("beautiful," "curious") does not count as changing the verb.

Question 2 Fix — Where the Comma Goes with "Since"

Great conjunction choice — tricky comma placement.

You scored 1/2 on this question.

The instruction was: "Combine the following sentences using the subordinating conjunction since."

1. Layla saw a butterfly.
2. She took out her camera.
Which sentence puts the comma in the right place?
A Since, Layla saw a butterfly, she took out her camera.
B Since Layla saw a butterfly, she took out her camera.
YOU WROTE From G3.4 Q2
"Since, Layla saw a butterfly, she took out her camera."

You picked the right word — "Since" combines the two sentences perfectly. But the comma landed in the wrong spot. A comma right after "Since" cuts the word off from the clause it belongs to.

CORRECT VERSION
"Since Layla saw a butterfly, she took out her camera."
One comma — placed at the end of the opening part, right before the main idea starts.
THE RULE: COMMA AT THE END OF THE OPENING, NOT AFTER THE FIRST WORD
When a sentence starts with Since, Because, When, After, Before, Although, If (or another subordinator), the comma goes at the end of the opening clause, not right after the first word.

Think of it as two parts:
• Opening part (with Since/Because/When) — no comma inside it
• Comma — goes at the very end of the opening
• Main idea — comes after the comma
Pattern to Memorize
OPENING Since Layla saw a butterfly
COMMA ,
MAIN she took out her camera.
QUICK CHECK BEFORE YOU FINISH
Read your sentence and ask: "Where does the opening idea stop and the main idea start?" That is where your comma goes. If you find yourself putting a comma right after the first word (Since, Because, When), pause — that is almost always wrong.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
You chose the right conjunction and built the right structure — you just lost 1 point on the comma. This is a 10-second fix and a common question on tests. Lock it in now and it becomes free points going forward.

Question 8 Fix — Comma Splices in Long Sentences

Strong content — two complete thoughts need more than just a comma.

You scored 3/3 on this question — nice work! But the sentence has a comma splice worth fixing before it costs points on a harder test.

The question asked what you thought was the most important moment in the story.

Which version joins the two thoughts correctly?
A ...Layla fights the urge to take the geode by showing it to the guide, this shows that no matter how bad Layla might want something she always follows the rules.
B ...Layla fights the urge to take the geode by showing it to the guide. This shows that no matter how bad Layla might want something, she always follows the rules.
YOU WROTE From G3.4 Q8
"I think the most important moment in the stroy is when Layla fights the urge to take the geode by showing it to the guide, this shows that no matter how bad Layla might want something she always follows the rules."

Your idea here is excellent — you identified the key moment and explained why it matters (Layla always follows the rules). The problem is the comma in the middle: after "to the guide," a second complete thought starts ("this shows..."). Two complete thoughts joined by just a comma is called a comma splice.

WHAT IS A COMMA SPLICE?
A comma splice is when you join two complete thoughts with only a comma. Each half could stand on its own as a sentence:

• "Layla fights the urge to take the geode by showing it to the guide." ← complete sentence
• "This shows that... she always follows the rules." ← also a complete sentence

A comma alone is not strong enough glue.
THREE WAYS TO FIX IT
1. Use a period. Split into two sentences.
• "...showing it to the guide. This shows that..."

2. Use a FANBOYS conjunction + comma (and, but, so, for, or, yet, nor).
• "...showing it to the guide, and this shows that..."

3. Use a subordinating conjunction (which, because, since).
• "...showing it to the guide, which shows that..."
ONE CLEAN FIX
"I think the most important moment in the story is when Layla fights the urge to take the geode by showing it to the guide. This shows that no matter how bad Layla might want something, she always follows the rules."
HOW TO CATCH COMMA SPLICES
Read your sentence out loud. When you hit a comma, ask: "Could both halves stand alone as full sentences?"
If yes → you need a period, a FANBOYS conjunction, or a subordinator — not just a comma.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
This did not cost you points this time — but on G4 and beyond, graders will start marking comma splices. Fixing the habit now means your long, thoughtful answers stay worth the full credit they deserve.

Question 10 Fix — Answer Every Part of the Prompt

When a prompt asks two things, your answer needs two parts.

You scored 2/3 on this question.

The prompt was: "What is one rule people should follow when visiting natural places? Why is it important?"

That is two questions in one prompt:

  • Part 1: What is one rule?
  • Part 2: Why is it important?
Which answer handles both parts of the prompt?
A One rule people should follow is "Take only pictures, leave only footprints."
B One rule people should follow is "Take only pictures, leave only footprints." This rule is important because it helps protect nature so other people can enjoy it too.
YOU WROTE From G3.4 Q10
"One rule people should follow is "Take only pictures, leave only footprints.""

The rule you picked is a great one — straight from the passage. But the prompt had two parts: the rule AND why it is important. You answered Part 1 but stopped before Part 2. That is why you got 2/3 instead of 3/3.

ONE WAY TO COMPLETE BOTH PARTS
"One rule people should follow is "Take only pictures, leave only footprints." This rule is important because it helps protect nature so other people can enjoy it too, and it keeps plants and animals safe from harm."
THE HABIT: COUNT THE QUESTION MARKS
Before you start writing, underline every question mark in the prompt. Then count them.
• One question mark → one answer
• Two question marks → two answers

Also watch for the words "and", "why", or "explain" tacked on at the end — those are signals that more is expected.
SENTENCE STARTERS THAT COVER BOTH PARTS
When you see a two-part prompt, use a structure like:
• "[Answer to Part 1]. This is important because [Part 2]."
• "[Answer to Part 1]. The reason this matters is [Part 2]."
• "[Answer to Part 1], which is important because [Part 2]."
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
You lost 1 full point here just for stopping early. The ideas were there — you know why the rule matters. Adding one more sentence ("This is important because...") turns 2/3 into 3/3 every time.
