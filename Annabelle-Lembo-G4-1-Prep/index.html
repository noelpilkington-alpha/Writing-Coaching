Your 6-Step Game Plan

Your test-day routine — the same process every time

Annabelle, this is the process that got you to 91% on G3.6. Use it again on G4.1 — same steps, same order. The process protects you from the small mistakes that cost a point here and there.

STEP 1: READ THE PASSAGE AND QUESTIONS TWICE
First read: understand the big picture. Second read: notice specific details, names, and facts you can use in your answers. The second read matters even more at G4 because the passages are longer — you will miss details on the first pass.
STEP 2: IDENTIFY THE SKILL EACH QUESTION IS TESTING
Before writing, ask: "Is this asking me to fix a fragment, combine sentences, write a response, or build a paragraph?" Knowing what the grader is looking for keeps you from answering the wrong question.
STEP 3: PLAN YOUR ANSWER
For Q1–Q5: figure out what needs fixing before you rewrite. For Q6–Q10: pick your specific reason or example FIRST, then write the sentence around it. For Q11: outline your paragraph in the scratchpad before drafting.
STEP 4: WRITE
Write clear, complete sentences. When two complete ideas belong together, join them smoothly with a connecting word like which, and, so, or because instead of letting them collide. Use specific details from the passage, not vague language.
STEP 5: PROOFREAD — READ EVERY WORD
After writing, read your answer back slowly, word by word. Check spelling on the tricky words, check that each sentence ends with a period, and check that your verbs match your subjects. This is your single biggest point-saver — the next card explains why.
STEP 6: PLAN Q11 BEFORE YOU DRAFT
Use the scratchpad to plan your paragraph: topic sentence, two or three details with an explanation after each, and a closing sentence. Then draft from your plan. This is exactly what earned you 20/20 on G3.6.

Lock In: Proofread Your Spelling

Your #1 point-saver — the easiest points to win back on G4.1

This is your single biggest opportunity. On G3.6 your ideas and sentences were strong, but a few words got misspelled on words you already know — "quills," "because," "from," and "their." None of these are hard for you. They slip through because the read-back step gets skipped. Catch them, and you protect the score.
G3.6 — SPELLING SLIPS ON WORDS YOU KNOW
You wrote: "A porcupine lifts its quils when it feels scared, becuase they make a warning sound."
You wrote: "Do sharp quills protect them form danger?"
You wrote: "I think porcupines do not make a good pet, because they can throw there quills."
Four small slips: quils → quills, becuase → because, form → from, there → their. Every one is a word you can spell — they just need a second look.
CORRECTED — CLEAN SPELLING
✓ "A porcupine lifts its quills when it feels scared, because they make a warning sound."
✓ "Do sharp quills protect them from danger?"
✓ "...they can throw their quills."

Same great sentences — just cleaned up. That is points back for almost no effort.
THE "POINT AT EACH WORD" TRICK
When you proofread, go slow and look at one word at a time. Two words to watch especially:

"from" vs "form" — "from" means coming out of something. "Form" means a shape. Read the sentence and ask which one you mean.

"there" vs "their" — "their" means it belongs to them ("their quills"). "There" means a place. If you can say "belongs to them," use their.

Reading word by word feels slow, but it takes under a minute and it catches every one of these.

B The owl hunts at night because it can see in the dark.
YOUR PROOFREAD STEP FOR THIS
On G4.1, after writing every answer, do one slow read-back pointing at each word. You are not looking for big mistakes — you are looking for the small spelling slips on words you already know. That habit alone defends the score you earned.

Watch: Connect Your Ideas

G3.6 Q9 — two complete thoughts collided; join them with a connecting word
🔍
What happened on G3.6 Q9: you packed two complete thoughts into one sentence and they collided with nothing connecting them. When two full ideas run together, the strongest fix is to join them with a connecting word like which, and, so, or because. That turns a run-on into one smooth sentence — exactly the kind of more complex sentence G4 rewards.
G3.6 Q9 — TWO IDEAS COLLIDED
You wrote: "Porcupines are special, because they have strong claws and rough paws this helps them hold on to tree branches tightly."
"...strong claws and rough paws" is one complete idea. "This helps them hold on to tree branches tightly" is a second complete idea. They got fused together with no connecting word linking them.
CORRECTED — JOINED WITH A CONNECTING WORD
✓ "Porcupines are special because they have strong claws and rough paws, which help them hold on to tree branches tightly."

The connecting word "which" links the two ideas into one smooth sentence. Nothing is chopped apart, and it reads as the stronger, more complex sentence G4 looks for.
THE "PICK A CONNECTING WORD" MOVE
When two complete ideas collide, do not just stack them — choose a connecting word to link them into one strong sentence:

which / that — adds more about the thing you just named
and — adds an equal second idea
so — shows a result
because — shows a reason

One connecting word turns two colliding ideas into one smooth, complete sentence.
Which version fixes the run-on?
A The fox has thick fur it keeps the fox warm in winter.
B The fox has thick fur, which keeps it warm in winter.
YOUR PROOFREAD STEP FOR THIS
On G4.1, whenever two complete ideas collide in one sentence, pick a connecting word (which, and, so, because) to join them smoothly. This takes 5 seconds and turns a run-on into the kind of strong, complete sentence the G4 grader rewards.

Your Paragraph Strategy for G4.1

You earned a perfect 20/20 on G3.6 — here is the exact recipe to repeat

Your winning paragraph recipe
Open One topic sentence that answers the question directly and names your main idea.
Detail 1 A fact from the passage + "This shows that..." explanation.

[Topic sentence] The porcupine's body helps it stay safe in the wild using quills and claws.
