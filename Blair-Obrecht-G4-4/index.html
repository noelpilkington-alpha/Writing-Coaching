Answer the Question the Prompt Asks

The #1 move that turns 15/20 into 19–20/20

Blair, here is the Q11 prompt you were given on G4.4 — read it carefully:

THE G4.4 PROMPT (WORD FOR WORD)
“Based on the information in the article, write one well-organized paragraph in response to the following: What lesson do you think readers can learn from Ferdinand Cheval's story?”

The prompt asks for a lesson. Not a summary of what Ferdinand did. A lesson — something a reader can take away and apply to their own life. Here is how your paragraph opened:

YOUR OPENING SENTENCE
“Ferdinand Cheval was a mail carrier who created a castle made of rocks over the course of 33 years.”
This is a strong fact sentence. But it tells the reader what Ferdinand did, not what the lesson is. The grader reads the first line looking for the main idea — and the main idea here should be the lesson.

Here is the same paragraph opened with a topic sentence that answers the prompt:

A TOPIC SENTENCE THAT ANSWERS THE PROMPT
“Readers can learn from Ferdinand Cheval's story that a big dream is worth the patience it takes to finish it. Cheval was a mail carrier who spent 33 years building a castle out of rocks…”
Same information, same voice. But now the first sentence names the lesson, and the Ferdinand facts become the evidence that supports the lesson.
The prompt asks: What lesson can readers learn from Ferdinand Cheval's story?
Which opening sentence answers the question the prompt actually asked?
A Ferdinand Cheval spent 33 years building a rock castle in his garden.
B Ferdinand Cheval's story teaches readers that even small actions repeated over many years can add up to something amazing.
THE TOPIC-SENTENCE FORMULA FOR “LESSON” PROMPTS
When a G4 paragraph prompt asks for a lesson, open with one of these patterns:

• “Readers can learn that __________.”
• “The story teaches readers that __________.”
• “From Cheval's story, readers learn __________.”

The blank is the actual lesson — one full sentence that could stand alone. That sentence is worth 2–3 points on Q11 by itself.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
The Q11 rubric has a whole section called Main Idea. When your topic sentence answers the prompt directly, that section scores at the top. When it retells events instead, it scores in the middle — which is roughly the 3–4 point gap between your 15/20 and a 19–20/20.

Use Evidence from the Article

The rubric says “use evidence from the selection” — so show that you did

Blair, look at the bulleted rules inside the G4.4 prompt. The article gave you a required list. One of the bullets is specific:

FROM THE G4.4 PROMPT
Your paragraph should:
• clearly state your main idea
• organize your writing
• develop your ideas in detail
• use evidence from the selection in your response
• use correct spelling, capitalization, punctuation, and grammar

Your paragraph had great facts about Ferdinand — but they were all in your own words. The rubric bullet is asking for something more specific: a quoted phrase or number from the article that shows you actually used the selection.

YOUR MIDDLE SENTENCES
“When Ferdinand was doing his job as mail carrier, he started grabbing rocks and stored them in his garden. At first, people thought his intentions were strange, but then Ferdinand finished his structure.”
These facts are accurate and well ordered. But there is no quoted phrase from the article, and no specific evidence detail beyond what a reader could guess from a title.
THE SAME MIDDLE WITH AN EVIDENCE QUOTE
“The article says Cheval worked on his castle for ‘33 years’, picking up rocks while he delivered the mail. At first, neighbors called his project strange, but Cheval kept building every single day until it was finished.”
Notice two moves: (1) the phrase “The article says…” signals an evidence quote is coming, and (2) the quoted number “33 years” is lifted straight from the selection. That hits the rubric bullet.
The Q11 rubric asks for “evidence from the selection.” Which sentence uses evidence correctly?
A Cheval worked very hard for a long time on his castle and many people did not understand him at first.
B According to the article, Cheval spent “33 years” building his castle while working as a mail carrier.
THE EVIDENCE SIGNAL-PHRASE TOOLKIT
When you quote the article, start with one of these phrases so the grader knows it is evidence:

• “According to the article, __________.”
• “The article says __________.”
• “The author writes that __________.”
• “In the text, __________.”

Put the exact words or number from the article inside quotation marks. Even one short quote per paragraph (like “33 years”) earns the evidence bullet.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
“Use evidence from the selection” is a full bullet on the rubric — that is worth roughly 1–2 points on Q11. One signal phrase plus one quoted word or number is all it takes to check that box.

Your G4.4 Paragraph — Rebuild It for 19–20/20

15/20 — strong retelling; now add the lesson layer

Blair, here is your full Ferdinand Cheval paragraph exactly as you wrote it. Read it once through before we map where each sentence lands on the Q11 rubric.

“Ferdinand Cheval was a mail carrier who created a castle made of rocks over the course of 33 years. When Ferdinand was doing his job as mail carrier, he started grabbing rocks and stored them in his garden. At first, people thought his intentions were strange, but then Ferdinand finished his structure. Now, many people admire him and his story.”
Your Paragraph, Mapped Against the Rubric
MISSING Topic sentence that names the lesson. Your first sentence gives facts, not the lesson. (See Card 3.)
STRONG Clear time order. “When he was doing his job… at first… but then… now…” is great structure.
MISSING Evidence quote from the article. No signal phrase, no quoted word or number. (See Card 4.)
STRONG Closing sentence with a shift. “Now, many people admire him” shows perspective change — a good instinct.
MISSING Conclusion ties back to the lesson. The closing line names admiration but not the takeaway for the reader.
ONE SMALL GRAMMAR FIX
You wrote: “he started grabbing rocks and stored them in his garden.”

“Grabbing” is the -ing form. “Stored” is the past form. In one sentence, both actions should match. Two clean rewrites:
• “he started grabbing rocks and storing them in his garden.”
• “he grabbed rocks and stored them in his garden.”

Small fix, but verb-tense consistency is on the conventions part of the rubric.

YOUR PARAGRAPH — REBUILT FOR 19–20/20

Readers can learn from Ferdinand Cheval's story that a big dream is worth the patience it takes to finish it. Cheval was a mail carrier who spent “33 years” building a castle out of rocks, according to the article. While he delivered the mail each day, he grabbed rocks and stored them in his garden, piece by piece. At first, people thought his project was strange, but Cheval kept working until the castle was finished. Now many people admire him and his story — which shows that small steps taken every day, even when others doubt you, can build something amazing.

Notice what changed and what didn't: the facts are yours, the time-order structure is yours, and the “now many people admire him” closing instinct is yours. The two additions are the topic sentence naming the lesson and the “33 years” evidence quote. Plus the closing line ties back to the opening lesson. That is the whole 4-point lift.
