What's an Essay?

The difference between a paragraph and an essay

The G6 test asks you to write a 5-paragraph essay instead of a single paragraph. But first — can you spot why structure matters?

Which structure would score higher on the G6 writing test?
A ONE LONG PARAGRAPH — all ideas crammed together in a single block
B Introduction Body 1 Body 2 Body 3 Ending

An essay has 5 paragraphs, and each one has a specific job:

The 5-Paragraph Essay Map
Introduction General Statement → Specific Statement → Main Idea Sentence (names your 3 topics)
Body 1 Your first topic — evidence from the passage + explain why it matters
Body 2 Your second topic — evidence from the passage + explain why it matters
Body 3 Your third topic — evidence from the passage + explain why it matters
Ending Main Idea Sentence (restated) → Specific Statement → General Statement
THE BRIDGE FROM PARAGRAPHS TO ESSAYS
You've been writing single paragraphs that usually score around 90%. An essay is just 5 paragraphs, each doing a specific job. You already know the building blocks — now you're learning how to arrange them.

The Introduction

How to start your essay strong

Your introduction is the first paragraph. It follows a pattern: start broad, get specific, then state your main idea. Which introduction follows this pattern?

Which introduction better previews the essay?
A "There are many reasons why playing team sports is good. Students can play many different sports. Playing team sports benefits students in school and in life."
B "Around the world, millions of young people participate in team sports every year. Many schools now require students to join at least one sport or physical activity. Playing team sports benefits students by improving physical health, teaching teamwork, and building confidence."

A strong introduction follows a 3-sentence pattern:

The Introduction Pattern
General Statement A broad opening about the topic — something most people would agree with
Specific Statement Narrows the focus toward what your essay is specifically about
Main Idea Sentence States your claim and names your 3 body paragraph topics
Example Introduction
General "Around the world, millions of young people participate in team sports every year."
Specific "Many schools now require students to join at least one sport or physical activity."
Main Idea "Playing team sports benefits students by improving physical health, teaching teamwork, and building confidence."
THE BRIDGE FROM YOUR PARAGRAPH WRITING
The main idea sentence works like a paragraph's topic sentence, but for the whole essay — it names what all 3 body paragraphs will cover. You already start your paragraphs with strong topic sentences (like "There are many ways that humans can benefit from letting robots help out"). Now just add a general and specific sentence before it to ease the reader in.

Body Paragraphs: Evidence + "So What?"

You already do this — let's make it even stronger

Here's a paragraph you actually wrote on your G4.2 test about robots. Look at how much you already do right:

YOUR PARAGRAPH (G4.2 — SCORED 90%)
"There are many ways that humans can benefit from letting robots help out in workplaces or at home. One example is at work. As stated in the article, 'In one factory in the Netherlands, workers use robot arms to put together parts for machines...the robots...make many more parts each hour.' This shows that letting robots help in a work environment increases efficiency. Another example is letting robots help out at home. Doing this reduces labor and time for people. In conclusion, letting robots help out with tasks in both workplaces and homes help reduce time and effort and increase productivity in workplaces."

Notice what you did naturally: topic sentence, evidence with a direct quote, and then "This shows that..." — that's the "So what?" explanation. You already write like this in your best work.

Now, can you spot the difference between a paragraph that just lists facts and one that explains them?

Which body paragraph would score higher?
A "Robots can help at work. In one factory, robot arms put together machine parts. Robots can also help at home. They can clean and do chores. Robots are helpful everywhere."
B "Robots can help at work. In one factory, robot arms put together machine parts. This shows that robots increase efficiency because they can make many more parts each hour. Robots can also help at home by doing chores. This matters because it gives people more free time to spend on things they enjoy."
Body Paragraph Formula
Topic Sentence States the paragraph's main point
Evidence A specific fact, quote, or example from the passage
So what? Why does that evidence matter? Explain the connection.
More Evidence A second fact or example from the passage
So what? Why does THIS evidence matter?
Closing Sentence Wraps up the paragraph's main idea

Now compare two of your own paragraphs:

Your Robot Paragraph (G4.2) — Strong Your Logo Paragraph (G5.2) — Could Be Stronger
Evidence: "robots make many more parts each hour"
"So what?": "This shows that letting robots help increases efficiency." Clear explanation!		 Evidence: "most people couldn't draw the Apple logo perfectly"
Then: "This is an example of a gist memory." Repeats the idea without explaining why it matters.
THE KEY RULE: AFTER EVERY EVIDENCE, ASK "SO WHAT?"
Your robot paragraph nailed this — "This shows that..." explained WHY the evidence mattered. In your logo paragraph, the evidence repeated itself instead of answering "So what?" After each piece of evidence, ask yourself: "So what? Why does this matter to the reader?" The answer IS your explanation.
See how to fix the logo paragraph
Before: "A study showed that most people couldn't draw the Apple logo perfectly from memory but understood the idea of it. This is an example of a gist memory."

After (with "So what?"): "A study showed that most people couldn't draw the Apple logo perfectly from memory but still recognized it instantly. This matters because it proves that a simple, well-designed logo sticks in people's minds even without perfect recall — and that's exactly what makes it valuable for a company."

The Ending + Transitions

How to finish strong and connect your paragraphs

Your ending is the last paragraph. It mirrors the introduction — but in reverse order. Can you spot which ending follows the right pattern?

Which ending would score higher?
A "In conclusion, this topic is very important for many reasons. Students should play team sports. It would help a lot of people."
B "In conclusion, playing team sports helps students grow stronger, work better with others, and believe in themselves. These benefits carry over into the classroom, where students who play sports often show better focus and cooperation. As students grow up, the lessons they learn on the field will help them succeed in school, in their careers, and in their relationships."

The ending mirrors the introduction — same 3 parts, but in reverse order:

The Ending Pattern (Mirror of the Introduction)
Main Idea Sentence Restate your main idea in different words than your introduction
Specific Statement Summarize or reflect on what the body paragraphs showed
General Statement End broad — connect to the bigger picture (the future, the real world, why the reader should care)

You already write solid conclusions in your paragraphs. Look at your endings:

  • G4.2: "In conclusion, letting robots help out with tasks in both workplaces and homes help reduce time and effort and increase productivity." — this restates the main idea well!
  • G5.2: "In conclusion, recognizable logos are helpful for reasons like gist memories and the account of attentional saturation." — good restatement, but could add the specific and general sentences

For your essay ending, follow the pattern: restate your main idea → get specific → end with a broad statement about why this matters.

Transitions between paragraphs:

Use transition words to connect your paragraphs smoothly:

Transition Words for Essays
Body 1 "First," • "One way..." • "To begin with,"
Body 2 "Another way..." • "In addition," • "Second,"
Body 3 "Finally," • "The last reason..." • "Most importantly,"
Ending "In conclusion," • "Overall," • "All in all,"
YOU ALREADY DO THIS
You already use great transitions in your paragraphs — "One example is...", "Another example is...", "In conclusion,..." In an essay, use these same patterns to connect paragraphs to each other, not just sentences.

Spelling & Proofreading

The easiest way to pick up free points on the G6 test

Across your 7 tests, the same types of spelling errors come up again and again. Can you spot the correct version?

Which sentence has the correct spelling?
A "Having a recongnizable logo is vaulable in any enviorment where people interact with eachother."
B "Having a recognizable logo is valuable in any environment where people interact with each other."

Here are your most common spelling patterns to watch for:

Pattern 1: Words with -tion and tricky letter order

recongnizable → recognizable
enviorment → environment
conditon → condition

Pattern 2: Double letters and swapped letters

seperated → separated
ribbion → ribbon
vaulable → valuable
recevied → received

Pattern 3: Word boundaries

eachother → each other (two words)
THE 3-MINUTE BACKWARD PROOFREADING RULE
Save the last 3 minutes of your test for proofreading. Read your essay backward — start with the last sentence and work your way up. When you read forward, your brain auto-corrects errors. Reading backward forces you to see each sentence on its own.
Why backward reading works
When you read your essay forward, your brain knows what you meant to write, so it fills in the correct spelling automatically. Reading backward breaks that flow — each sentence feels fresh, and spelling errors jump out. Focus especially on long words with -tion, double letters, and words that might actually be two words.

Practice: Plan Your Essay

The 30-second trick that guarantees 5 organized paragraphs

Imagine the G6 test gives you this prompt:

Based on the article about school gardens, write an essay explaining how school gardens benefit students.

Before you write a single sentence, use the 30-Second Planning Trick:

Step 1: Write 5 labels in the margin
Introduction General → Specific → Main Idea (names 3 topics)
Body 1 (leave blank until Step 2)
Body 2 (leave blank until Step 2)
Body 3 (leave blank until Step 2)
Ending Main Idea (restated) → Specific → General
Step 2: Add 2–3 word topics next to each body paragraph
Introduction General: gardens are popular → Specific: schools use them → Main Idea: 3 benefits
Body 1 healthy eating
Body 2 science skills
Body 3 teamwork + responsibility
Ending Restate main idea → what we learned → gardens help beyond school

That's it. This takes 30 seconds. Now when you write, you know exactly what each paragraph will be about — no getting lost or cramming everything into one block.

Tap to see a completed example introduction
Try writing the plan first, then check
EXAMPLE INTRODUCTION FROM THIS PLAN
General: More and more schools across the country are finding creative ways to help students learn. Specific: One popular approach is school gardens, where students grow fruits and vegetables right on campus. Main Idea: School gardens benefit students by teaching them about healthy eating, building hands-on science skills, and developing teamwork and responsibility.

Notice: starts broad (schools finding creative ways), gets specific (school gardens), then the main idea sentence names all 3 body topics.

WHY PLANNING IS THE SINGLE MOST POWERFUL WRITING STRATEGY
Research shows that planning before writing produces bigger improvements than any other writing strategy. Students who plan their structure before writing produce better essays — even if the plan is just 5 labels in the margin. The plan is not extra work; it is the shortcut.
How this connects to what you already do
In your paragraph writing, you already naturally organize your thoughts: topic sentence first, examples in the middle, conclusion at the end. Planning an essay is the same thing at a bigger scale — you're just deciding what goes in each of your 5 paragraphs before you start. Think of it as writing 5 tiny topic sentences (just 2–3 words each) before you begin.
