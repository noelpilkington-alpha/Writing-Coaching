Step 2 Error: Conjunction Logic (Q3)

Cause-vs-effect order matters

Tyson, on Q3 you chose the wrong conjunction because you confused which clause is the cause and which is the effect. This is the same error pattern as other students on this test, and it comes down to one thing: label the parts before choosing.

🔎 How conjunction logic works: "Because" introduces the cause (the reason). "So" introduces the effect (the result). If you pick the wrong one, the logic of the sentence flips and does not make sense.
THE FIX: LABEL CAUSE AND EFFECT FIRST
Before choosing a conjunction, label the two halves:

• Which half is the reason (why something happened)?
• Which half is the result (what happened because of it)?

Then match:
• "because" → what follows is the CAUSE
• "so" → what follows is the EFFECT
• "since" → what follows is the CAUSE
The school's electricity bill dropped. Why? Because the solar panels generated clean energy. Which sentence shows correct cause-effect logic?
A The solar panels generated clean energy because the electricity bill dropped significantly.
B The electricity bill dropped because the solar panels generated clean energy throughout the summer.
THE FIVE-SECOND CHECK
After picking a conjunction, read the sentence out loud and ask: "Does this make logical sense? Is the reason actually the reason?" If the sentence sounds backwards, you have the cause and effect swapped. Switch the conjunction.

Step 1 Error: Topic Sentence (Q7)

You picked the enabler, not the result

Tyson, on Q7 you were asked to identify the topic sentence that best matches a paragraph. You picked the sentence that described what made something possible (the enabler), not what actually happened as a result. The question wanted the result.

🔎 Enabler vs Result: An enabler is what allows something to happen ("The school installed solar panels"). A result is what actually happened because of it ("The electricity bill dropped by 40%"). Topic sentences usually state the main result or claim that the paragraph proves.
HOW TO PICK THE RIGHT TOPIC SENTENCE
When a question asks "Which is the best topic sentence for this paragraph?" look at what the paragraph is mostly about:

• If the paragraph gives examples of results (savings, improvements, benefits), the topic sentence should state the result.
• If the paragraph explains how something works, the topic sentence should name what it does.

The enabler (what made it possible) is usually mentioned IN the paragraph, but it is not the MAIN POINT.
A paragraph discusses how the school saved money, bought new equipment, and hired a science tutor after installing solar panels. Which is the best topic sentence?
A The school installed solar panels on the roof of the gymnasium last spring.
B The solar panel project transformed the school's budget and created new opportunities for students.
STEP 1 IN ACTION
Reading the paragraph twice before choosing reveals what it is mostly about. If every sentence gives an example of a result or benefit, the topic sentence should name that result. The enabler is supporting information, not the main point.

Your Essay Needs More Development

14/20 → 18+ means more explanation after every piece of evidence

Tyson, your essay earned 14 out of 20. It engages with the Solar Panel Project passage and has clear ideas. But it stays at 14 because you present evidence without fully explaining what it means and why it matters.

THE D+E PATTERN
After you state a fact or piece of evidence, add:

D (Development): Explain what the evidence means in your own words.
E (Explanation): Connect it back to the essay's main argument.

Without D+E: "The school's electricity bill dropped by 40%." (states a fact, stops)
With D+E: "The school's electricity bill dropped by 40%. This means thousands of dollars were freed up for educational materials. In other words, the solar panels did not just help the environment; they directly improved student learning."
