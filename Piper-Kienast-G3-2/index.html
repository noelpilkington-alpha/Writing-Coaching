Make It Concise

When a question says "concise," shorter is better

Piper, Question 1 asked you to rewrite a sentence to make it concise — that means shorter and cleaner, saying the same thing with fewer words. Here is what you wrote:

YOU WROTE (G3.2 Q1)
"That day, the Hershey Bears broke the record of how many bears went on the ice, by collecting 45,650 stuffed animals that day for children during the holidays."
This actually got longer, and "that day" appears twice.

Your idea is correct — but the task was to trim, not add. "That day" is repeated, and there are a few extra words. Concise means: keep the core idea, cut the repeats.

Which rewrite is actually concise?
A That day, the Hershey Bears broke the record that day for how many stuffed animals were collected that day for children.
B The Hershey Bears set a record by collecting 45,650 stuffed animals for children during the holidays.
THE RULE FOR "CONCISE"
When a question asks you to make a sentence concise:

1. Find the one main idea and keep it.
2. Delete any word or phrase that repeats (like "that day" twice).
3. Cut extra words that do not add meaning.

If your rewrite is longer than the original, it is not concise yet.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Concise rewrites are an easy point to lock in. The skill is simple: read the original, ask "what is the one thing this says?", and write that in as few clear words as you can. Watch for repeated words — they are the first thing to cut.

One Stop Between Two Thoughts

Two complete ideas need a period or a joining word

Piper, on a couple of answers you joined two complete thoughts with only a comma. That is the one structural slip showing up in your writing. Here are your two real examples:

FROM G3.2 Q7
"I think you should'nt throw the bears on the ice, you should make a stand..."
Two complete thoughts joined by only a comma.
FROM G3.2 Q9
"Yes, it is good that they are giving teddy bears to children that don't have people during the holidays, I dont like the idea of throwing them on the ice."
Same thing — two full ideas, only a comma between them.
Which version joins the two ideas correctly?
A It is good that they are giving teddy bears to children, I do not like the idea of throwing them on the ice.
B It is good that they are giving teddy bears to children. I do not like the idea of throwing them on the ice.
THE RULE
When you have two complete thoughts (each could stand alone as a sentence), you cannot join them with just a comma. Two easy fixes:

1. Put a period between them and start a new sentence.
2. Add a joining word: and, but, so, because.

✗ "I like the giving, I do not like the throwing."
✓ "I like the giving, but I do not like the throwing."
✓ "I like the giving. I do not like the throwing."
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
This is the one structural habit standing between your already-strong answers and full marks on the reflection questions. On your proofread pass, look for any comma in the middle of a long answer and ask: "are these two complete thoughts?" If yes, swap the comma for a period or add a joining word.
