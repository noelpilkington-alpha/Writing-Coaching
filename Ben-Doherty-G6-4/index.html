The 5-Paragraph Essay Blueprint

A fill-in-the-blanks plan that makes the essay easy

Ben, an essay feels big until you see it as five small pieces. Each piece has one job. Once you have the blueprint, you just fill it in — and the passage gives you everything you need.

The 5-Paragraph Blueprint
INTRO A thesis that answers the prompt and previews your 3 points
BODY 1 Point 1 + a detail from the passage + explain why it matters
BODY 2 Point 2 + a detail from the passage + explain why it matters
BODY 3 Point 3 + a detail from the passage + explain why it matters
CONC Restate your thesis and sum up the three points in a new way

The G6.4 prompt asked about how students learn new languages effectively. "The Age of Language" hands you three ready-made points:

YOUR THREE POINTS, STRAIGHT FROM THE PASSAGE
1. Immersion classes — surrounding yourself with the language
2. Pen pals — practicing with a real person who speaks it
3. Starting young — learning is easier at an early age

That's the whole essay planned in three lines. Now watch how easily those lines turn into real paragraphs:

MODEL INTRO (previews 3 points)

There are several effective ways for students to learn a new language. According to "The Age of Language," three of the best methods are immersion classes, writing to pen pals, and starting at a young age. Each one helps a learner pick up the language faster and remember it longer.

MODEL BODY 1 (point + detail + explanation)

One effective way to learn a new language is through immersion classes. The passage explains that immersion surrounds students with the language so they hear and use it constantly. This matters because the more a learner is surrounded by the language, the more natural it becomes to think and speak in it, instead of translating word by word.

SEE THE PATTERN?
Every body paragraph follows the same three moves: (1) state the point, (2) back it with a detail from "The Age of Language" (quoted or in your own words), (3) explain why it matters. Repeat for pen pals and starting young, add a conclusion, and the 20-point essay is done.
So what? You don't have to invent an essay from nothing. The blueprint gives you the shape and the passage gives you the content. Fill in the blanks and you have a full essay every time.

The One Multiple-Choice Miss: "because"

Question 3 — a tiny comma rule, easy to lock in
🔍 Nice work: 9 of your 10 multiple-choice answers were correct. The only miss was Q3, and it comes down to one small punctuation rule with the word "because."

Ben, on Q3 you picked the version with a comma before "because." Here is the version you chose:

YOU CHOSE (G6.4 Q3)
"Students learn new words, because they hear them used in real conversations."

The idea is right — the only problem is that little comma before "because."

Which version is punctuated correctly?
A Students learn new words, because they hear them used in real conversations.
B Students learn new words because they hear them used in real conversations.
THE RULE, SIMPLY
When "because" gives the reason for the main idea that comes right before it, do not put a comma before "because."

✗ "Students learn new words, because they hear them."
✓ "Students learn new words because they hear them."

The reason and the main idea belong together, so the sentence flows without a stop.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
This is the same "because" you'll use in your essay's body paragraphs to connect a point to its reason. Getting the comma right keeps those sentences clean — and it's the one easy point to recover on the multiple-choice section next time.
