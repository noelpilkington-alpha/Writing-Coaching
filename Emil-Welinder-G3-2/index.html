#1 Focus: Be Concise — Cut Repeated Words

When a question asks you to tighten a sentence, say each idea once

Emil, some questions ask you to make a sentence concise. Concise means saying the same idea with fewer words — cut anything that is repeated or extra so each idea appears only once. Look at two real spots from G3.2:

YOU WROTE (G3.2 Q1 — "make this concise")
"That day, the Hershey Bears, a fun ice hockey team, broke their own record by collecting 45,650 stuffed animals that day."
The time phrase "that day" appears at the start AND the end — the same words twice. The task was to trim, but the repeat is still doubled.
TIGHTENED
"That day, the Hershey Bears broke their own record by collecting 45,650 stuffed animals."
One "that day" is plenty. Same meaning, fewer words — that is concise.

Here is the same pattern on Q3 — a word that slipped in twice:

YOU WROTE (G3.2 Q3)
"Does the fun event event sell out every year?"
"event" is typed twice in a row.
TIGHTENED
"Does the fun event sell out every year?"
Which sentence is concise (says each idea once)?
A That day, the Bears broke their record by collecting 45,650 stuffed animals that day.
B That day, the Bears broke their record by collecting 45,650 stuffed animals.
THE RULE
Concise = each idea said once. When a question asks you to tighten a sentence, hunt for words or phrases that repeat ("that day"…"that day", "event event") and remove the extra copy. Adding more detail is great in other questions, but a "make it concise" question wants you to take words out, not add them.
THE READ-BACK CHECK
Read your tightened sentence out loud in your head. If a word or time phrase shows up twice, cut one. If you can remove a word and the meaning stays the same, remove it.

#2 Focus: Add a Specific Reason

Opinion answers earn full marks when they give a concrete "because"

Emil, on the opinion questions (Q6–Q10) the goal is one complete sentence that answers the question and gives a specific reason. A few of your answers stayed a bit general — they repeated the question words instead of naming a concrete reason. Here is the clearest example:

YOU WROTE (G3.2 Q6 — why is the Teddy Bear Toss popular with fans?)
"The Teddy Bear Toss is a fun event that can let you have some fun with teddy bears."
This circles back to "fun…fun" without saying why it is popular. It repeats the idea instead of giving a real reason.
FULLER — ANSWER + because + A SPECIFIC REASON
"The Teddy Bear Toss is popular because fans get to throw teddy bears onto the ice for charity, which is exciting and helps others."
Now there is a concrete reason: throwing bears for charity. One sentence, real detail.
SAME FIX ON Q7
Your Q7 idea (letting fans skate on the ice with the bears) was good — just add a quick because: "I would let fans skate on the ice with the bears because it would make the event feel even more special." One sentence, with a reason.
Which answer fully explains why the event is popular?
A The Teddy Bear Toss is a fun event that can let you have some fun with teddy bears.
B The Teddy Bear Toss is popular because fans get to throw teddy bears onto the ice for charity, which is exciting and helps others.
THE SHAPE TO USE
[your answer] + because + [a specific reason].

Write one complete sentence that answers the question and includes a concrete reason or example. If your sentence repeats the question's words ("a fun event…have fun"), ask yourself: why? Then add that "because" with a real detail.
