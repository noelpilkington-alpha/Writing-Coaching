"Would Have," Not "Would Of"

Q6 & Q9 — A common mistake that's easy to fix

You wrote "would of" twice on this test. This is a very common mistake — it sounds right when you say it out loud, but "of" is not the right word. The correct phrase is "would have."

Q6 — Would you have helped the seal?

FIXED

Q9 — What would you have done differently?

YOU WROTE
I would of called animal control and let it help it correctly.
Tap to see the improved version
Read the explanation above first
FIXED
I would have called animal control and let them help the seal correctly.
WHY THIS HAPPENS
When people say "would've" out loud, it sounds like "would of." But "would've" is actually short for "would have." The word "of" is never part of this phrase.

would of → would have (would've)
could of → could have (could've)
should of → should have (should've)
Quick fix: Every time you write "of" after would/could/should, change it to "have."

You added a detail ("habitat"), but an appositive is a description placed right after a noun, set off by commas. It renames or describes the noun.

Tap to see the improved version

WITH AN APPOSITIVE

WHAT IS AN APPOSITIVE?
An appositive is a short description placed right after a noun, surrounded by commas. It tells the reader more about who or what the noun is.

Formula: Noun, description, rest of sentence.
Example: Sally, the baby seal, swam to the other seals.

Think of it as a quick nickname or label you tuck in right after the name.

Build Your Paragraph

Q11 — Let's learn the paragraph structure from scratch

Your paragraph scored 0/20 because you wrote:

YOUR PARAGRAPH
i dont know

That's okay — everyone has to start somewhere. The paragraph is worth 20 points, which is almost half the whole test. Learning how to write one will make a huge difference. Here's the plan:

A G3 paragraph follows this pattern:

Paragraph Plan — TS → D1 → W → D2 → W → D3 → W → CS
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail from the article  •  W = Why it matters  •  CS = Concluding Sentence
TS Satoshi Tajiri's childhood love of catching bugs led him to create Pokémon.
D1 As a child, Tajiri spent hours outside catching bugs — his friends called him "Dr. Bug."
W This shows how much he loved collecting creatures, which became the main idea behind Pokémon.
D2 When he grew up, he remembered his bug-catching joy and turned it into a video game idea.
W This shows that without that childhood memory, the game might never have been created.
D3 Pokémon became huge — over 150 characters, TV shows, movies, apps, and more.
W This shows that one kid's hobby turned into something millions of people enjoy.
CS Tajiri's childhood hobby of catching bugs turned into one of the most popular games in the world.
HOW TO START WHEN YOU DON'T KNOW WHAT TO WRITE
Step 1: Read the prompt. It tells you exactly what to explain.
Step 2: Write one sentence that answers the prompt. That's your Topic Sentence (TS).
Step 3: Find 3 details from the article that support your topic sentence. Those are D1, D2, D3.
Step 4: After each detail, write one sentence explaining why it matters. Ask yourself: "So what?"
Step 5: End with a Concluding Sentence (CS) that restates your main point.

Even if you're unsure, writing something that follows this plan will earn you points. "I don't know" earns zero, but even a short paragraph with a topic sentence and one detail can earn several points.
