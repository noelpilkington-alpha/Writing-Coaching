Appositives Rename, They Don't Join

#1 focus — a renaming phrase tucked between commas

Mia, Question 1 asked you to rewrite sentences 1 and 2 as one sentence that includes an appositive. Here is what you wrote:

YOU WROTE (Q1 — the task asked for an appositive)
"My family has two dogs and their names are Lucy and Rocket."

Your instinct was right — you combined the two sentences into one, smoothly. But you joined them with "and", and the task asked for an appositive. An appositive doesn't join two ideas. It renames the noun right beside it, tucked between commas — no "and" needed.

THE APPOSITIVE VERSION
"My family has two dogs, Lucy and Rocket."

See how "Lucy and Rocket" sits right next to "two dogs" and renames it? Two dogs = Lucy and Rocket. That renaming phrase, set off with a comma, is the appositive.

The task says: combine into ONE sentence that includes an APPOSITIVE. Which sentence follows the task?
A My family has two dogs, Lucy and Rocket.
B My family has two dogs and their names are Lucy and Rocket.
THE RULE
An appositive is a renaming phrase that sits right next to a noun, tucked between commas.

• My family has two dogs, Lucy and Rocket.
• My brother, Sam, loves soccer.
• We visited Austin, the capital of Texas.

The quick test: can you put an equals sign between the noun and the phrase? Two dogs = Lucy and Rocket. If yes, it's an appositive. If you needed the word "and" to connect them, it isn't one yet.

The bolded sentence is the move: when a prompt asks for the ways something matters, give your best way the star treatment — like your food chain — and then name at least one more way with a sentence of explanation.
