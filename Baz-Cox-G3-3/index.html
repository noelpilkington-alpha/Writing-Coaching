THE RULE: NAME THE REASONS, THEN FINISH EACH ONE
Your first sentence names the reasons. You did that. Then each reason gets a DETAIL · SHOW, a fact from the article, and a DETAIL · SO WHAT: This shows plus what the fact proves. Every sentence has to land: a sentence that starts So when needs the part that says what happened. Then an ending that puts the two reasons together. The check: read each sentence alone. Fix 2: Two Sentences Become One with a Joining Word

Q4: both facts, in order, and nothing at the seam
THE RULE: WHEN TWO SENTENCES BECOME ONE, SOMETHING HAS TO JOIN THEM
Combine means one sentence, and the two parts need a joiner: and, but, so, because, where, when, or a comma plus one of those. Two sentences pushed together with nothing between them (ran to the beach there they found) is a run-on. A comma on its own (two dog, their names are) cannot do the job either. The check: find the spot where sentence 1 ends and sentence 2 begins. Is there a joining word there?
YOU WROTE (Q4, 1 OUT OF 2)
“Anna and Jim ran to the beach there they found a baby seal in a fishnet.” TWO SENTENCES, NOTHING AT THE SEAM
Both facts, in the right order. The seam between beach and there is bare, so the grader reads two sentences in one.
A JOINER AT THE SEAM
Anna and Jim ran to the beach, where they found a baby seal in a fishnet.
Or: Anna and Jim ran to the beach and found a baby seal in a fishnet. Either joiner earns the second point.
YOU WROTE (AUGUST, G3.1 Q1, 1 OUT OF 2)
“My family has two dog, their names are Lucy and Rocket.” A COMMA DOING A JOINER’S JOB
The same seam, with a comma in it. The appositive version needs no joiner at all: My family has two dogs, Lucy and Rocket.
