Read It Back Before You Submit

Two sentences broke apart, and one read-back catches both

This is not about slowing down. The 93% came in 20 minutes, so speed clearly works. This is about the one pass at the end, where you read what you wrote back to yourself and listen for whether it still holds together. Here is what that pass would have found on G5.1:

QUESTION 8 — 1 OUT OF 3
“She would of came across as more sincere in trying to fix what her mistake.”
Two separate breaks in one short sentence. Start with the first, because your ear is not wrong here. People say “would’ve,” and “would’ve” sounds exactly like “would of” — so the sound you wrote down was right, and only the spelling is a trap. That short form stands for would have, which means written out it is always have and never “of.” Then, once it is “would have,” the word after it becomes come rather than “came.” And “fix what her mistake” has one spare word in it — you meant “fix her mistake.”
QUESTION 11 — INSIDE THE PARAGRAPH
“When scientists showed horses pictures of human facial expressions, The horses showed signs of…”
A capital T in the middle of a sentence, right after a comma. Nothing started there — so it stays lowercase.
QUESTION 11 — A WRONG WORD, NOT A MISSPELLING
“When a human gets angry, they express it though their facial movements.”
You wanted through. “Though” is a real word spelled correctly, which is exactly why only a read-back finds it.
Which version is ready to submit?
AShe would of came across as more honest if she had stayed.
BShe would have come across as more honest if she had stayed.
WHAT THE READ-BACK IS LISTENING FOR
Not spelling — that gets its own card next, because it needs a different fix. The read-back pass is for the things your ear catches and your eye does not:
  • “of” where a helper belongs → the helpers are have, has, had. Never “of.”
  • A spare or missing word — “fix what her mistake” sounds wrong the moment you say it out loud in your head.
  • A capital letter that is not starting anything.
  • A real word in the wrong slot — though / through, there / their, of / or.
One pass, top to bottom, before you submit — it is the shortest thing on any of these cards, and the best-paying minute on the test.

Your Paragraph: 16 out of 20

The thinking is ahead of the grade — the order is what cost the points

The question: Explain what new research reveals about horses’ ability to communicate. Here is what you wrote:

“Horses have ways of communicating to each other through facial expressions, just as humans do. When a human gets angry, they express it through their facial movements. We didn’t even study horses’ facial expressions until recently. Horses use facial expressions, and so do many other animals. ‘So why horses?’ you may ask. ‘If other animals do this, then why are we so centered on horses?’ Well the truth is that horses have more identified facial expressions than the other animals, thus making them our topic of study. Horses use expressions to convey messages to other horses, such as ones that mean: get out of my way, or this is my domain. When scientists showed horses pictures of human facial expressions, the horses showed signs of recognizing the feelings showed in the expressions. Their heartbeats quickened, and they turned to look at the picture with the left eye, which is something that dogs surprisingly also do when shown angry faces. I think that we can all agree that nature is a wonderful mystery book, with puzzles to solve. Thanks to our scientists, these wonderful mysteries in nature are solved.”
Spelling shown corrected here so the structure is easy to see.
Topic sentence answers the question Real analysis, not just retelling Uses the article’s actual findings The findings arrive ninth Ending leaves the topic

Two things here are genuinely strong, and neither is common. First, sentence one states a central idea and it answers the question directly — horses communicate through facial expressions, like humans do. Second, you do not just retell the article. “So why horses?” is you noticing a gap in your own argument and closing it, and “more identified facial expressions than the other animals, thus making them our topic of study” is real reasoning about why the research happened. That is a level above summarizing.

So where did four points go? Not the thinking — the order and the ending.

The question asked what the new research reveals. Your actual research findings — the quickening heartbeats, the turn to the left eye, the dog parallel — are the answer, and they are the strongest sentences in the paragraph. They arrive ninth and tenth out of eleven. Everything before them (human faces, other animals, the two questions) is setup. Then the last two sentences leave the subject completely for “nature is a wonderful mystery book” — and they claim the mysteries “are solved,” when the article is about research that is still going.

YOUR PARAGRAPH, REORDERED (closer to 20/20)

“Horses have ways of communicating with each other through facial expressions, just as humans do. So why horses? Because horses have more identified facial expressions than any other animal, which made them the obvious subject to study. Horses use those expressions to send messages to other horses, such as ones that mean: get out of my way, or this is my domain. Then researchers showed horses pictures of human facial expressions, and the horses showed signs of recognizing the feelings in them. Their heartbeats quickened, and they turned to look at the picture with the left eye, which is something that dogs surprisingly also do when shown angry faces. That last part is the real discovery: horses are not only making faces at each other, they are reading ours.”

Notice what did not happen there. No fact was removed. Your “So why horses?” move survived — it just got compressed from three sentences into one, so it works as a pivot instead of a detour. What changed: the research findings moved up, and the two decorative closing sentences were replaced by one sentence that says what the findings mean.

SO WHAT?
Your paragraph does not need more ideas — it has more than most. It needs the ideas in the order the question asked for. Two habits: put the research findings in the first half of the paragraph, not the last; and end on what the evidence means, not on a pretty closing thought. A paragraph that ends by answering the question is worth two to three more points than one that ends by admiring the subject.
