Essay Fix — Go Deeper with "So What?"

Can you spot the difference? Then we'll break it down.
📈 This is part of a pattern across your last 4 tests. Your essay scores average 74% — and the consistent gap is between giving evidence and explaining why it matters. When you add "So what?" explanations, your essays jump to the next level.

Here's a sentence from your essay about pen pals. Which version explains the evidence better?

Tap the version that explains WHY pen pals help students:
A "Children are able to write letters or emails and share stories about their daily lives. These friendships can motivate students to practice more and help them understand different cultures."
B "Children are able to write letters or emails and share stories about their daily lives. This helps them learn because they must actually use the new language to communicate real ideas, not just fill in worksheets. These real conversations motivate students to practice more."

Here's the pattern: your essay gives evidence, but sometimes skips the explanation of why that evidence matters.

What You Wrote (Evidence Only) With "So What?" Explanation Added
"Children are able to write letters or emails and share stories about their daily lives." "Children are able to write letters or emails and share stories about their daily lives. This helps them learn because they must actually use the new language to communicate real ideas, not just practice on worksheets."

The bolded sentence is the "So what?" — it explains WHY pen pals help language learning, not just WHAT students do.

THE "SO WHAT?" RULE
After every piece of evidence, ask yourself: "So what? Why does this matter?" The answer IS your explanation sentence. Evidence without explanation is just a list of facts. Evidence WITH explanation shows you understand the topic deeply.
See another example from your essay
Your evidence: "In immersion class, students hear it during lessons, group work, and even during lunch."

"So what?": "This constant exposure helps children learn faster because their brains get used to hearing and thinking in the new language throughout the entire day, not just during one class period."

The "So what?" sentence explains WHY hearing the language all day matters for learning.

Essay Fix — Stronger Main Idea Sentence

Your introduction hook is great. The main idea sentence needs a small upgrade.

Your introduction starts with a great hook: "Have you ever wondered what is the point of learning another language?" That grabs the reader's attention.

But your main idea sentence needs to preview your 3 specific topics:

Which main idea sentence better previews the essay's 3 topics?
A "Learning another language can strengthen memory and improve problem-solving skills."
B "Students can learn new languages effectively through immersion classes, real-world conversations, and by starting at an early age."

Your current main idea sentence talks about memory and problem-solving — but those aren't your 3 body paragraph topics. A strong main idea sentence is like a roadmap for your essay:

Your Essay's Actual Topics
Body 1 Immersion classes
Body 2 Real conversations and pen pals
Body 3 How age affects language learning

Your main idea sentence should name all 3 of these topics so the reader knows what's coming.

Inside each body paragraph, use this structure:

Body Paragraph Building Blocks
Topic Sentence States the paragraph's main point
Evidence A specific fact or example from the passage
Explain Why Why does that evidence matter? ("So what?")
More Evidence A second fact or example from the passage
Explain Why Why does THIS evidence matter?
Closing Sentence Wraps up the paragraph's main idea
MAIN IDEA SENTENCE FORMULA
After your hook, write one sentence that names your 3 body paragraph topics:
"[Topic] can be achieved/understood through [Body 1 topic], [Body 2 topic], and [Body 3 topic]."

This takes 10 seconds to write and earns you structure points every time.
Why this matters for scoring
The grading feedback looks for a main idea sentence that previews the essay's direction. When your main idea sentence matches your body paragraphs, it shows your writing is planned and organized — and that's worth real points in the "Stay organized and on topic" category.

3-MINUTE PROOFREADING RULE
Save the last 3 minutes of your test for proofreading. Read your essay backward (last sentence first) — when you read forward, your brain auto-corrects errors. Reading backward forces you to see each sentence on its own.
Why backward reading works
When you read your essay from start to finish, your brain already knows what you meant to write, so it fills in the gaps automatically. You'll read "practice" even when you typed "pracrive." But when you read the last sentence first, then the second-to-last, your brain treats each sentence as new — and errors jump out.
