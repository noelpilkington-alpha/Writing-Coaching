QUESTIONS 8 AND 10 — THE SAME WORD, BOTH TIMES
“…because its different structure from other plants…”
“I enjoy christmas during winter because its a fun time…”
These two need opposite things, which is exactly why the word is a trap. The first one is correct as its — the structure belongs to the plant. The second needs it’s, because it is short for it is: “because it is a fun time.”
THE ONE-SECOND TEST
Say “it is” in place of the word. If the sentence still makes sense, you need it’s with the apostrophe. If it turns into nonsense, you need its with no apostrophe. “Because it is a fun time” works — so that one takes the apostrophe. “Because it is different structure” does not work — so that one does not.

#1 Fix: An Appositive Is Information, Not Commas

Q4 — 0 out of 2 — the only zero on the test, and the clearest fix on it

Question 4 gave you this sentence and asked you to add an appositive that gives more information about bluebonnet festivals:

“Many people attend bluebonnet festivals in small towns all over Texas.”

Here is what you submitted:

QUESTION 4 — 0 OUT OF 2
“Many people, attend bluebonnet festivals, in small towns all over Texas.”

Put those two side by side and look at the words. They are identical. Not one word was added, removed, or changed — the only difference is two commas. So the question asked for more information about bluebonnet festivals, and the sentence still says nothing more about them than it did at the start.

What happened here is easy to name: you have learned that an appositive has commas around it, which is true. But the commas are only the wrapping. The appositive itself is the information inside them — a few words that rename the noun and tell the reader what it actually is.

THE SAME SENTENCE, WITH AN ACTUAL APPOSITIVE
“Many people attend bluebonnet festivals, colorful spring celebrations of the state flower, in small towns all over Texas.”
Six new words sit between the commas, and they answer the question “what is a bluebonnet festival?” That is the appositive. The commas just hold it in place.
THE DID-I-ADD-ANYTHING CHECK
This one is objective, and it takes five seconds. After you write your answer, compare your word count to the original. If you did not add any new words, there is no appositive in your sentence, no matter where the commas went. An appositive always makes the sentence longer, because it is made of information that was not there before.
Which sentence actually adds an appositive about the festivals?
AMany people attend bluebonnet festivals, in small towns all over Texas.
BMany people attend bluebonnet festivals, yearly celebrations of the state flower, in small towns all over Texas.
WHY THIS IS THE #1 FIX — AND WHY IT IS GOOD NEWS
Two of your six lost points came from this one question, so it is the biggest single item on the test. And here is the encouraging part: you already do the harder half of this skill. Q3 and Q5 both asked you to add your own information to a sentence, and both earned full marks — “Since bluebonnets grow best in sunny places, they would best survive in the summer” is you supplying real content with no help. Adding information is not the problem. The word appositive just sent you reaching for punctuation instead of content. Now you know it means content, and Q3 proves you can produce it.

#2 Fix: The Comma That Splits a Sentence

Three questions, the same comma — and one of them got away with it

Tap: Question 4

“Many people, attend bluebonnet festivals…”

“A wildflower with bright blue petals, is soon to be a beautiful blanket that covers the land.”

Tap: Question 8
Third time, and this one cost a point
Q8 — 2 OUT OF 3
“…because its different structure from other plants, is what makes it special…”
Who is it about? Its different structure. What does it do? Is. Same comma, same place.

Three times, the comma landed in the one spot a comma can never go: between the thing the sentence is about and the word that says what it does. Nothing separates those two. Ever.

THE RULE, USING YOUR OWN BEST SENTENCE
From Q9, full marks: “The mango tree in my backyard is special to my family because it was planted by my sister and my uncle seven years ago.”
Long subject — “the mango tree in my backyard” — and no comma before is. You already got this exactly right here. That is the model.
Which sentence is punctuated correctly?
AThe mango tree in my backyard, is special to my family.
BThe mango tree in my backyard is special to my family.
WHY THE COMMA KEEPS LANDING THERE
Almost always it is because the subject is long. “A wildflower with bright blue petals” is six words, and after six words it feels like time to breathe — so a comma goes in. But a pause in your voice is not the same as a comma on the page. The check: say who or what the sentence is about, then say what they did. If your comma sits between those two answers, take it out. That single habit protects Q2, Q4 and Q8 all at once.

Opens with a hook Thesis previews the whole paragraph Evidence cited by paragraph number Covers both home and workplace Real conclusion Most evidence not explained

Four things here are doing real work. The hook question pulls the reader in. Sentence two is a genuine thesis — it says what the paragraph will show and it previews the order (factories, home, other places), which is exactly what the question asked for. You cite evidence by paragraph number — “in paragraph 2 it states” — which is a move most students at this level do not make at all. And you close with a real conclusion instead of trailing off.

So where are the last two points? Almost all of it is one habit. You have three pieces of evidence — the Netherlands factory, Cleo and Leo at the Hotel EMC2, and the robot pets — and only the third one gets explained. “They may not have emotion but can still keep you company” is you telling the reader what the pet robots mean for a person. That sentence is the best one in the paragraph, and the first two pieces of evidence never got one.

Which matters especially here, because the question asked how humans benefit. The factory robot and the delivery robots are facts. The benefit is the part you say about them.

YOUR PARAGRAPH, DEVELOPED (20/20)

“Do you think people are ready for robots? Robots can help in many ways by helping in factories, at home, or in other places. For example, in paragraph 2 it states that robots help by making machines in a factory in the Netherlands. That means the workers who used to do that heavy lifting can spend their day on jobs that need a human mind instead. There are also two robots named Cleo and Leo that help people with deliveries in the Hotel EMC2. Every trip those two make is a trip a staff member does not have to, which frees that person up for guests who need real help. Some robots are made to be pets for people who have allergies. They may not have emotion but can still keep you company. In conclusion, robots can help us in different ways every day and can inspire us to keep making more.”

Nothing was taken out. Your hook, your thesis, your three pieces of evidence, your explanation of the pet robots and your conclusion all survive exactly as written. Two sentences were added, each doing the same job your best sentence already does — saying what the fact means for a person.

SO WHAT?
You are already good at the part most students find hardest: finding evidence in an article and putting it on the page with a source. The last two points are a smaller habit. After every piece of evidence, add one sentence starting “That means…” or “This shows that…” You did it once here, unprompted, and it produced the best sentence in the paragraph. Do it after all three and this paragraph is at full marks.
