#1 Fix: Unpack the Paragraph

Your Paragraph: 12 out of 20 — three ideas squashed into one sentence

The question: Explain the ways that soil is important to Earth — using evidence from the article. Here is everything you wrote:

“Soil is important to earth because it brings life to earth, any important animals live in soil, without soil there would be no food. That is why soil is important to earth.”
Central idea in the first words Three real reasons A closing sentence All three reasons in one sentence No details from the article Two sentences total

First, what is right — because the skeleton is right. “Soil is important to earth because it brings life” is a true central idea. Then you give three reasons: it brings life, animals live in it, without it there is no food. Then you close. That is the shape of a paragraph. It is just folded up very small.

Now look at the commas in your first sentence. Each one is a place where a new sentence wanted to start. “…brings life to earth, any important animals live in soil, without soil there would be no food.” Three complete thoughts, glued together. Unfold them and you have three sentences instead of one — and each of those three can carry one detail from the article: worms and groundhogs living in the soil, fallen leaves rotting into humus that feeds plants, plants feeding animals and people.

YOUR PARAGRAPH, UNPACKED

“Soil is important to Earth because it brings life to Earth. Many important animals live in soil. The article says worms and groundhogs make their homes there, so without soil they would have nowhere to live. Soil also grows the plants we eat. Fallen leaves and dead bugs rot into humus, which is food for plants, and those plants become food for animals and people. So without soil there would be no food. That is why soil is important to Earth.”

Count them: seven sentences, and every one of your original ideas is still there in your original order. What changed is that each comma became a period, and each reason got one fact from the article standing behind it. That is the whole distance from 12 to about 17.

SO WHAT?
When you catch yourself writing “this, this, this” in one sentence, stop. Those are your paragraph’s sentences. Give each one its own line and one detail from the passage, and the points follow. Five extra sentences here were worth about five points — nothing else on the test pays that well.

#2 Fix: A Comma Cannot Hold Two Sentences

Q1, Q2 and Q11 — the same slip three times, and two ways to fix it

The paragraph was not the only place a comma did a period’s job. Tap through:

Tap: Question 1
The task asked for an appositive
Q1 — 1 OUT OF 2
“My family has two dogs, their names are Lucy and Rocket”
“Their names are Lucy and Rocket” is a whole sentence on its own, so the comma is holding two sentences. The task wanted an appositive — a naming phrase with no verb of its own: “My family has two dogs, Lucy and Rocket.” Two words shorter, and a period at the end.
Tap: Question 2
The statement stayed, and a question got glued on
Q2 — 1 OUT OF 2 — “REWRITE SENTENCE 4 AS A QUESTION”
“Yet there is one place that we don’t take our dogs, do you want know where?”
The original statement is still sitting there, with a second sentence attached by a comma. A rewrite turns the whole thing into the question: “Is there one place we don’t take our dogs?” The verb moves to the front and nothing gets glued on.
Tap: Question 11
Three sentences behind two commas
Q11 — FIRST SENTENCE
“…it brings life to earth, any important animals live in soil, without soil there would be no food.”
Three complete thoughts. Two commas doing the work of two periods — and, as the last card showed, hiding the fact that you had a whole paragraph in there.
THE CHECK
Cover the comma and read each side on its own. If both sides could be their own sentence, the comma is the wrong tool. Use a period and start a new sentence. The one exception is when the task asks for an appositive — then the part after the comma should be a naming phrase with no verb, like “Lucy and Rocket,” not “their names are Lucy and Rocket.”
