Your #1 Recurring Error: Comma Splices

Q8, Q9, Q10 — three instances of the same pattern
🔍
What is a comma splice? When you join two complete sentences with just a comma and no connecting word (and, but, so, because). Both sides of the comma are full sentences with their own subject and verb. That is a structural error.
Q8 — YOUR ANSWER (2 out of 3)
"The bluebonnets bloomed across the field, they created a sea of blue that stretched for miles."

Left side: "The bluebonnets bloomed across the field" = complete sentence. Right side: "they created a sea of blue that stretched for miles" = complete sentence. Two complete sentences joined by just a comma = comma splice.

Q9 — YOUR ANSWER (2 out of 3)
"Robots can help in hospitals, they deliver medicine to patients quickly."
Q10 — YOUR ANSWER (2 out of 3)
"People worry about robots taking jobs, some workers might not be needed anymore."

The same pattern three times: Complete sentence + comma + complete sentence. No conjunction.

THREE WAYS TO FIX A COMMA SPLICE
1. Add a conjunction: "The bluebonnets bloomed across the field, and they created a sea of blue."

2. Use a period: "The bluebonnets bloomed across the field. They created a sea of blue."

3. Use "because" or "so": "Robots can help in hospitals because they deliver medicine quickly."
Which sentence fixes the comma splice from Q10?
APeople worry about robots taking jobs, some workers might not be needed anymore.
BPeople worry about robots taking jobs because some workers might not be needed anymore.
THE COMMA SPLICE TEST
Before you submit any sentence with a comma, do this check:

1. Find the comma.
2. Is what is on the LEFT a complete sentence? Is what is on the RIGHT a complete sentence?
3. If BOTH are complete, you need: a conjunction (and/but/so/because) OR a period.

This one check catches all three errors from this test.

Your #2 Target: Use Appositives Correctly

Q4 — rewrote instead of adding an appositive
Q4 — YOUR ANSWER (1 out of 2)
"The beautiful bluebonnets grew everywhere in the spring and people loved looking at them."

The question asked: Add an appositive to the sentence about bluebonnets.

The problem: You rewrote the sentence entirely instead of inserting an appositive phrase. An appositive is a phrase set off by commas that renames or describes a noun right next to it.

WHAT AN APPOSITIVE LOOKS LIKE
"Bluebonnets, the state flower of Texas, bloomed across the field in spring."

The phrase "the state flower of Texas" is the appositive. It renames "Bluebonnets" and is set off by commas.
HOW TO ADD AN APPOSITIVE
1. Find the main noun in the sentence (bluebonnets, robots, the girl).
2. Add a comma after it.
3. Insert a phrase that describes or renames that noun.
4. Add another comma after the phrase.
5. Continue the original sentence.

Original: "Bluebonnets bloomed across the field."
With appositive: "Bluebonnets, the state flower of Texas, bloomed across the field."

THE DETAIL + EXPLANATION PARAGRAPH PATTERN

1. Topic sentence: State your main idea.
2. Evidence #1: A specific detail from the passage.
3. "This shows...": Explain what that evidence proves or why it matters.
4. Evidence #2: A second detail from the passage.
5. "This shows...": Explain what the second detail proves.
6. Closing: Wrap up and connect back to your main idea.

Every piece of evidence needs its own explanation sentence. That is what separates a 17 from a 19 or 20.

YOUR NEXT LEVEL
You already have the structure. To move from 17 to 19+, make sure every evidence sentence is followed by a "This shows..." or "This is important because..." sentence. Two developed points with explanations are better than three quick points without.
