Appositives = Descriptive Noun Phrase

A location alone is not an appositive

On Q3, you were asked to add an appositive to describe Peter Tabichi. You wrote:

Q3 — 1 OUT OF 2
"Peter Tabichi, from Kenya africa, teaches high school science."

Two problems here:

  • "from Kenya africa" is a prepositional phrase (a location), not a descriptive noun phrase that renames who Peter Tabichi is.
  • "africa" needs a capital A, and there should be a comma between "Kenya" and "Africa" if both are included.

An appositive must be a noun phrase that renames or describes the noun beside it. Think of it as answering "Who is this person?" not "Where is this person from?"

FULL-CREDIT VERSION
"Peter Tabichi, an award-winning science educator from Kenya, teaches high school science."

Notice: "an award-winning science educator from Kenya" is a noun phrase (starts with "an," has a noun at its core: "educator"). It renames Peter Tabichi — it tells us WHO he is.
Which sentence uses a correct appositive?
A Peter Tabichi, from Kenya, teaches high school science.
B Peter Tabichi, a dedicated science educator, teaches high school science.
THE RULE
An appositive is a noun phrase placed next to another noun to rename or describe it. It is set off by commas.

Test: Does your appositive start with "a," "an," or "the" and have a noun as its main word? If yes, it is likely correct.

✗ "from Kenya" (prepositional phrase = location, not a renaming)
✓ "an award-winning science educator" (noun phrase = tells us WHO)
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
You scored 1 out of 2 on Q3. The full point you lost was because the appositive was a location phrase instead of a descriptive noun phrase. Swapping "from Kenya africa" for something like "an award-winning educator" earns full credit.

Avoid Comma Splices and Tangled Sentences

Two quick fixes worth 2 points total

On Q8 and Q10, you lost a point each because of sentence-construction errors. The ideas are good — the wiring just needs a small fix.

Q8 — 2 OUT OF 3
"One way I can show kindness is by letting people know what they are capable of, I feel like this is important because..."

This is a comma splice: two complete thoughts joined by just a comma. Each side of that comma could stand alone as its own sentence.

FIX: SPLIT INTO TWO SENTENCES
"One way I can show kindness is by letting people know what they are capable of. I feel like this is important because..."
Q10 — 2 OUT OF 3
"One skill I have that I think would help others my support, although I might not be the smartest..."

A word is missing. After "others" you need "is" to connect the subject to the rest: "...would help others is my support." Without it, the sentence gets tangled and hard to follow.

Which sentence correctly avoids the comma splice?
A One way I can show kindness is by letting people know what they are capable of, I feel like this is important.
B One way I can show kindness is by letting people know what they are capable of. I feel like this is important.
THE COMMA SPLICE TEST
When you see a comma in your writing, cover everything after it. Ask: "Could the part before the comma be its own sentence?" Then cover everything before. "Could the part after be its own sentence?" If BOTH answers are yes, you have a comma splice. Fix it with a period, or add a connecting word like "because" or "and."
THE MISSING-WORD CHECK
Read your sentence out loud. If it sounds tangled or you stumble, a word is probably missing. On Q10, reading "...would help others my support" out loud sounds wrong because the verb "is" is missing between "others" and "my."
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q8 and Q10 are each worth 3 points. You scored 2 out of 3 on both. Fixing the comma splice on Q8 and adding the missing word on Q10 would earn you 2 more points — that alone pushes you from 86% to 90%.
