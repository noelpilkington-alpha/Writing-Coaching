Make Your Thesis a Roadmap

Tell the reader where your essay is going

Here's your thesis (the last sentence of your introduction):

YOUR THESIS
"Many people are taking action to ensure that monarch butterflies make the trip to Mexico."

This tells the reader what your essay is about — but not how. A strong thesis previews the three specific topics your body paragraphs will cover. Think of it as a GPS for your reader: it tells them exactly where the essay is going.

Which thesis previews the three body paragraph topics?
A "Many people are taking action to ensure that monarch butterflies make the trip to Mexico."
B "Citizens are helping monarch butterflies survive by tagging them before migration, counting caterpillars, and sharing their findings with scientists online."
THE FIX
After you plan your three body paragraphs, go back to your introduction and name all three topics in your thesis. Use this template:

[Main idea] by [topic 1], [topic 2], and [topic 3].

For this essay: "Citizens are helping monarchs survive by tagging them before migration, counting caterpillars, and sharing their findings online."
SO WHAT?
A thesis that previews your topics earns points in two rubric areas: structure (shows your essay is planned) and organization (helps the reader follow your argument). One sentence, double the payoff.

Transitions and Fragments

Picking the right connecting word + a fragment in your essay

Question 9 asked you to connect these two sentences:

Sentence 1: "Fewer butterflies are surviving their trips."
Sentence 2: "People are taking action to protect them."

You chose Meanwhile. The correct answer was However.

The key: Sentence 1 is bad news and Sentence 2 is good news. That's a contrast. "However" signals contrast. "Meanwhile" signals two things happening at the same time — it doesn't capture the shift from bad to good.

Which transition best connects these sentences?
"The journey south is long and dangerous for monarchs. _____, thousands of butterflies complete the trip every year."
A As a result
B However
Transition Quick Reference
Contrast However • On the other hand • In contrast • Nevertheless
Time Meanwhile • Afterward • Next • Then • Finally
Cause As a result • Therefore • Because of this • Consequently
Addition Additionally • Furthermore • In addition • Also

One more thing: Your essay also has a fragment in body paragraph 1:

FRAGMENT
"Aswel as letting scientists figure out migration paths."

This phrase can't stand alone as a sentence. It needs to be attached to the sentence before it: "This deed can help scientists track how many monarchs are making the full trip as well as figure out migration paths."

QUICK CHECK
Phrases starting with "as well as," "such as," "including," or "along with" are connecting phrases, not sentences. They always need to be joined to a main sentence — never standing alone with a period.

PROOFREADING STRATEGY
Read your essay backwards, one sentence at a time. Start with the last sentence, then the second-to-last, and so on. When you read forward, your brain auto-corrects errors. Reading backwards forces you to see each word on its own.

Focus especially on words you use often: their, people, before, because, species, scientists. These are the words that trip you up most.
Why doubled letters matter
When you add -ing or -ed to a short word that ends in a consonant, you usually double the last letter:

tag → tagging
submit → submitting
stop → stopped

If the word already ends in two consonants or a silent e, you don't double: jump → jumping, write → writing.

The error types are the same — swapped letters and missing doubled letters. Backwards proofreading is the single highest-value habit you can build right now.
