Fragments vs. Complete Sentences

Q1 — the one MCQ you missed

You chose this as a complete sentence:

YOUR ANSWER
"Because characters often face big problems."

The problem: This starts with "Because" — that makes it a dependent clause. It depends on a main clause to be complete. By itself, it's a fragment.

A complete sentence needs: subject + verb + complete thought. "Because characters often face big problems" has a subject and verb, but the "because" makes the thought incomplete — the reader is left asking "what happens because of that?"

CORRECT ANSWER
"Animated movies are fun and colorful."

This is a complete sentence: subject (animated movies) + verb (are) + complete thought (fun and colorful). No dangling "because" — it stands on its own.
QUICK TEST
If a sentence starts with because, since, although, when, if, or unless and there's no main clause after it, it's a fragment. These words create dependent clauses — they need a main clause to form a complete sentence.

Fragment: "Although the movie was long."
Complete: "Although the movie was long, the audience enjoyed every minute."

The Body Paragraph Pattern

How to build a strong body paragraph every time

Every body paragraph in a G6+ essay should follow this pattern:

Body Paragraph Formula
TS Topic Sentence — states the paragraph's main point (one life lesson)
E1 Evidence 1 — a specific fact, quote, or example from the passage
X1 Explanation 1 — explain why that evidence matters
E2 Evidence 2 — a second fact or example from the passage
X2 Explanation 2 — explain why that evidence matters
CS Concluding Sentence — wraps up the paragraph's main idea

Here's how your full essay should be planned:

G6 Essay Plan
INTRO Hook → Thesis: Animated movies teach life lessons such as bravery, kindness, and perseverance.
BP1 Bravery — TS → E1 (passage example) → X1 → E2 (passage example) → X2 → CS
BP2 Kindness — TS → E1 (passage example) → X1 → E2 (passage example) → X2 → CS
BP3 Perseverance — TS → E1 (passage example) → X1 → E2 (passage example) → X2 → CS
CONC Restate 3 life lessons → broader message about why these lessons matter

BP = Body Paragraph • E = Evidence • X = Explanation • TS = Topic Sentence • CS = Closing Sentence

MODEL BODY PARAGRAPH
[TS] One valuable life lesson that animated movies teach is bravery. [E1] According to the passage, animated characters often face dangerous challenges and must find the courage to protect the people they care about. [X1] This shows young viewers that being brave does not mean having no fear — it means choosing to act even when you are afraid. [E2] The passage also explains that these characters sometimes have to stand up to much stronger opponents to do what is right. [X2] This teaches children that courage is about doing the right thing, even when it is difficult or scary. [CS] By showing characters who face their fears, animated movies help children understand that bravery is something they can practice in everyday life.
THE KEY RULE
Every E (evidence) must be followed by an X (explanation). Evidence without explanation is just a list of facts. The explanation is where you show the reader why that evidence supports your point. Ask yourself: "So what? Why does this matter?"

PROOFREADING TRICK
Read your essay backwards, sentence by sentence (start with the last sentence, then the second-to-last, and so on). When you read forward, your brain auto-corrects errors. Reading backwards forces you to see each sentence on its own, which makes misspellings jump out.
Why "valuable" trips you up
Many people drop the second a in "valuable." Think of it this way: something of value → valuable. The word "value" is hiding inside "valuable." If you can remember that, you'll catch it every time.
