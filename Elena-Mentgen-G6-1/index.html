WHAT A DEVELOPED ESSAY PARAGRAPH LOOKS LIKE
Each body paragraph in a G6 essay needs at least 3–4 sentences:

• Topic sentence: state the point you are making
• Evidence: a specific detail or example from the passage
• Explanation: explain HOW that evidence supports your point
• Connection: link it back to the essay's main idea (perseverance)

A single sentence like "she got polio" is evidence, but without explanation it does not earn full credit. The grader needs to see you explain why that matters.
Which body paragraph about Wilma Rudolph is more developed?
A Wilma Rudolph got polio when she was young. It made her left leg weak. But she did not give up.
B When Wilma Rudolph was a young child, she got polio, which made her left leg so weak that doctors said she might never walk normally again. Instead of accepting that, Rudolph did physical exercises every single day until she could walk without her leg brace. This shows perseverance because she refused to let her illness define what she could accomplish, and she put in the daily work even when progress was slow.
THE "SO WHAT?" TEST
After you write a piece of evidence, ask yourself: "So what? Why does this matter?" Then write the answer. That answer IS your explanation sentence. If you wrote "she got polio," the "so what?" is: "This shows perseverance because even though her body was working against her, she chose to fight back every single day." That extra sentence is where the rubric points live.

Essay Structure — Thesis + Evidence + Conclusion

The blueprint for a complete G6 essay

A G6 essay that scores 16/20 or higher follows a clear structure. The grader is looking for 5 paragraphs: an introduction with a thesis, three body paragraphs with evidence, and a conclusion.

Essay Blueprint for the Wilma Rudolph Prompt
INTRO State your thesis: Wilma Rudolph showed perseverance by overcoming challenges that would have stopped most people.
BODY 1 Evidence #1: She had polio as a child and was told she might never walk — explain how she overcame it with daily exercise.
BODY 2 Evidence #2: She went from wearing a leg brace to becoming a runner — explain the years of work this took.
BODY 3 Evidence #3: She won three Olympic gold medals — explain how this proves that perseverance led to an extraordinary result.
CONCL Restate thesis in new words: Rudolph's story proves that refusing to give up, even when things seem impossible, can lead to greatness.

Notice: each body paragraph has one piece of evidence and one explanation of how that evidence connects to perseverance. Three body paragraphs, three pieces of evidence, three explanations. That is the structure the rubric rewards.

THE 3-MINUTE PLAN
Before you start writing the essay, take 3 minutes to jot down:

• Thesis: one sentence stating your main claim
• Evidence 1: one word or phrase reminding you what to write about
• Evidence 2: one word or phrase
• Evidence 3: one word or phrase

For the Wilma Rudolph prompt, that might look like: "Thesis: perseverance. E1: polio/exercises. E2: brace to runner. E3: Olympic gold." That plan takes 2 minutes and ensures you write a complete essay with all three body paragraphs.
Which essay plan will produce a higher-scoring response?
A Plan: Write about Wilma Rudolph having polio. Explain that she was brave.
B Plan: Thesis = perseverance through challenges. E1 = polio + daily exercises. E2 = leg brace to running. E3 = Olympic gold medals. Conclusion = refusing to quit leads to greatness.
WHY PLANNING SAVES TIME
It feels like planning "wastes" time because you are not writing yet. But students who plan for 3 minutes and then write for 20 minutes produce longer, more organized essays than students who start writing immediately and run out of steam after 5 minutes. The plan gives your brain a roadmap so the writing flows faster.
