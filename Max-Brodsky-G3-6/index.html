Q2 Fix — Keep All the Details

When you combine sentences, do not drop any information.
📈 You scored 100% on combining sentences on G3.2 and G3.3 — you know how to do this. On this question, you got the structure right but left out one detail. That is the one thing to watch for.

The question asked you to combine these two sentences using "because":

Sentence 13They have strong claws and rough paw pads.
Sentence 14They can hold on to tree branches tightly.
Which combined sentence keeps ALL the information from both sentences?
A They can hold on to tree branches tightly, because they have strong claws.
B They can hold on to tree branches tightly, because they have strong claws and rough paw pads.

Here is what happened:

YOU WROTE
"They can hold on to tree branches tightly, because they have strong claws."
COMPLETE VERSION
"They can hold on to tree branches tightly, because they have strong claws and rough paw pads."

Your sentence structure was right! The only problem was dropping "rough paw pads." When combining sentences, every detail from the original sentences needs to show up in the new one.

THE "CHECKLIST" TRICK
After you write a combined sentence, look back at the original sentences. Check off each detail — can you find it in your new sentence? If anything is missing, add it back in.
See it in action
Original: "The dog has soft fur and floppy ears."
Combined: "The dog is cute, because it has soft fur." (Missing "floppy ears"!)
Fixed: "The dog is cute, because it has soft fur and floppy ears." (All details included!)

Q6 Fix — Small Grammar and Spelling Slips

Your answer was strong — just two small fixes needed.

The question asked: "Why does a porcupine lift its quills when it feels scared?"

Which sentence has correct grammar and spelling?
A A porcupine lift its quills when it feels scared, because it wants to scare off preditors.
B A porcupine lifts its quills when it feels scared, because it wants to scare off predators.
YOU WROTE
"A porcupine lift its quills when it feels scared, because it wants to scare off preditors."
CORRECTED VERSION
"A porcupine lifts its quills when it feels scared, because it wants to scare off predators."

Two small fixes:

lift → lifts
preditors → predators
VERB MATCHING RULE
When the subject is one thing (like "a porcupine" or "the dog"), the verb gets an -s at the end:
• "A porcupine lifts" • "The dog runs" • "She reads"

Quick test: if you can replace the subject with "he" or "she," add the -s.
More examples
"The bird flies south." (one bird = "it flies")
"Birds fly south." (many birds = "they fly")
"A porcupine lifts its quills." (one porcupine = "it lifts")

Q9 Fix — Make Your Reason Stronger

Good idea, but the reason needs to be more accurate.

The question asked: "What makes porcupines special animals?"

Which answer gives a stronger, more accurate reason?
A A porcupine's quills make them special, because no other animal has them.
B A porcupine's quills make them special, because they can have over 30,000 quills that protect them from predators.
YOU WROTE
"A porcupines quills make them special, because no other animal has them."
STRONGER VERSION
"A porcupine's quills make them special, because they can have over 30,000 quills that protect them from predators."

Two things to fix:

  • "A porcupines" needs an apostrophe → "A porcupine's" (the quills belong to the porcupine)
  • "No other animal has them" is not accurate enough. A stronger reason uses a specific fact from the passage — like how many quills they have or how the quills work.
THE "SO WHAT?" TEST
After you write your reason, ask yourself: "Is this actually true? Can I back it up with a fact from the passage?"

If your reason is too general or not quite right, go back to the passage and grab a specific detail. Facts from the passage are always stronger than guesses.
Apostrophe reminder
When something belongs to someone or something, add 's:
• "the porcupine's quills" (the quills belong to the porcupine)
• "the dog's tail" (the tail belongs to the dog)
• "Max's pencil" (the pencil belongs to Max)

3-MINUTE PROOFREADING RULE
Save the last 3 minutes of your test for proofreading. Read your paragraph backward (last sentence first) — when you read forward, your brain skips over mistakes. Reading backward forces you to see each sentence on its own.
Tricky words to practice
These words show up a lot in writing tests. Practice spelling them:
• than (comparing things) vs. then (what happens next)
• actually (act-u-al-ly)
• protect (pro-tect, not proc-tect)
• enemies (en-e-mies)
• predators (pred-a-tors, not pred-i-tors)
