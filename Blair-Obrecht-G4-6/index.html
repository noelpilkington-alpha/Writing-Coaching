Appositive vs. Participle Phrase

Q3 — 1 out of 2

On Q3, the question asked you to combine two sentences using an appositive. You used a participle phrase instead, which is a different structure.

YOU WROTE
"Garrett Morgan built an early traffic signal, helping save lives and reduce crashes."

The problem: "Helping save lives and reduce crashes" is a participle phrase (it starts with an -ing verb). An appositive is a naming phrase that renames or describes a noun.

What is an appositive?

An appositive is a noun phrase placed next to another noun to rename or describe it. It is set off with commas.

Participle: "...signal, helping save lives"
Appositive: "...signal, a device that saved lives and reduced crashes"
Tap to see a correct appositive
Look for the naming phrase after the comma
CORRECT APPOSITIVE
"Garrett Morgan built an early traffic signal, a life-saving device that reduced crashes."

"A life-saving device that reduced crashes" is a noun phrase that renames the traffic signal. That is an appositive.

HOW TO TELL THEM APART
Appositive = a naming phrase (starts with a/an/the + noun):
• "My dog, a golden retriever, loves to swim."
• "Garrett Morgan, an inventor from Cleveland, changed road safety."

Participle phrase = starts with an -ing or -ed verb:
• "The signal, flashing yellow, warned drivers to slow down."
• "Morgan, hoping to prevent crashes, built a new device."

Quick test: Can you replace the phrase with "who is/was ___"? If yes, it is an appositive.
More appositive examples
• "The traffic light, a three-color signal, tells drivers when to stop."
• "Cleveland, a city in Ohio, was Morgan's home."
• "His invention, the first electric traffic signal, saved many lives."

Notice: each appositive is a noun phrase that renames the word before it.

Spelling + Verb Tense

Q4 — 1.5 out of 2  •  Q6 — 1 out of 3

Two questions lost points because of spelling mistakes and a verb tense error. These are fixable with a quick proofread habit.

Q4 — Spelling error:

YOU WROTE
"Cars, wagons, bicycles, and people all moved at the same time, so crashes happend often."
"happend" → "happened"

The past tense of "happen" adds -ed, keeping both letters: happen + ed = happened.

Q6 — Spelling + tense errors:

YOU WROTE
"Garrett Morgan notices a problom and works to fix it."
"problom" → "problem"
"notices" (present tense) → "noticed" (past tense)

The passage describes events that already happened, so your answer should use past tense: "noticed," "worked." Also, the correct spelling is problem (not "problom").

PROOFREAD HABIT: TENSE + SPELLING
After you finish writing, do two quick checks:

1. Tense check: If the passage is about something that already happened, every verb in your answer should be past tense (-ed endings).
2. Spelling check: Read your answer backward, word by word. This forces your brain to look at each word individually instead of skipping over errors.

Common words to watch: happened, problem, because, different, really.

Answer the LESSON, Not Just the Topic

Q6 — 1 out of 3

Q6 asked: what lesson does the passage teach? Your answer was too generic. You wrote that Morgan "notices a problem and works to fix it," but that could describe anyone doing anything. It does not name the specific lesson from this passage.

YOU WROTE
"Garrett Morgan notices a problom and works to fix it."

The problem: This tells us the topic (Morgan fixing something) but not the lesson. A lesson is what the reader can learn or take away from the story.

Topic vs. Lesson:

Topic: "He saw a problem and fixed it"
Lesson: "One person's invention can save thousands of lives when they solve a problem everyone else ignores"
Tap to see a lesson-focused answer
Notice: it names what the reader learns
LESSON-FOCUSED ANSWER
"The passage teaches that paying attention to everyday dangers, like chaotic intersections, can lead to inventions that save lives for generations."

This names the specific lesson: paying attention to everyday dangers leads to life-saving inventions. It is not just "he fixed a problem."

TOPIC vs. LESSON
Topic = what the passage is about (traffic signals, Garrett Morgan)
Lesson = what the reader learns from the story

When a question asks for the lesson, your answer should finish this sentence:
"This passage teaches the reader that ___."

If your answer could apply to any passage about any person solving any problem, it is too generic. Add details that are specific to this passage.
Another way to check
After writing your answer, ask: "Could someone read my answer and know which passage I read?" If the answer is no, your response is too generic. Add a detail from the passage that makes it specific. For this passage, that means naming intersections, traffic signals, or the danger of crashes.

Paragraph: Detail + Explanation

Q11 — 17 out of 20 — strong, can develop further

Your paragraph scored 17/20, which is solid work. To push toward 19-20, you need to make sure every detail from the passage is followed by an explanation sentence that connects it back to your main point.

What is Detail + Explanation?

Every time you give a fact from the passage, follow it with a sentence that explains why that fact matters to your main idea about traffic signals and Garrett Morgan.

G4 Paragraph Plan: TS → D + E → D + E → D + E → CS
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail from the passage  •  E = Explanation (why it matters)  •  CS = Concluding Sentence
TS Garrett Morgan's traffic signal changed road safety by solving a problem that was injuring and killing people every day.
D Before traffic signals, cars, wagons, bicycles, and people all moved through intersections at the same time.
E This meant that crashes happened constantly because no one knew whose turn it was to go.
D Morgan witnessed a terrible crash at an intersection and decided to invent a solution.
E This shows that his invention came from real experience, not just an idea in his head.
D His signal used three positions: stop, go, and an all-stop that let people cross safely.
E The third position was the key improvement because it gave everyone a safe moment to cross without any vehicles moving.
CS Morgan's simple invention proved that one person paying attention to a daily danger can save thousands of lives.
DETAIL + EXPLANATION IN ACTION
After each detail, ask yourself: "Why does this matter to my main point?"
Your answer to that question is your Explanation sentence.

Detail alone: "Morgan saw a crash at an intersection."
Detail + Explanation: "Morgan saw a crash at an intersection. This experience motivated him to invent the traffic signal because he realized no system existed to keep people safe."
