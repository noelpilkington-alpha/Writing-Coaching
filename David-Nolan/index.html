Q5: Subordinating Conjunctions

Words like Although, Since, and Because

The question asked you to start the sentence with a subordinating conjunction:

Original
Anna was sad to see Sally go.
YOU WROTE
Sadly, Anna watched Sally swim away.

You had the right feeling — you knew the sentence was about sadness. But "Sadly" is an adverb, not a subordinating conjunction. A subordinating conjunction connects two ideas and shows how they relate.

WITH A SUBORDINATING CONJUNCTION
Although Anna was sad to see Sally go, she was happy the seal was back with her family.
THE SUBORDINATING CONJUNCTION PATTERN
A subordinating conjunction starts a dependent clause — a piece that can't stand on its own. It always pairs with an independent clause to make a complete sentence:

[Subordinating conjunction] + [dependent clause], [independent clause].

Common subordinating conjunctions: Although, Because, Since, When, If, After, Before, Until

• Although Anna was sad, she knew it was the right thing to do.
• Because Sally was a wild animal, she needed to return to the ocean.
• Since the seal was healthy, the family decided to set her free.

Q3: Appositives Need Two Commas

One comma to open, one comma to close

You added an appositive to your sentence — great job knowing what one is! But you left out the closing comma:

YOU WROTE
Sally, the cute and gentle baby seal swam to the other seals.

You have the comma before the appositive ("Sally,") but not after it. Without the closing comma, the sentence runs the description right into the verb and gets confusing.

Tap to see the improved version
Read the explanation above first
WITH BOTH COMMAS
Sally, the cute and gentle baby seal, swam to the other seals.
THE TWO-COMMA RULE
An appositive in the middle of a sentence always needs two commas — one to open and one to close. Think of the commas as parentheses:

Name, [appositive], rest of sentence.

• Anna, the oldest child, spotted the seal first.
• The fishnet, a tangled mess, was wrapped around the pup.
• Sally, the cute and gentle baby seal, swam to the other seals.

If the appositive comes at the end of the sentence, you only need one comma: "They rescued Sally, a baby seal."

Q10: Explain the Lesson, Not the Story

What should the reader learn?

The question asked: "What lesson does this story teach?"

YOU WROTE
It teaches carring for things that can't help it's self.

You're close — you're reaching for the right idea about caring. But there are two problems:

1. This describes the story more than the lesson. A lesson is a big takeaway the reader should remember — something that applies beyond just this story.

2. Spelling and grammar errors:

carring → caring
it's self → itself

"It's" means "it is." The possessive form is its (no apostrophe). And "itself" is one word, not two.

Tap to see the improved version
Read the explanation above first
LESSON, NOT STORY
This story teaches that we should help animals that can't help themselves, because the family's kindness gave Sally a chance to return to her family.
STORY vs. LESSON
Story: "It's about a family that helps a baby seal." (What happened)
Lesson: "We should help animals that can't help themselves." (What we learn)

Ask yourself: "Could this lesson apply to a completely different situation?" If yes, you've found the lesson. If it only describes what happened in this story, you're summarizing the plot.

Plan Before You Write

Use a paragraph plan to organize your ideas

Your paragraph scored 18/20 — you included tons of great details. The main issue is that your opening sentence doesn't answer the prompt directly. In AlphaWrite, you learn to make an outline before you write. On a test, you can do a quick paragraph plan:

Your Opening
"Pokemon has been very popular throughout the years."

This is true, but the prompt asks you to explain how Tajiri's childhood influenced Pokémon. Your first sentence should answer that directly.

Paragraph Plan — "How Tajiri's childhood influenced Pokémon"
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail from the article  •  W = Why it matters  •  CS = Concluding Sentence
TS Satoshi Tajiri's love of catching bugs as a kid led him to create Pokémon.
D1 As a boy, Tajiri loved catching bugs — his friends gave him the nickname "Dr. Bug."
W This shows how much he loved collecting creatures, which became the main idea behind Pokémon.
D2 He turned his bug-catching hobby into a video game where players could catch Pokémon.
W This matters because without that childhood memory, the game might never have been created.
D3 Pokémon became huge — millions of copies sold, plus a card game, app, TV show, and movie.
W This shows that one kid's hobby turned into something millions of people enjoy worldwide.
CS Tajiri's childhood hobby turned into one of the most popular games and shows ever made.

You actually included most of these details — you just needed to start with a topic sentence that answers the prompt and add "why it matters" sentences after each detail.

THE TOPIC SENTENCE TRICK
Read the prompt, then turn it into your first sentence:

Prompt: "Explain how Satoshi Tajiri's childhood influenced the creation of Pokémon."
Topic sentence: "Satoshi Tajiri's childhood love of catching bugs influenced him to create Pokémon."

See? You just flip the prompt into a statement. Then your details come after.
