THE 90% ESSAY FORMULA
Every body paragraph should have this five-part shape:

1. Topic sentence — states the body paragraph's focus.
2. Evidence 1 — specific detail from the passage.
3. Explanation — "This shows..." / "This proves..." / "In other words..."
4. Evidence 2 — second specific detail, or a quote from the passage.
5. Tie-back — one sentence that connects both details back to the thesis.

That shape works for every G6 expository essay. Once you lock it in, the 5-point "evidence and explanation" row becomes 5/5.

The 4-Step G6 Essay Process

Read, plan, draft, revise — in that order, every time

Rocco, at G6 the writing test gives you room for a real essay — not a paragraph. The strongest G6 writers don't just start writing: they work through four stages, and they spend real attention on each one.

Step 1
Read
Read the passage twice. Mark 4–5 facts.
Step 2
Plan
Thesis + 3 body topics + which facts go where.
Step 3
Draft
5 paragraphs — intro, 3 body, conclusion.
Step 4
Revise
Read your draft. Fix spelling, add missing evidence.

Most of the 90%+ essays happen because of Step 2. One minute spent planning saves five minutes of rewriting and adds a whole point to your score. Here is a quick planning template you can copy in your head:

The 1-Minute G6 Essay Plan
THESIS The main claim your essay will prove (1 sentence)
BODY 1 First reason + 2 facts from the passage
BODY 2 Second reason + 2 facts from the passage
BODY 3 Third reason + 2 facts from the passage
CLOSE Tie back to the thesis in a new way
Which approach is more likely to score 19–20 out of 20?
A Start writing immediately. Come up with each body paragraph as you go. Stop when you think you are done.
B Read the passage twice and mark facts. Jot a thesis and 3 body topics. Draft 5 paragraphs in order. Revise: fix spelling and add one missing detail.
WHAT "REVISE" MEANS AT G6
The revise step is NOT optional. Before you submit, read every paragraph once and check:
• Does each body paragraph have two facts from the passage? If not, add one.
• Does each body paragraph have a "this shows" or "this proves" sentence? If not, add one.
• Does the conclusion reuse the thesis in new words? If not, rewrite it.
• Any spelling or grammar slips? Fix them.

Revise takes a few minutes and is worth 2–3 rubric points.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Every 90%+ G6 essay in history was planned before it was drafted. The four-step process above is how you turn a 16/20 essay into a 19/20 essay — not by writing more, but by writing the right things in the right order.

Evidence + Analysis

The one sentence that unlocks 4 points per essay

Rocco, here is the single sentence that separates a 15/20 G6 essay from a 19/20. It is called the analysis sentence — the one that comes right after you present evidence. It answers the question: "So what? Why does this evidence matter for my thesis?"

Evidence + Analysis Starters
EVIDENCE "According to the passage..." / "The article states..."
ANALYSIS "This shows that..." / "This proves..." / "In other words..." / "This is significant because..."

The analysis sentence is where the grader gives you credit for thinking about your evidence, not just copying it. Two examples from what a Mithi River essay might include:

WITHOUT ANALYSIS (partial credit)
"The article states that the water was twenty percent cleaner than before. This was good for the community."
The evidence is named, but the second sentence just says "this was good" — no actual analysis of why.
WITH ANALYSIS (full credit)
"The article states that the water was twenty percent cleaner than before. This shows that the cleanup worked not just as a symbolic action but as a measurable success — the community could point to real data proving their effort made the river safer."
Same evidence, but now the follow-up sentence explains what the 20% improvement proves about the main idea (teamwork works).
Which sentence is a proper analysis follow-up to a piece of evidence?
A This shows that the volunteers did a lot of work and that was helpful.
B This shows that community-led effort can produce real environmental change, not just good intentions — the data proves the volunteers' work mattered.
THE "SO WHAT" TEST
After every evidence sentence, imagine the grader asking: "So what? Why does that matter for your thesis?"

Your next sentence answers that question. If you can't answer it, the evidence is either the wrong evidence or the analysis sentence is missing. Don't move on until you have written it.

Rule of thumb: Evidence = 1 rubric point. Analysis of that evidence = 1 more rubric point. Skip analysis and you are leaving half the evidence row on the table.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
You have 3 body paragraphs per essay. If each one has 2 pieces of evidence with 2 analysis sentences, that is 12 content sentences in the body of your essay. Your G6.4 essay had about 7. Closing that gap — from 7 to 12 — is the jump from 16/20 to 19/20.
