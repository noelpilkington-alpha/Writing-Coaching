The Missing Apostrophes

Three slips in one paragraph — all catchable in one read-back

Jaiden, all three of your spelling slips landed in the same place: the 20-point paragraph. Here they are, straight from your own writing:

FROM YOUR G3.2 PARAGRAPH
“Thats where the Camel Mobile Library comes in…”
“…because they dont have books.”
“…I think more people should dop it.”

The first two are the same rule. That's and don't are contractions — two words squeezed into one — and the apostrophe stands exactly where the missing letters used to be: that is → that's, do not → don't. The third one, dop, is just a finger slip for do — the kind of typo everyone makes and only a read-back catches.

#1 Fix: Statement or Command?

Q4 — 0 out of 2 — your only miss in the first ten questions

Jaiden, Question 4 showed you this sentence and asked what type it is:

QUESTION 4 — 0 OUT OF 2
“Workers spent over 30 minutes clearing the ice.”
You answered imperative. It is declarative — and here is how to never mix those two up again.
THE FOUR SENTENCE TYPES
Declarative — tells you something. Ends with a period. “Workers spent 30 minutes clearing the ice.”
Interrogative — asks something. Ends with a question mark. “How long did it take?”
Imperative — gives a command. Speaks straight AT you. “Clear the ice.”
Exclamatory — bursts with feeling. Ends with an exclamation point. “What a night that was!”

The trap you fell into: the sentence talks about workers doing something, so “command” feels close. Here is the test that settles it every time: a command has no named subject — it is aimed at YOU (“[You] Clear the ice”). The moment a sentence names who did it (“Workers spent…”), it is telling you a fact — declarative.

Which of these is the imperative sentence?
AThe fans threw teddy bears onto the ice.
BThrow your teddy bear onto the ice.
YOUR TWO-SECOND CHECK
When a question asks for the sentence type, ask: “Who is the subject?” A named subject doing something = declarative. No subject, aimed at you = imperative. Question mark = interrogative. Exclamation point = exclamatory. Those 2 points are yours from now on.

YOUR PARAGRAPH, DEVELOPED (closer to 20/20)

“The Camel Mobile Library has helped many people in Garissa. Many people in Garissa move a lot, so a traditional library would not be good for them. That's where the Camel Mobile Library comes in. This mobile library moves around to places where people camp and gives them free books to read, because they don't have books of their own. That changes what is possible for the readers: families who might never see a library can keep reading and learning no matter where they move. This is a huge act of kindness, and I think more people should do it.”

One bolded sentence is the whole upgrade: it answers “so what happened for the people?” — which is exactly what the prompt asked. The rest is the proofread from earlier in this card set: That's, don't, do, and a period where the comma splice was.

SO WHAT?
Your paragraph already explains the how. On your next test, after you explain how something works, add one sentence that starts like “That changes…” or “Because of this…” and tells the reader what is different now. That single habit is worth several points on every paragraph prompt — and the paragraph is where your last five points went.
