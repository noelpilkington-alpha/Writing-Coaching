#1 Fix: “Because” Is Not an Answer

Q7, Q8 and Q9 — five points, one habit, and your own Q10 already has the fix

Read the first word of your answers to Q7, Q8 and Q9:

THREE ANSWERS, ONE OPENING
“Because over time as you practice you get better…”
“Because without those tiny details the project would not be the same thing.”
“because sometimes working with someone can be beneficial…”
Each one jumps straight to the reason and never states the thing the reason is for. A “because” clause on its own is not a complete sentence — it is half of one, waiting for the other half. Q8 and Q9 are exactly that: the reason with no statement attached, which is why they scored 1 out of 3 each.

Now look at the one you got completely right, two questions later:

Q10 — 3 OUT OF 3 (YOURS)
“They might feel proud of themselves because their hard work was not for nothing.”
Statement first — they might feel proud — then because, then the reason. That is the whole shape. You know it. You used it on Q10 and Q6 and skipped it on the three in between.

THE CHECK
After you write a three-point answer, look at the noun after “because.” If it is thing, stuff, it, or your life, you have described nothing yet. Swap in a person, an object or a number — the passage always has one ready.

Your Paragraph: 15 out of 20

A real idea, no article — and your May paragraph shows exactly what was missing

The question: Why do many business experts believe that a recognizable logo is valuable? — with evidence from the article. Here is what you wrote:

“I think many business experts believe a recognizable logo is valuable because it will cause people to be more familiar with the logo so the company is well known. When the company is more well known they will be popular and have more customers.”
A real central idea in sentence one A chain of reasoning: familiar → well known → customers No evidence from the article Two sentences — ends as it starts “I think” hedges the answer

The idea here is correct and it is yours. A recognizable logo makes people familiar with a company, familiarity makes the company well known, and well known means customers. That is a chain, not a list, and it answers the question. The problem is that a reader has no way to tell you read the article. The question said “use evidence from the selection,” and there is none — no fact, no quote, no “according to.”

YOUR PARAGRAPH, DEVELOPED

“Many business experts believe a recognizable logo is valuable because it makes people familiar with a company, and familiarity is what makes a company well known. According to the article ‘What Can We See in a Logo?’, companies spend years developing logos that people can identify instantly. This shows how much a moment of recognition is worth to them. When a company is well known, people trust it, and the article explains that customers are more likely to choose a familiar logo over an unknown brand — which means more customers. The article also points out that logos use colors and shapes to send a message without any words, so a good logo is doing the company’s talking before anyone reads its name. That is why experts treat a recognizable logo as one of a company’s most valuable assets.”

Your chain — familiar, well known, customers — is still the spine. Three facts from the article were hung on it, each followed by what it shows, and a closing sentence finished the thought. “I think” came off the front, because the experts’ belief is the fact you were asked to explain, not your opinion.

SO WHAT?
The five paragraph points are the same five every time: “According to the article…” plus a specific fact, plus one sentence saying what it shows. You wrote exactly that in May. Write it again and this paragraph is back at 17 or 18 — and with the three short answers fixed, the test is past 90.
