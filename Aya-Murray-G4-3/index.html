THE RULE: THE FIRST SENTENCE ANSWERS THE QUESTION IN ITS OWN WORDS, THEN THE EVIDENCE IS SORTED UNDER THAT ANSWER
The question asked how Yacouba’s ideas and character helped. A TOPIC SENTENCE for it names the idea and names the character trait. Then each half gets one DETAIL · SHOW from the article and a DETAIL · SO WHAT: This shows plus what the detail proves about the idea or the character. The CONCLUDING SENTENCE puts the two halves together. Telling the events in order is a retelling. It can score 16. It cannot score 20, because the reader has to do the sorting. The check: are the key words of the question in your first sentence?

Fix 2: A Boundary Between Complete Thoughts

Q9 here. Five answers on G4.2, three on G4.1. Points on five of your seven tests
THE RULE: WHERE ONE COMPLETE THOUGHT ENDS AND ANOTHER BEGINS, A COMMA ALONE IS NOT ENOUGH, AND NOTHING AT ALL IS A RUN-ON
You write long, fluent sentences, and the ideas in them are good. The points go at the seams. Two complete thoughts need one of three things between them: a period, a comma plus and, but or so, or a joining word such as because, which or when that turns one of them into a part. On the one-sentence questions a run-on or a comma splice costs the conventions point every time, with no second chance for good ideas. The check: at every comma, cover it with your finger. Can the words on both sides each stand alone as a sentence? Then the comma needs a partner.

THE RULE: READ THE INSTRUCTION TWICE, DO THAT ONE JOB IN THE PLAINEST WAY, AND CHANGE NOTHING ELSE
Half the points on an editing question are for the exact skill named. Each of your three misses did something more interesting than the job: a new verb, an appositive, a describing clause. Expand the fragment means the subject gets a verb it does. The check: after you type, read the instruction once more and point to the exact place where you did what it names. Then check that the rest of the sentence is still the original.
