Finish Every Sentence

A sentence needs a subject AND a main verb that finishes the thought
💡
What happened on Q10: the question asked for one sentence about a skill that could help others. You wrote a great topic — but the sentence never finished the thought, so it counted as a fragment. That was the one short answer that lost points.
Q10 — your answer (1 out of 3 points)
The skill of giving and reciving feedback so others can give feedback and use it too.

Why it is a fragment: "The skill of giving and receiving feedback…" names a topic, but it never says what the skill does. There is no main verb finishing the thought. It is like starting to say "My favorite thing about school…" and stopping — the listener is still waiting for the rest.

A COMPLETE VERSION
One skill I have is giving and receiving feedback, so I can help others improve their work and use it too. Now there is a subject ("One skill I have") and a main verb ("is") that finishes the thought.

You are already good at this when a question asks for it — on Q2 and Q4 you turned fragments into complete sentences and earned full credit. The next step is catching the fragments you write on your own. One quick check on Q11 shows the same idea: "as the article go on" should be "goes on" so the verb matches the subject.

Which one is a complete sentence?
AOne skill I have is giving and receiving feedback.
BThe skill of giving and receiving feedback so others can use it too.
THE RULE
Every sentence needs a subject and a main verb that finishes the thought. When you start with a topic like "The skill of…" ask: what does it do? Add "is / helps / lets me…" to finish it.

Appositives & Precision

You already use appositives — now punctuate them

An appositive is a little phrase that renames the noun right before it, and it is set off with a comma on each side. You already do this well — on Q3 you wrote it correctly:

Q3 — you did this right (full credit)
Peter Tabichi, a teacher, is from Kenya, Africa, and teaches high school science. The commas around "a teacher" set off the appositive perfectly.

The next level is remembering those commas every time. In your Q11 paragraph, the same kind of phrase appeared — but this time without its commas:

Q11 — the appositive is missing its commas
Cody one of the main charaters in Surprise Kick has changed opinions over time…
THE FIX
Cody, one of the main characters, in Surprise Kick has changed his opinions over time. A comma before and after "one of the main characters" sets it off cleanly.
Which sentence sets off the appositive correctly?
ACody, one of the main characters, changed his opinion of Mud.
BCody one of the main characters changed his opinion of Mud.
THE RULE
An appositive renames the noun right before it. Set it off with a comma on both sides: Cody, one of the main characters, … The commas are like handles that lift the extra detail out of the sentence.

Cody, one of the main characters in Surprise Kick, has changed his opinions over time as the article goes on, but how did he change them? Well, at the beginning of the article he thought that Mud was slow and that he could help him. Then he saw how fast Mud was and scored a goal, and he was stunned at his moves. Then Cody spent the rest of the game chasing the ball but never managed to catch up. So Cody probably now thinks that Mud is fast, based on what he saw. That is how Cody changed his opinions in the article Surprise Kick.

Notice: your ideas, order, and evidence did not change at all. The only fixes were the appositive commas, capitalizing "Cody," a few spellings, and "go on" → "goes on." That is what takes a strong paragraph to a top one.
