Fix 1: Answer the Question That Was Asked

Q11: a paragraph about camels, for a question about people
THE RULE: THE POINT, THEN THE HOW, THEN ONLY FACTS THAT BELONG
The first sentence has two parts. POINT answers the question in its own words. HOW names the two or three reasons the paragraph will prove. Then every fact has to belong to one of the hows, and every fact has to be on the page. The check: read the question again, then your first sentence. Is it an answer to that question? Then, for each fact, point to the how it belongs to and point to it in the article. A fact that fails either check stays out, however interesting.

Fix 3: Do the Job the Instruction Names

Q4: name the type. Q10: use because, but or so. Both answers did a different job
THE RULE: FIND THE JOB WORD, THEN THE WORDS YOU WERE GIVEN
Every Q1 to Q5 instruction names a job and sometimes hands you the exact words to use. What type of sentence means name one of the four types. Use because, but, or so means one of those three, not and. The check: after you write, find the job word in the instruction, then find it in your answer.

The four sentence types, with the test for each:

DECLARATIVEWorkers spent over 30 minutes clearing the ice.
Tells something. Has a doer (workers) and ends with a period.
INTERROGATIVEDid workers spend over 30 minutes clearing the ice?
Asks. Ends with a question mark.
IMPERATIVEClear the ice before the next period.
Gives an order. Starts with the action word; the doer, you, is left unsaid.
EXCLAMATORYWhat a shower of teddy bears that was!
Shows strong feeling. Ends with an exclamation mark.
YOU WROTE (Q4, 1 OUT OF 2)
“It is an imperative sentence.” WRONG TYPE
Workers spent over 30 minutes clearing the ice has a doer, workers, and a period. It tells; it does not order anyone to do anything. Declarative.

Fix 4: Two Sentences Never Share One Comma

Q8 on this test, Q5 on the last. And no comma before because
THE RULE: COVER THE COMMA
Cover the comma and read the words on each side. If both sides could stand alone as sentences, the comma cannot hold them together by itself: add and, but, so or because next to it, or make two sentences. If the right side starts with because, the comma goes: because joins without one. The check: every comma you type, cover it and read both sides.
YOU WROTE (Q8, 2 OUT OF 3)
“I would love to attend a Teddy Bear Toss event, it seems like a really fun event and it helps local charities.” TWO SENTENCES, ONE COMMA
Cover the comma. Left: I would love to attend a Teddy Bear Toss event. Right: it seems like a really fun event. Both stand alone. The idea earned its two points; the comma cost the third.
WITH BECAUSE DOING THE JOINING
I would love to attend a Teddy Bear Toss event BECAUSEbecause it seems like a really fun event and it helps local charities.

YOU WROTE (Q2 AND Q6, FULL MARKS, ONE HABIT TO DROP)
“Fans tossed bears onto the ice, because the Bears scored a goal.”   “…is popular, because it is a fun and engaging event…”
No points lost this time. In the paragraph, where conventions are five points, a comma before every because adds up. Because joins on its own.

Where commas go, and where they do not:

KEEPThe ice was covered in bears, so the players skated among them.
Both sides stand alone, and so is beside the comma.
KEEPAs soon as the Bears scored, fans tossed the bears onto the ice.
An opening part that cannot stand alone gets a comma after it.
NO COMMAFans tossed bears onto the ice because the Bears scored a goal.
Because joins without a comma.
TWO SENTENCES, ONE COMMAThe event sells out every year, fans look forward to it.
Cover the comma: both sides stand alone, no joining word. Add and, or make two sentences.
