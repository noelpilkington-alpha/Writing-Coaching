Q5 Fix — Adding Specific Details

Can you spot the difference? Then we'll break it down.
📈 You got "adding details to sentences" questions right on 4 straight tests (G6.1 through G6.5) — but missed it here on G6.6. Your history proves you CAN do this. The pattern: this time you picked a version that only added one vague word ("carefully") instead of real, specific detail.

The question gave you a short sentence (called a "kernel"): "The dog listens."

Your job was to add real, specific detail to make it longer and more informative. Which version does that?

Tap the one that actually adds new information:
A "The dog listens carefully to the student."
B "The golden retriever listens calmly as the third-grader reads aloud from a picture book."

Now here's why this matters:

Vague Addition (restates the idea) Specific Expansion (adds real detail)
"The dog listens carefully to the student." "The golden retriever listens calmly as the third-grader reads aloud from a picture book."

The specific version answers WHAT KIND?, WHO?, and WHAT? — that's what makes it a real expansion, not just a minor rewording.

THE EXPANSION CHECKLIST
When you need to add detail to a short sentence, run through: WHAT KIND? WHO? WHERE? WHEN? HOW? WHY?
If your longer version doesn't answer at least one of these with a specific answer, you've just said the same thing in slightly different words. Real details add new information the reader didn't already have.
See it in action with another example
Short sentence: "The student reads."
Vague: "The student reads quietly to the animal." (we already know they're reading — "quietly" and "animal" are too general)
Specific: "The nervous fifth-grader reads a chapter of Charlotte's Web to the therapy dog curled up beside her." (WHO + WHAT + WHERE)
SO WHAT? WHY THIS MATTERS FOR YOUR ESSAYS
This is the same skill that makes body paragraphs convincing. When you write evidence in an essay, specific details are always more persuasive than vague statements. Instead of "dogs help students," write "therapy dogs help nervous readers feel comfortable by sitting quietly and never judging mistakes."

Body Paragraph Building Blocks (use for each body paragraph)
Topic Sentence States the paragraph's main point — one clear reason or argument
Evidence A specific fact, quote, or example from the passage
Explain Why Why does that evidence matter? ("So what?")
More Evidence A second fact or example from the passage
Explain Why Why does THIS evidence matter?
Closing Sentence Wraps up the paragraph's main idea
KEEP DOING THIS
Your essays are using this pattern well — that's why your scores keep climbing. Every body paragraph should follow this structure: claim → evidence → explain why → more evidence → explain why → wrap up.
