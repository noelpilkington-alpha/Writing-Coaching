Q5 Fix — Adding Specific Details

Can you spot the difference? Then we'll break it down.
📈 You got "adding details to sentences" questions right on 4 straight tests (G6.1 through G6.5) — but missed it here on G6.6. Your history proves you CAN do this. The pattern: this time you picked a version that only added one vague word ("carefully") instead of real, specific detail.

The question gave you a short sentence (called a "kernel"): "The dog listens."

Your job was to add real, specific detail to make it longer and more informative. Which version does that?

Tap the one that actually adds new information:
A "The dog listens carefully to the student."
B "The golden retriever listens calmly as the third-grader reads aloud from a picture book."

Now here's why this matters:

Vague Addition (restates the idea) Specific Expansion (adds real detail)
"The dog listens carefully to the student." "The golden retriever listens calmly as the third-grader reads aloud from a picture book."

The specific version answers WHAT KIND?, WHO?, and WHAT? — that's what makes it a real expansion, not just a minor rewording.

THE EXPANSION CHECKLIST
When you need to add detail to a short sentence, run through: WHAT KIND? WHO? WHERE? WHEN? HOW? WHY?
If your longer version doesn't answer at least one of these with a specific answer, you've just said the same thing in slightly different words. Real details add new information the reader didn't already have.
See it in action with another example
Short sentence: "The student reads."
Vague: "The student reads quietly to the animal." (we already know they're reading — "quietly" and "animal" are too general)
Specific: "The nervous fifth-grader reads a chapter of Charlotte's Web to the therapy dog curled up beside her." (WHO + WHAT + WHERE)
SO WHAT? WHY THIS MATTERS FOR YOUR ESSAYS
This is the same skill that makes body paragraphs convincing. When you write evidence in an essay, specific details are always more persuasive than vague statements. Instead of "dogs help students," write "therapy dogs help nervous readers feel comfortable by sitting quietly and never judging mistakes."

Question 11 — Spelling Fixes

Your essay had 8 spelling errors — they fall into 3 patterns, each with a different fix

Your spelling errors aren't random. They fall into three types, and each one has a different fix:

Type 1: Letters in the Wrong Order
becuase → because
spesific → specific
Acording → According

Fix: You know these words — the letters are just out of order. When proofreading, look at the middle of long words. That's where letters get swapped.

Type 2: Missing Letters or Sounds
stres → stress (double s)
reveive → receive (i before e after c)
alound → aloud (no n — it's just "loud" with an a)

Fix: Say the word slowly. Does each sound have a letter? "stress" = str-ess (you need both s's). "aloud" = a-loud (there's no "n" sound).

Type 3: Word Boundaries and Apostrophes
infront → in front (two words, like "in back")
wont → won't (will + not = won't)

Fix: These aren't typos — these are rules to learn. "In front" is always two words. Contractions always need an apostrophe: won't, can't, don't, wouldn't.

Which sentence has no spelling errors?
A "Dogs must follow strict rules becuase they need spesific training."
B "Dogs must follow strict rules because they need specific training, or students won't feel safe."
3-MINUTE PROOFREADING STRATEGY
Save the last 3 minutes of every essay for proofreading. Here's how to use that time:
1. Read your essay from the last sentence to the first — reading backward forces you to see each sentence on its own instead of skimming
2. On each sentence, look at the middle of long words — that's where letters get swapped (Type 1)
3. Check for contractions — if you wrote "wont," "cant," or "dont," add the apostrophe

This one habit could earn you 1–2 more points on every essay.

Because, But, So — these connect two ideas. The trick is choosing the one that matches the relationship between the ideas:

Conjunction Quick Reference
because Gives a reason: "Dogs help kids because they reduce stress."
but Shows a contrast: "Dogs are gentle, but they still need training."
so Shows a result: "Kids felt calmer, so their test scores improved."
Which conjunction fits? "Students were nervous about reading aloud, ___ the therapy dog helped them feel safe."
A so
B but

Transition words connect paragraphs and ideas. You already use them well in your essays ("Firstly," "Secondly," "As a result"). Here's a quick reference for the test:

Purpose Transition Words
Adding more Also, Additionally, Furthermore, In addition
Contrast However, On the other hand, Although, Despite
Cause / Result Therefore, As a result, Consequently, Because of this
Sequence First, Then, Next, Finally
SO WHAT? HOW THIS SHOWS UP ON THE TEST
On the multiple choice, you'll be asked "which transition word best connects these sentences?" The answer is always about the relationship between the ideas — are they adding, contrasting, showing a result, or sequencing? Identify the relationship first, then pick the transition that matches.

You used "As a result" and "However" correctly in your G6.6 essay — you already know this instinctively. The test questions just need you to slow down and think about which relationship the passage is showing.
