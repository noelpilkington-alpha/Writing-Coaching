Sentence Types — Declarative vs Imperative

Your biggest slip on G3.2 — and a declining skill
📉
Declining skill alert: Your "Change the Sentence Type" score went from 100% on G3.1 down to 50% on G3.2. The four types are slipping a bit — let's lock them back in.
FROM G3.2 Q4 — IDENTIFY THE SENTENCE TYPE
The prompt said: "Workers spent over 30 minutes clearing the ice."
You wrote: Imperative.
The correct answer is Declarative. This sentence is telling us a fact — it ends with a period and it's a statement. An imperative sentence tells someone to do something, like "Clear the ice." You lost 2 full points here.
The Four Sentence Types — Use the End Punctuation!
. Declarative = a statement. It tells you something. Ends with a period.
Example: Workers cleared the ice.
? Interrogative = a question. It asks something. Ends with a question mark.
Example: Did the workers clear the ice?
! Exclamatory = strong feeling. Shows excitement or surprise. Ends with an exclamation point.
Example: That ice pile is huge!
cmd Imperative = a command. It tells someone to do something. Usually ends with a period, but the VERB is the giveaway — it's giving an order.
Example: Clear the ice. / Please sit down.

THE EVIDENCE SANDWICH — HOW TO USE PASSAGE DETAILS
When you use something from the passage, make a little sandwich: your idea → the detail from the passage → why it matters. Here's what to DO and what NOT to do:
DO DO NOT
Quote a short phrase with quotation marks, like: The passage says the camels carry "books on their back." Copy a whole sentence from the passage without quotes or explanation.
Paraphrase in your own words — say the same idea a different way. Change just one word and pretend it's your own writing.
Explain why the detail matters to your main idea in the next sentence. Drop a detail in and move on without connecting it.
Use at least two different details — one per body sentence. Repeat the same detail two ways and call it two details.

Commas with "because" and "so"

Comma goes BEFORE the word — not after

Helio, here's a small habit showing up on your test: every time you use a joining word like because, so, or instead, you put a comma right AFTER it. The comma actually goes BEFORE the word (if any comma is needed at all).

FROM G3.2 Q2 — SUBORDINATING CONJUNCTION
"Fans tossed bears onto the ice because, the Bears scored a goal."
The comma after "because" shouldn't be there. When "because" joins two parts of a sentence and comes in the middle, you usually need no comma at all — or if one is needed, it goes BEFORE "because."
FROM G3.2 Q10 — BASIC CONJUNCTIONS
"The ice was coverd in teddy bears so, the players skated among the bears."
Same pattern — comma AFTER "so." The right spot is BEFORE "so": "The ice was covered in teddy bears, so the players skated among the bears."
The Comma Rule for because / so / but / and
YES When so, but, or and joins two full sentences, put a comma BEFORE it.
The fans cheered, so the players waved.
YES When because comes in the middle of the sentence, usually no comma is needed.
Fans tossed bears because the team scored a goal.
NO Never put a comma right AFTER because, so, but, or and. That's the habit we're breaking.
