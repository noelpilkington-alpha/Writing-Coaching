Spelling Patterns: Letters Swapped and Doubled

"Would Have" not "Would Of"

Q7 — a common mix-up with a simple rule

Brooks, on Q7 you used "would of" and "would'nt of." This is a very common mistake because when people talk, "would have" sounds like "would of." But in writing, "of" is never correct after "would," "could," or "should."

Q7 — YOUR ANSWER (2 out of 3 weighted points)
"I would of show the guide because i would'nt of known what it was."

Three problems in one sentence:

  • "would of" should be "would have"
  • "would'nt" should be "wouldn't" (the apostrophe goes where the missing letter is)
  • "show" should be "shown" (after "would have," use the past form)
CORRECTED VERSION
"I would have shown the guide because I wouldn't have known what it was."
THE RULE
Would HAVE. Could HAVE. Should HAVE. Never "of."

When you shorten it: would've, couldn't have, shouldn't have.

After "would have," use the past form of the verb: would have shown (not "show"), would have gone (not "go"), would have known (not "know").
Which sentence is correct?
A I would of went to the park if it was sunny.
B I would have gone to the park if it was sunny.
QUICK CHECK
Whenever you write "would," "could," or "should," look at the next word. If it is "of," change it to "have." This one fix would have saved you a full point on Q7.

Write Complete Sentences

Q6 — starting with "That" created a fragment

Brooks, on Q6 your answer started with the word "That," which made it a fragment instead of a complete sentence. A fragment is a group of words that looks like a sentence but is missing something important.

Q6 — YOUR ANSWER (2 out of 3 weighted points)
"That she likes nature, but she is obedient and follows rules."

The problem: "That she likes nature" is not a complete thought on its own. It makes the reader ask: "That she likes nature... what about it?" A complete sentence needs a subject doing something.

COMPLETE SENTENCE VERSION
"The story shows that she likes nature, but she is obedient and follows rules."

Or even simpler:

ANOTHER FIX
"Layla likes nature, but she is obedient and follows rules."
THE RULE: EVERY SENTENCE NEEDS A SUBJECT
A complete sentence must have:

1. A subject (who or what the sentence is about)
2. A verb (what the subject does)

"That she likes nature" has no clear subject doing something on its own. But "Layla likes nature" or "The story shows that she likes nature" both have a subject and a verb working together.
Which of these is a complete sentence?
A That the butterfly was colorful and landed on a flower.
B The butterfly was colorful and landed on a flower.
QUICK CHECK
After writing your answer, read the first few words. If your sentence starts with "That..." ask yourself: "That... what?" If the sentence does not answer that question on its own, add a subject at the beginning.

Add Detail and Explanation

Q11 at 14/20 — good structure, needs more development

Brooks, your paragraph on Q11 earned 14 out of 20. You have a topic sentence and a conclusion, which is good. The 6 points that slipped away were mostly about developing your ideas further. Each point you make needs a sentence that explains why or how.

THE PATTERN FOR A STRONG PARAGRAPH
For each point you make, follow this pattern:

1. State your point (one sentence)
2. Give a specific example or detail (one sentence)
3. Explain why it matters using "This shows..." or "This means..." (one sentence)

Two fully developed points are worth more than three quick points with no explanation.
