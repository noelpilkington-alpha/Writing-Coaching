The Comma Splice (Q8)

Two full sentences cannot be joined by just a comma

Joan, Q8 asked you to write one sentence describing a unique plant. Your idea was great — bluebonnets with their imperfect buds. But the answer slipped to 0.67 out of 1 because you wrote two full sentences joined by just a comma.

YOU WROTE (Q8)
"One unique flower that I have seen is a bluebonnet, they are unique to me beacuse they have all of those imperfect buds on them and they look beautiful."
Two full sentences are glued together with just a comma. That is a comma splice. Also — "beacuse" should be "because."
HOW TO TELL IF YOU HAVE A COMMA SPLICE
Cover up everything after the comma. Can what is before the comma stand alone as a sentence?
Before: "One unique flower that I have seen is a bluebonnet." → Yes, full sentence.

Now cover up everything before the comma. Can what is after stand alone?
After: "They are unique to me because they have all of those imperfect buds on them and they look beautiful." → Yes, full sentence.

Two full sentences joined by just a comma = comma splice.

You have three clean fixes. Pick whichever one fits best for the sentence:

FIX 1 — USE A CONJUNCTION (because, and)
"One unique flower that I have seen is a bluebonnet because it has all of those imperfect buds on it and looks beautiful."
This folds the two ideas into one sentence using because — the same move you nailed on Q5.
FIX 2 — MAKE IT ONE DESCRIPTION
"One unique flower that I have seen is the bluebonnet, a flower with beautiful imperfect buds that stand out to me."
This turns the second half into an appositive (a noun phrase) — the same move you nailed on Q4.
FIX 3 — USE A PERIOD
"One unique flower that I have seen is the bluebonnet. It is unique to me because it has those imperfect buds that look beautiful."
Two full sentences = two periods. This is the simplest fix — but Q8 asked for one sentence, so Fixes 1 or 2 score better.
The prompt says: Write one sentence describing a unique plant.
Which one avoids a comma splice and scores full credit?
A A Venus flytrap is a cool plant, it actually eats bugs to survive.
B A Venus flytrap is a cool plant because it actually eats bugs to survive.
YOUR COMMA-SPLICE CHECK
Before you submit a one-sentence answer (Q6–Q10), run this two-second test on any comma you used:

1. Cover what is after the comma — is what is before a full sentence?
2. Cover what is before the comma — is what is after a full sentence?

If both answers are yes, you have a splice. Swap the comma for because, and, or a period — whichever keeps your meaning.
WHY THIS MATTERS
Q8 was worth 1 point and came in at 0.67. Knowing the comma-splice rule is a 0.33-point recovery — and the same rule protects every Q6–Q10 answer going forward. You already use because correctly on Q5. Q8 just needed the same move.

Paragraph Conventions Polish (Q11)

Three small slips — each one is worth points

Joan, your robots paragraph is well organized and well developed — that is why it scored 17/20. But three small convention slips are hiding in it. Each one is the kind of thing a read-back pass catches in 10 seconds.

"Robots can help people in so many ways. Some ways in houses, others in offices or hotels. For example, a robot could clean the floor while you go get some food. Or a robot could get you something while your at a hotel. Some robots don't do chorse, but they do comfort you if you are sad. This can help people who feel alone. These are all ways robots can help humans."

Here are the three slips mapped out:

Slip 1 — Fragment
Some ways in houses, others in offices or hotels.
No verb — it's a list, not a sentence. Fix: "Some help in houses, while others help in offices or hotels."
Slip 2 — Homophone
your at a hotel→you're at a hotel
"Your" = belongs to you. "You're" = you are. Here it means "you are at a hotel."
Slip 3 — Spelling
chorse→chores
Letters swapped. Saying the word slowly in your head catches this.

That alone is worth 2–3 points on Q11.

THE 3-STEP PARAGRAPH READ-BACK
Step 1 — Every sentence needs a subject AND a verb. Read each sentence alone. If there is no verb, it is a fragment — fix it.

Step 2 — Homophone sweep. Stop on every your / you're, there / their / they're, to / too. Pick the right one.

Step 3 — Slow-read every word. Say each word in your head. "Chorse" falls apart. "Chores" holds up.
