What Is an Appositive?

Q1 — you joined the sentences; the task wanted a different join

Question 1 gave you two sentences and asked you to rewrite them as one sentence that includes an appositive.

The two sentences to fold into one:
“My family has two really cute dogs.”
“Their names are Lucy and Rocket.”
QUESTION 1 — 1 OUT OF 2
“My family has two dogs and their names are Lucy and Rocket.”

You earned a point here for doing the genuinely useful half: two sentences became one, and the meaning survived. What the question also wanted was the specific tool named in the instruction — an appositive, which is a short naming phrase that sits between commas and renames the noun right before it. Joining with “and” makes one sentence, but it does not make an appositive.

THE APPOSITIVE VERSION
“My family has two really cute dogs, Lucy and Rocket.”
The comma does the joining, and Lucy and Rocket renames dogs. Shorter than your version, and it is exactly what the instruction asked for.
THE PATTERN, AND THE CHECK
[noun], [naming phrase], [rest of the sentence].

The check: take the naming phrase out. If what is left is still a complete sentence, you built it right. “My family has two really cute dogs” — still a sentence. Correct.
Which sentence uses an appositive?
AMy family has two dogs and their names are Lucy and Rocket.
BMy family has two dogs, Lucy and Rocket.

Your Paragraph: 15 out of 20

The thinking is real — the article never made it onto the page

The question: Explain the ways that soil is important to Earth — using evidence from the article. Here is what you wrote:

“Plants need to grow with soil. Animals eat plants. If there was no soil, plants or animals can’t survive. Some animals can’t survive without soil.”
A real cause-and-effect chain Explains instead of retelling No evidence from the article Four sentences — ends as it gets going Last sentence repeats the third

Start with what is genuinely good here, because it is the hard part. Your four sentences build a chain: soil grows plants, animals eat plants, so no soil means neither survives. That is reasoning — you connected facts into an argument instead of listing them. Plenty of longer paragraphs never do that.

So why 15 and not 20? The prompt asked you to use evidence from the selection, and there is not one detail from “Digging on Dirt” on the page. The article gave you worms and groundhogs living in soil, dead leaves rotting into humus that feeds plants, the different kinds of soil — and any of them would have made your chain stronger. Right now a reader cannot tell you read the article at all, and that is exactly what the missing points were for.

The other slip: your last sentence — “Some animals can’t survive without soil” — says what the sentence before it already said. When that happens, it is a sign the paragraph ran out of material, and the article is where the material was.

Tap to see your chain, with the article behind it
Your reasoning stays — evidence gets added under it
YOUR PARAGRAPH, DEVELOPED

“Soil is important to Earth because almost every living thing depends on it. Plants need soil to grow, and the article says fallen leaves, twigs, and dead bugs rot in the soil and turn into humus, which is food for plants. Animals eat plants, and animals like worms and groundhogs even make their homes in the soil itself. If there was no soil, plants and animals couldn’t survive — and since people need both, we could not survive either. That is why soil matters to every living thing on Earth.”

Notice what did not change: the chain is still yours — soil to plants, plants to animals, no soil means no survival. What got added is proof from the article under each link, and an ending that pushes the chain one step further instead of repeating it.

THE MOVE TO PRACTISE
After each idea, add one sentence that starts “The article says…” That single habit turns your reasoning into an evidence paragraph — and it is the difference between 15 and 20. The thinking is the part that cannot be taught quickly, and you already have it.
