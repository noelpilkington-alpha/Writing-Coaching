What IS an Appositive?

Q4 — the question asked for a naming phrase, not a conjunction

David, Q4 asked you to write a sentence using an appositive. An appositive is a naming phrase that sits right next to a noun and renames or describes it, set off by commas.

Q4 — YOUR ANSWER (1 out of 2 weighted points)
"Bluebonnets only grow for a short time so many people around texas attend bluebonnet festivals."

The problem: You used "so" to connect two ideas (a conjunction). The question asked for an appositive, which is a phrase that renames a noun. Also, "texas" needs a capital T because it is a proper noun (the name of a state).

WHAT AN APPOSITIVE LOOKS LIKE
An appositive is a naming phrase placed right next to a noun, set off by commas:

• Bluebonnets, the state flower of Texas, only bloom for a short time.
• The festival, a celebration of spring, draws visitors from across the state.

The phrase between the commas renames the noun before it. That is what makes it an appositive.
A STRONGER ANSWER USING AN APPOSITIVE
"Bluebonnets, the official state flower of Texas, only grow for a short time, so many people attend bluebonnet festivals."

See the difference? The phrase "the official state flower of Texas" renames "Bluebonnets." That is an appositive.

Which sentence contains an appositive?
A Bluebonnets grow in spring so people plan trips to see them.
B The bluebonnet, a wildflower native to Texas, blooms every spring.
HOW TO SPOT THE DIFFERENCE
If the question says "appositive," look for a chance to rename a noun. Put the renaming phrase right after the noun, between two commas. Do not use "so," "and," or "because" as your main move. Those are conjunctions, not appositives.

Fix Run-On Sentences

Q8 and Q9 — multiple independent clauses need periods

David, on Q8 and Q9 you wrote sentences that run multiple complete thoughts together without the punctuation needed to separate them. Here is what happened:

Q8 — YOUR ANSWER (2 out of 3 weighted points)
"I once saw a bluebonnet it was very unique because it was very colorful and beautiful."

The problem: "I once saw a bluebonnet" is a complete thought. "It was very unique..." is another complete thought. They are joined with nothing between them. That is a run-on. You need a period after "bluebonnet" and then start a new sentence.

Q9 — YOUR ANSWER (1 out of 3 weighted points)
"There is a place that I love and it is very calm I sit by the river and sit for hours with my friends."

The problem: Three independent clauses are pushed together: (1) "There is a place that I love," (2) "it is very calm," (3) "I sit by the river and sit for hours with my friends." Each needs its own sentence or proper punctuation to separate them.

FIXED VERSIONS
Q8: "I once saw a bluebonnet. It was very unique because it was colorful and beautiful."

Q9: "There is a place that I love. It is very calm. I sit by the river for hours with my friends."
THE RUN-ON TEST
Read your sentence out loud. Every time you could take a full breath (a complete thought ends), ask: is there a period here? If the answer is no, add one. At G4, the safest fix for a run-on is a period and a new sentence.

B I love going to the park. It is quiet there. I can relax for a long time.
WHY THIS MATTERS
