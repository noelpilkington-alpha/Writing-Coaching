What IS an Appositive?

Q3 — 1 out of 2 — you added an adverb instead
💡
What happened: The question asked you to add an appositive to the sentence. You wrote "Joyfully, Sally swam to the other seals." That's an adverb, not an appositive. The question was testing whether you know what an appositive is.
YOUR ANSWER (Q3)
"Joyfully, Sally swam to the other seals."
WHAT IS AN APPOSITIVE?
An appositive is a naming phrase that renames or describes a noun, placed right next to it and set off by commas. It answers: "Who or what is this?" not "How did they do it?"
Appositive vs. Adverb
ADVERB "Joyfully, Sally swam to the other seals." (tells HOW she swam)
APPOSITIVE "Sally, a playful seal pup, swam to the other seals." (tells WHO Sally is)
THE PATTERN
Noun, [appositive phrase], rest of sentence.

The appositive sits between two commas and renames the noun before it.
• "Anna, the girl who found the seal, ran to get help."
• "Jim, her older brother, carried the towel."
• "Sally, a baby seal, needed to get back to the ocean."
Which sentence correctly adds an appositive about Sally?
A Quickly, Sally swam to the other seals.
B Sally, a young gray seal, swam to the other seals.
C Sally swam to the other seals in the cold water.
REMEMBER
When a question says "add an appositive," look for the noun, then add a comma + a phrase that renames it + another comma. The phrase should answer "Who or what is [noun]?" not "How?" or "Where?"

Combining Sentences Correctly

Q4 — 1 out of 2 — "in addition" doesn't combine
📝
What happened: You were asked to combine two sentences into one. You wrote: "Anna and Jim ran to the beach, in addition, they found a baby seal." The phrase "in addition" doesn't actually join two sentences into one. It's a transition, not a connector.
YOUR ANSWER (Q4)
"Anna and Jim ran to the beach, in addition, they found a baby seal."
WHY "IN ADDITION" DOESN'T WORK HERE
"In addition" is a transition phrase that starts a new thought. It doesn't glue two sentences into one the way "and," "where," or "because" does. Using it with commas on both sides actually creates a run-on.
Ways to Truly Combine Two Sentences
AND "Anna and Jim ran to the beach and found a baby seal."
WHERE "Anna and Jim ran to the beach, where they found a baby seal."
NO "Anna and Jim ran to the beach, in addition, they found a baby seal." (not combined)
THE RULE
To combine two sentences into one, use a real connector:
• and — adds a second action
• where — connects a place to what happened there
• because — gives a reason
• so — shows a result

Words like "in addition," "also," "furthermore," and "moreover" are transitions. They start new sentences but don't combine old ones.
Which version correctly combines "Anna and Jim ran to the beach" + "They found a baby seal" into ONE sentence?
A Anna and Jim ran to the beach, in addition, they found a baby seal.
B Anna and Jim ran to the beach, also they found a baby seal.
C Anna and Jim ran to the beach, where they found a baby seal.
QUICK CHECK
When a question says "combine these two sentences," ask yourself: "Is my connecting word a real glue word (and, where, because, so, but) or just a transition (in addition, also, furthermore)?" If it's a transition, it doesn't combine.

Titus, your Q11 paragraph mentions that Tajiri loved catching bugs and turned that into a game. That's great. But you need to include one more specific fact from the passage and then explain what it shows about the main idea.

The Detail + Explanation Pattern
DETAIL A specific fact from the passage (names, numbers, what happened).
EXPLAIN Why it matters or what it shows about the main idea. This is the sentence most students leave out.
EXAMPLE FROM YOUR PASSAGE
Detail: "Tajiri's love of catching bugs as a kid gave him the idea to create a game where players collect creatures."
Explanation: "This shows that a simple childhood hobby can become the spark for something millions of people enjoy."

The explanation connects the fact to the bigger idea (inspiration from everyday life). Without it, the reader just has a fact.
