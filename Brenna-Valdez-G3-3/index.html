Your #1 Win: Pick the Right Little Word

Small connector words carry the whole meaning of a sentence

Brenna, on two of your sentence answers, the answer was almost right — but a single small word was off. One word changed the whole meaning of the sentence. Look at this:

FROM Q5 — picking the best connector
You wrote: "But there was no sign of the seals, the family placed Sally in a fish box and motored into the bay."
But means "the opposite of what you'd expect." That doesn't fit here. The sentence really means "When there was no sign of the seals..." or "Since there was no sign of the seals..." — the seals being gone is the reason the family took action, not the opposite of it.
FROM Q2 — adding details
You wrote: "Dad freed the pup in a shallow part or the water."
You meant "a shallow part of the water" (the water's shallow part belongs to the water). Or means "choice between two things." Of means "belonging to." Read your own sentence back out loud and you will usually hear the right one.
SMALL WORDS, BIG MEANINGS
Each little connector tells the reader a different story:

• When / Since / Because → reason or time. "Since the seals were gone, they left the bay."
• But / However → opposite / surprise. "They looked for the seals, but none were there."
• And / Also → adding more. "They looked, and they waited."
• Of → belonging to. "a part of the water"
• Or → choice. "the beach or the bay"

Before you lock in your answer, read the sentence back and ask: does this little word match what I mean?
Which version uses the right connector to show cause and effect?
A But there was no sign of the seals, the family placed Sally in a fish box.
B When there was no sign of the seals, the family placed Sally in a fish box.
THE "REPLAY IT IN YOUR HEAD" CHECK
After you write your answer, replay the whole sentence in your head. Ask: is this really a surprise (use "But"), a reason (use "Since" / "Because" / "When"), or just adding more (use "And")?

On Q5, a five-second replay would have caught "But" and swapped it for "When."
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q2 and Q5 together cost you 1 full point. That is the difference between 80% and 82% on this test alone. Across the whole year, picking the right connector could be worth 2–3 points every test.

Your #2 Win: Answer the Question That Was Asked

The fastest points come from matching your answer to the prompt

Brenna, on two of your sentence questions, your writing was clean — but your answer was for a different question. The grading feedback can only give credit if your answer matches what was asked. Look at this:

FROM Q7 — "what was the most meaningful moment?"
The prompt asked: What do you think was the most meaningful moment in the story? Explain why in one sentence.

You wrote: "Yes I would because I love animals and I don't want animals to suffer."
That reads like an answer to "would you help the seal?" — which was Q6, not Q7. Q7 asked you to pick a moment from the story (like the rescue, or the release back to the sea) and explain why that moment was meaningful.
FROM Q3 — "rewrite and add an appositive"
You wrote: "Sally swam to the other seals, as they all played together."
You added a clause, but an appositive is a little naming-phrase right after a noun, like "Sally, the rescued pup, swam to the other seals." Your extra part tells what happened next, not what Sally is.
THE "MATCH" HABIT
Before you start typing, find the exact action word in the prompt:

• "What moment..." → name a moment from the story
• "Add an appositive" → add a naming phrase that renames the noun (Sally, the baby seal,...)
• "Change into a question" → the answer must end with ?
• "Combine the sentences" → one sentence that contains both ideas

Then check your answer against that action word before you submit.
Which answer correctly adds an appositive to: "Sally swam to the other seals"?
A Sally swam to the other seals, as they all played together.
B Sally, the rescued pup, swam to the other seals.
THE 4-STEP PROCESS FOR EVERY ANSWER
Use this on every sentence question:

1. Read the prompt slowly and find the action word ("add," "combine," "change," "explain").
2. Plan a short answer in your head that does exactly that action.
3. Draft your answer in the response box.
4. Revise: re-read your answer next to the prompt — does it match?

Thirty extra seconds on step 4 is often worth a full point.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
On Q3 and Q7, you had clean, complete sentences — but they answered a slightly different question than the one asked. Matching the prompt exactly on those two could have moved you from 19/25 to 20 or 21/25 on the sentence side.

POLISHED VERSION (what 20/20 looks like)

Many people from all around the world have played a game called Pokemon. Pokemon is a game that a lot of people enjoy. The game was made by a boy named Satoshi Tajiri who wanted to make a game that was new and fun. As a boy, Tajiri loved bugs and video games, so he put those two hobbies together to make a really fun game. His game has over 150 Pokemon for players to collect. So it turns out that his childhood hobby turned into a whole entire game that lots of people love.

What changed: your sentence "Pokemon is a game that a lot of people enjoy, the game was made by..." was two complete thoughts joined with only a comma (a run-on). Splitting it into two sentences with a period fixes it cleanly.

THE RUN-ON FIX
SPOT IT Two complete thoughts joined with only a comma. Each side could stand alone.
FIX IT Replace the comma with a period, then start a new sentence with a capital letter.
OR Keep the comma and add a joining word like and, but, or so.
Which version splits the run-on correctly?
A Pokemon is a game that a lot of people enjoy, the game was made by a boy named Satoshi Tajiri.
B Pokemon is a game that a lot of people enjoy. The game was made by a boy named Satoshi Tajiri.
THE "COULD EACH SIDE STAND ALONE?" TEST
When you have a comma in the middle of a sentence, cover up one side and read the other out loud. If both sides sound like complete sentences on their own, you have a run-on.

Fix: swap the comma for a period and start a new sentence, OR add a joining word (and, but, so, because) right after the comma.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Splitting that one run-on cleanly is the difference between 17/20 and 20/20 on your paragraph. Combined with sharper sentence answers, you are looking at a 90% test next time.
