COMMA SPLICE vs. PERIOD
A comma splice is when two complete ideas are joined with only a comma. The fix is simple: replace the comma with a period.

Comma splice: "We sat at the table drinking tea, Sometimes we listened to music."
Fixed: "We sat at the table drinking tea. Sometimes we listened to music."

If the word after the comma could start its own sentence (like "Sometimes," "Then," "She," "We"), you probably need a period instead of a comma.

Appositive = Naming Phrase, Not a Clause

Q1 — 1 out of 2
🔍
The concept: An appositive is a short naming phrase that renames a noun right next to it. It does NOT contain a verb like "who always did." If it has "who/that/which + verb," it is a relative clause, not an appositive.
G5.2 Q1 — 1 OUT OF 2
You wrote: "Grandma Addie, a grandma who always did the pie competition, has taken second place..."
"A grandma who always did the pie competition" is a relative clause (it has "who" + a verb "did"). An appositive would be a short naming phrase like "a lifelong baker" or "the family's pie champion."
Appositive vs. Relative Clause
APPOSITIVE A naming phrase. No verb. Example: "Grandma Addie, a lifelong baker, entered the competition."
RELATIVE CLAUSE Has who/that/which + verb. Example: "Grandma Addie, who always entered the competition, won first place."
Which sentence uses a true appositive (naming phrase, no verb)?
A Grandma Addie, who has won many ribbons, entered again this year.
B Grandma Addie, the family's pie champion, entered again this year.
THE QUICK TEST FOR APPOSITIVES
When you write an appositive, check it with this test:

1. Remove the phrase between the commas. Does the sentence still make sense? (It should.)
2. Does the phrase contain a verb? If yes, it is a relative clause, not an appositive. Strip the verb out and make it a short naming phrase instead.

Your version: "Grandma Addie, a grandma who always did the pie competition, ..."
Fixed: "Grandma Addie, a devoted pie competitor, ..."

See the difference? The fixed version is just a name/title. No "who," no verb.
PRACTICE PATTERN
Noun, [short naming phrase], rest of sentence.

"My dog, a golden retriever, loves swimming."
"Mr. Torres, our math guide, gave us extra time."
"Austin, the capital of Texas, gets very hot in summer."

Notice: no verbs inside the commas. Just a name, title, or description.

"Practicing leads to success because you get better by practicing" and "details make a difference by adding details" are circular. They say the same thing twice instead of explaining how or why.
G5.2 Q7 — 2 OUT OF 3
You wrote: "Practicing somethong over time can lead to success because you get better at it every time you practice."
Two issues: (1) "somethong" should be "something." (2) The reasoning is circular: "practicing leads to success because you get better by practicing." You need to explain the SPECIFIC way practice helps (e.g., you notice mistakes, build muscle memory, get feedback).
G5.2 Q8 — 2 OUT OF 3
You wrote: "Small details can make a big difference in a finished project by adding more details to make it more specific."
Circular: "details make a difference by adding details." You repeated the same idea in the explanation. A better version names the SPECIFIC way details help (e.g., they make the reader picture the scene, or they show the grader you read closely).
Which answer to Q7 avoids circular reasoning?
A Practicing something over time can lead to success because the more you practice the more skilled you become at practicing it.
B Practicing something over time can lead to success because each attempt helps you notice small mistakes and fix them, so your work improves little by little.
THE "HOW SPECIFICALLY?" TEST
After you write a "because" sentence, ask yourself: "How specifically?"

If your answer to "how?" is just the same claim in different words, you are being circular. Push yourself to name the specific mechanism.

Circular: "Details make a difference by adding more details."
Ask: "How specifically do details make a difference?"
Fixed: "Small details make a big difference because they help the reader picture exactly what happened, like describing the golden crust of the pie instead of just saying it looked good."

The fixed version gives a concrete example of how details work. That is what full credit requires.
WHY THIS MATTERS
Q7 and Q8 are each worth 3 points and you scored 2 out of 3 on both. That is 2 points on the table from one reasoning habit. Name the specific "how" or "why" in your explanation and you pick up those points cleanly.
