Use the Given Sentence Starter

Q4 — 1 out of 2

On Q4, the prompt gave you a sentence starter beginning with "If he wanted to..." and asked you to complete it. Instead of building on that starter, you wrote your own simple sentence.

YOU WROTE
"He wanted to make a difference."

The problem: The question asked you to complete a fragment that starts with the subordinating conjunction "If." Your answer ignores that starter entirely. It is a complete sentence on its own, but it does not follow the instructions.

What you should have done: Take the given starter ("If he wanted to...") and finish the thought by adding what happens next.

Think: what would follow "If he wanted to..."?
CORRECT COMPLETION
"If he wanted to make a difference, he would need to dedicate his time and energy to helping his students succeed."

Notice how the given starter ("If he wanted to") is kept, and the sentence is completed with a result clause after the comma.

THE RULE
When a question gives you a sentence starter, you must use those exact words as the beginning of your answer. Do not rewrite the idea in your own words. Build directly on what is given.

Steps:
1. Copy the given starter word-for-word.
2. Finish the thought so it makes a complete sentence.
3. Re-read to make sure the full sentence flows from start to finish.
Why "If" starters need a result
"If" introduces a condition. Every condition needs a result. Think of it like: "If [condition], then [result]." Your job is to supply the result half. Example: "If he wanted to help his community, he had to start by changing the school."

Conjunction Logic: "But" vs. "So"

Q5 — 1 out of 2

On Q5, you used the conjunction "but" in a spot where "so" was needed. The meaning of your sentence did not match the conjunction you chose.

YOU WROTE
"His dedication has transformed the school, but due to his efforts, enrollment has doubled..."

The problem: "But" signals a contrast — it tells the reader something unexpected or opposite is coming. However, "enrollment has doubled" is a positive result of his dedication, not a contrast. The two ideas point in the same direction.

"but" (contrast) → "so" (cause/effect)
Tap to see the corrected sentence
Which conjunction shows cause and effect?
CORRECTED
"His dedication has transformed the school, so enrollment has doubled..."

"So" tells the reader that the second part is a result of the first. Dedication caused enrollment to grow — that's cause and effect, not contrast.

CONJUNCTION CHEAT SHEET
Before you pick a conjunction, ask: What is the relationship between these two ideas?

"But" = contrast (the second idea is surprising or opposite)
• "He studied hard, but he still failed the test."

"So" = cause/effect (the first idea caused the second)
• "He studied hard, so he passed the test."

"And" = addition (both ideas go together)
• "He studied hard and practiced every day."
Quick test to check your conjunction
Cover the conjunction and read both halves. Ask: "Does the second half surprise me given the first half?" If yes, use "but." If the second half is a result of the first, use "so." If it just adds more information, use "and."

Proofread: Comma, Spelling, Possessive

Q8 — 2 out of 3

On Q8, your answer had three small errors that cost you a point. Each one is easy to catch with a quick re-read.

YOU WROTE
"One way you can show kindness to others in your daily life, is giving a complement because it can change someones day."

Error 1: Unnecessary comma

Do not put a comma between the subject ("One way you can show kindness...") and the verb ("is"). The subject flows directly into the verb with no pause needed.

"daily life, is giving" → "daily life is giving"

Error 2: Wrong word — "complement" vs. "compliment"

A compliment is a nice thing you say to someone. A complement means something that completes or goes well with something else. You meant the nice-words version.

"complement" → "compliment"

Error 3: Missing apostrophe — possessive

"Someones" needs an apostrophe because the day belongs to someone. When something belongs to a person, add 's.

"someones day" → "someone's day"
Tap to see the fully corrected sentence
All three fixes applied
CORRECTED
"One way you can show kindness to others in your daily life is giving a compliment because it can change someone's day."
PROOFREADING CHECKLIST
Before you move on from any answer, scan for these three things:

1. Commas: Read the sentence aloud. If you would not pause there, remove the comma.
2. Sound-alike words: "Complement" and "compliment" sound similar but mean different things. When in doubt, think about the meaning.
3. Possessives: If something belongs to someone, add an apostrophe + s.

  • Strong concluding sentence. End by restating your main idea in a fresh way — do not just repeat your topic sentence word for word.
G4 Paragraph Polish: TS → D + E → D + E → CS
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail  •  E = Explanation  •  CS = Concluding Sentence
TS State your main point: how Cody changed opinions about Mud.
D Give a specific detail from the passage showing what Cody did or said.
E Explain WHY that detail matters — how does it prove opinions changed?
D Give a second detail showing the result or reaction.
E Explain WHY this second detail proves your point even further.
CS Wrap up by restating the main idea in different words.
POLISH TIP
You are already close. The difference between 18 and 20 is usually one or two explanation sentences that connect your details back to the main idea. After each detail, ask: "So what? Why does this matter?" Write that answer down — that is your explanation sentence.
