The Detail + Explanation Pattern

The biggest single lift on your essay score

Rhys, when the rubric says "develops ideas with evidence," it is looking for a specific two-part move in each body paragraph:

THE DETAIL + EXPLANATION PATTERN
Step 1 — Detail: Name a specific fact, event, or quote from the passage.
Step 2 — Explanation: Write a sentence that starts with "This shows..." or "This means..." and explains WHY that detail supports your point.

Without Step 2, you are summarizing. With Step 2, you are analyzing. The rubric gives full marks for analysis.

Here is the difference. Imagine you are writing about Wilma Rudolph's perseverance:

WITHOUT EXPLANATION (SUMMARY ONLY)
"Wilma Rudolph had polio as a child and could not walk without a brace. She worked hard and eventually became a track star."
WITH DETAIL + EXPLANATION (FULL DEVELOPMENT)
"According to the passage, Wilma Rudolph was told she would never walk without a brace after being diagnosed with polio at age four. This shows her perseverance because she refused to accept that limit — she worked through years of physical therapy until she could not only walk, but run fast enough to win three Olympic gold medals."

The second version uses the same facts but adds the "this shows..." sentence that connects the detail to the essay's main idea. That connection is what earns the development points.

Which body paragraph uses the Detail + Explanation pattern correctly?
A Wilma Rudolph had polio when she was young. She had to wear a leg brace. Later she became a track star and won gold medals at the Olympics. She was very fast.
B The passage states that Wilma Rudolph was diagnosed with polio at age four and was told she might never walk normally again. This shows her incredible perseverance because she did not give up even when doctors doubted her — instead, she pushed through years of therapy until she could compete at the highest level in track and field.
YOUR GOAL FOR EACH BODY PARAGRAPH
Every body paragraph in your essay should have at least one Detail + Explanation pair. A strong body paragraph looks like this:

1. Topic sentence (what this paragraph is about)
2. Detail from the passage (a specific fact, event, or quote)
3. "This shows..." explanation (why it matters)
4. Optional: a second detail + explanation for extra depth

If you write three body paragraphs and each one has at least one Detail + Explanation pair, you are hitting the development row of the rubric.

Five Paragraphs — All Present and Separated

Making sure the rubric can check every structural box

The G6 essay rubric expects 5 clearly separated paragraphs: an introduction, three body paragraphs, and a conclusion. Each one starts on a new line with an indent or a line break. If any are missing or merged together, the grader cannot give full structure marks.

The 5-Paragraph Map
Intro State your main idea (thesis). Hook the reader. Mention the passage topic.
Body 1 First reason or example + Detail + Explanation from the passage.
Body 2 Second reason or example + Detail + Explanation from the passage.
Body 3 Third reason or example + Detail + Explanation from the passage.
Conclusion Restate your main idea in different words. End with a final thought.

Before you start drafting, plan your three body paragraph topics. Write one-word labels at the top of your page:

QUICK PLAN EXAMPLE FOR WILMA RUDOLPH
Body 1: Childhood illness (polio, brace, doctors said no)
Body 2: Hard work (years of therapy, learning to walk again)
Body 3: Olympic success (three gold medals, proving everyone wrong)

Three labels = three body paragraphs = guaranteed structure credit.

B Intro (thesis about perseverance) → Body 1 (childhood illness) → Body 2 (years of hard work) → Body 3 (Olympic triumph) → Conclusion (restate thesis)
THE PARAGRAPH-BREAK TEST
After you finish your essay, count your paragraph breaks. You should see 4 breaks (which creates 5 paragraphs). If you only see 3 or fewer, you are probably missing a body paragraph or have merged two topics into one block. Go back and split them apart — each topic gets its own paragraph.

Proofread Pass — Catching the Last Points

Small errors add up on the mechanics row of the rubric

Rhys, once your essay has strong content and clear structure, the final points come from clean mechanics: spelling, grammar, punctuation, and sentence completeness. A quick proofread pass before you submit catches the small errors that cost you on the mechanics row.

THE BACKWARDS-SENTENCE TRICK
When you read forward, your brain auto-fills what it expects to see, not what is actually on the page. That is why small errors slip through.

Read each sentence starting from the last sentence and working backward. Not word-by-word backward — sentence-by-sentence. Your brain has to process each one on its own, without the story flow carrying you past errors. Spelling and grammar catches jump dramatically with this technique.

Here is what to look for on your proofread pass:

  • Spelling: Names from the passage (Wilma Rudolph, not "Wilma Rudolf"). Common words (their/there, because, definitely).
  • Capitalization: First word of every sentence. Proper nouns (names, places).
  • Complete sentences: Does every sentence have a subject AND a verb? No fragments left hanging.
  • Punctuation: Period at the end of every sentence. Commas after introductory phrases.
Which version has been properly proofread?
A wilma Rudolph showed persiverance throughout her life. she never gave up and became a champion.
B Wilma Rudolph showed perseverance throughout her life. She never gave up, and she became a champion on the world stage.
SAVE 5 MINUTES AT THE END
Aim to save about 5 minutes at the end for a backward-read proofread. Those 5 minutes are worth more than an extra sentence of content — they protect the points you already earned.
