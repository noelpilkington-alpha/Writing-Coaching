Fix 1: One Sentence Means One Sentence

Q9 held three sentences together with commas. Eight of your fifteen Grade 4 one-sentence answers did the same
THE RULE: A COMMA CANNOT HOLD TWO SENTENCES TOGETHER
A sentence ends at its period. If a second whole sentence follows, the seam needs a joining word: because, so, and, but, when. A comma on its own is not enough. Each one-sentence answer is three points: one for the answer, one for a reason, explanation or example, one for spelling, capitals and punctuation. A comma seam, or three slips in one line, costs that third point every time. The check: at every comma, read the words after it on their own. If they could stand as a sentence, the comma needs a joining word.
YOU WROTE (Q9, 2 OUT OF 3)
“There was a time when me and my friends decided to want to go shopping by ourselfs, my mom was shocked, we had never done it before and she didn’t get any notice earlier.” THREE SENTENCES, TWO COMMA SEAMS
Read the words after the first comma: my mom was shocked is a whole sentence. After the second: we had never done it before is another. Both idea points were earned, the answer and the reason. The seams cost the third. Also my friends and I, and ourselves.
THE SAME IDEAS, ONE SENTENCE
One time my friends and I asked to go shopping by ourselves, and my mom was shocked because we had never done it before.
Two seams, two joining words.
YOU WROTE (AUGUST, G4.2 Q10, 2 OUT OF 3)
“In fall during thanksgiving we always host a big party and invite friends and extended family, I always love it because I always get to make a giant pie with my friends.” A COMMA HOLDING TWO SENTENCES
The second half already has its because. The seam in front of I always love it needed an and. Thanksgiving takes a capital.

Fix 2: An Appositive Is a Naming Phrase

Q3 now, Q4 in August, Q3 in June, Q1 on Grade 3. Four tries, four different guesses
THE RULE: AN APPOSITIVE IS A NOUN PHRASE THAT RENAMES THE NOUN NEXT TO IT, INSIDE COMMAS
He asked his mom, a sleepy early riser, for some lettuce. The appositive sits right beside the noun and gives it another name. It has no verb of its own. It is not a who was clause, not describing words in front of the noun, not a second sentence joined with and. The check: swap the appositive in for the noun. He asked a sleepy early riser for some lettuce is still a sentence, so it passes.
YOU WROTE (Q3, 1 OUT OF 2)
“He asked his mom, who was still tired from the morning, for some lettuce.” A WHO CLAUSE, NOT A NAMING PHRASE
Right place, right commas, and the spelling-and-punctuation point was kept. But who was still tired has its own verb. It tells about Mom instead of renaming her. Swap test: He asked who was still tired from the morning for some lettuce. Fails.
THE APPOSITIVE
He asked his mom, a tired early riser, for some lettuce.
Swap test: He asked a tired early riser for some lettuce. Passes.
YOU WROTE (AUGUST, G4.2 Q4, 1 OUT OF 2)

Two describing words before the noun. Nothing renames the festivals, and the instruction asked for more information about them.

Fix 4: The Point, the Hows, and This Shows

Q11 asked how Yacouba’s ideas and character helped. The paragraph named both and explained neither
THE RULE: FIRST SENTENCE = THE POINT + THE HOWS. EACH DETAIL, THEN THIS SHOWS. THE ENDING ADDS THE HOWS UP
The prompt hands you the hows: ideas and character. The first sentence answers the question as a statement and names both. Each how gets one DETAIL · SHOW, a fact you can point to in the article, and one DETAIL · SO WHAT that says what the fact proves. The ending names both hows and says what they add up to. Not a question, not In conclusion I think, not advice to the reader. The check: after each detail, is there a sentence that starts This shows?
