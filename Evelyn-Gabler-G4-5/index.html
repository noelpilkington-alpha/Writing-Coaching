Appositives — What They Actually Are

A noun phrase that renames another noun

Evelyn, on Question 3 — the appositive question — you've been treating it like a different task. Here are your two attempts:

FROM G4.1 Q3
"Peter Tabichi, from Kenya, Africa, teaches high school science"
(You removed content from the original sentence — an appositive adds a renaming phrase; it doesn't strip information.)
FROM G4.5 Q3
"Sharyl is a jingle-dress dancer, so she spends hours..."
(You used the conjunction "so" — that joins two sentences. An appositive uses commas, not a conjunction.)

An appositive is not a new sentence and it's not a conjunction. It's a noun phrase that renames another noun, set off by commas. Here's the shape:

THE APPOSITIVE SHAPE
Noun , a noun phrase that renames it , rest of sentence.

Example: "Sharyl, a jingle-dress dancer, spends hours preparing her costume."
→ The noun is "Sharyl." The appositive is "a jingle-dress dancer" — it renames Sharyl. Both commas stay.
Which sentence contains a valid appositive?
A "Sharyl is a jingle-dress dancer, so she spends hours preparing her costume."
B "Sharyl, a jingle-dress dancer, spends hours preparing her costume."
THE APPOSITIVE RULE
An appositive is:
• A noun phrase (not a full sentence, not a conjunction)
• It renames the noun right before it
• It is set off by commas (both sides, like parentheses)
• The sentence should still make sense if you take it out
QUICK TEST
If you remove the part between the two commas, does the sentence still make sense?
• "Sharyl, a jingle-dress dancer, spends hours..." → "Sharyl spends hours..." ✓ still makes sense → appositive works.
• "Sharyl is a jingle-dress dancer, so she spends hours..." → "Sharyl is a jingle-dress dancer she spends hours..." ✗ doesn't work → not an appositive.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q3 on the G4 test is specifically an appositive question. The rule is short and memorable: noun phrase + two commas + renames. Once you have it, it's a reliable point every test.
