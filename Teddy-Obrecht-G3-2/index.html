Your Paragraph: 13 out of 20

The right idea is in there — the reader just can’t see who it is about

The question: write one well-organized paragraph about how the camel library program helps children, using the article “One Book, One Camel.” Here is what you wrote:

“It helps them learn to read even though they move a lot. The program is a great way to understand grammer, reading, writing, and language. It also helps camels exercise while kids learn at the same time.”
The main idea of the article, found Three different points, not a retell “It” and “them” are never named No details from the article Three sentences — ends early

First, the good part — and it is the important part. “Learn to read even though they move a lot” is the whole heart of the article. The families move from place to place, so the library comes to them. You found the exact right idea. Many students retell the story instead; you went straight to why it matters.

Now read your first sentence the way a stranger would: “It helps them learn to read…” — what is “it”? Who are “them”? You know, because you just read the article. The reader does not. A paragraph’s first sentence has one job: name the topic before you call it “it.” “The camel library helps children in Kenya learn to read even though they move a lot” — same sentence, and now everyone can follow.

The other missing points are details. The article is full of them — camels carrying boxes of books across the desert, children waiting for library day — and none made it into the paragraph. Each idea you wrote deserves one detail from the article standing behind it.

Tap to see your paragraph, named and grown
Your three ideas stay — they get names and details
YOUR PARAGRAPH, DEVELOPED

“The camel library helps children learn to read even though their families move a lot. Camels carry boxes of books across the desert to villages that have no library of their own. The program is a great way to understand grammar, reading, writing, and language, because the children get new books to practice with each time the camels visit. Without the camel library, many of these children would have no books at all — and that is why the program matters.”

Your first idea is still the opening — it just says who and what now. Each point got one detail from the article behind it, and the ending says why the whole thing matters. That is the road from 13 to 20.

THE MOVE TO PRACTISE
Before writing a paragraph, finish this sentence first: “This paragraph is about ____.” Then make sure that name — not “it” — is in your first sentence. Everything else follows from there.
