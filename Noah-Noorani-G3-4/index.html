#1 Skill: Make It a Real Question

An interrogative asks something — it does not just add a "!"

Noah, on Question 3 the task asked you to rewrite the sentence as an interrogative — a real question that ends with a question mark. Instead, you kept it as a statement and changed the ending to an exclamation mark. Here is what you wrote:

YOU WROTE (G3.4 Q3)
"The geode felt like a perfect souvenir to Layla!"
This is still a statement — it just ends with "!". It never asks anything, so it is not an interrogative.
A REAL QUESTION (INTERROGATIVE)
"Did the geode feel like a perfect souvenir to Layla?"
Starting with a helping verb ("Did") turns the statement into a real question, and it ends with a "?". That is an interrogative.
THE RULE: AN INTERROGATIVE ASKS SOMETHING
An interrogative is a sentence that asks a question. It usually starts with a question word or a helping verb, and it always ends with a question mark:

• Question words: Who, What, Where, When, Why, How
• Helping verbs: Did, Does, Is, Are, Can, Will

To build one, put the question word or helping verb at the front, then end with "?". Changing a statement's punctuation to "!" does not make it a question — the sentence still has to actually ask something.
Which one is a real interrogative (a true question)?
A Did the geode feel like a perfect souvenir to Layla?
B The geode felt like a perfect souvenir to Layla!
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Turning a statement into a question is an easy, repeatable point once you know the move: put a question word or helping verb at the front, and end with "?". Just swapping the punctuation to "!" leaves it a statement. Ask something, and you have a real interrogative.

#2 Skill: One Clean Idea per Sentence

Give each complete idea its own boundary — and match subject and verb

Noah, in your paragraph two complete ideas were joined by only a comma — that is a comma splice. Each complete thought needs its own clean boundary: a period, or a connector like "so." Here is what you wrote:

YOU WROTE (G3.4 Q11)
"People thought factory work was boring and repeated, sports gave them excitement they wanted in their free time."
"People thought factory work was boring and repeated" and "sports gave them excitement..." are both complete ideas — a single comma cannot hold them together.
CORRECTED
"People thought factory work was boring and repeated, so sports gave them excitement they wanted in their free time."
Adding "so" (or using a period) gives each idea a clean boundary. Now the comma has a real job, and the two ideas connect properly.
THE RULE: ONE CLEAN IDEA, MATCHED SUBJECT AND VERB
When you have two complete ideas, give each a clean boundary — a period or a connector like "so," "and," or "but." A comma alone is not enough.

Also match your subject and verb. A plural subject needs a plural verb:
• "Rules were the first big change." (not "Rules was")
🔎 This showed up in your paragraph too: "Rules was the first big change..." "Rules" is plural, so it needs were: "Rules were the first big change." Read back and check that your subject and verb agree.
Which sentence gives each idea a clean boundary?
A Factory work was boring, so sports gave people excitement.
B Factory work was boring, sports gave people excitement.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Two complete ideas need a real boundary — a period or a connector like "so." A comma by itself cannot hold them together. Match your subject and verb at the same time, and your strong ideas read clean and correct.
