Error #1: Spelling and Comma Splice

Q1 and Q5 — small mechanical errors

Eva, two of your answers lost points to mechanical errors that a quick read-back would catch.

Q1 — YOUR ANSWER (1.5 out of 2 weighted points)
"Peter Tabichi recevied the Global Teacher Prize for his work in Kenya."

The fix: "recevied" should be "received". The letters "ei" were swapped.

Q5 — YOUR ANSWER (1 out of 2 weighted points)
"Peter Tabichi gives most of his salary to students, he wants them to have better opportunities."

The fix: This is a comma splice — two complete sentences joined by just a comma. Fix it by using a period, or by adding "because": "Peter Tabichi gives most of his salary to students because he wants them to have better opportunities."

THE PROOFREAD CHECK
Before submitting, read each answer back and check:

1. Spelling: Does any word look strange? "Recevied" looks off. Sound it out: re-CEIVED. The "ei" is in the wrong order.

2. Comma splice: If you have a comma followed by a new subject + verb (like ", he wants"), you have two sentences. Either use a period or add a connecting word (because, so, and).
Which sentence fixes the comma splice?
A "He gives his salary to students, he believes in their potential."
B "He gives his salary to students because he believes in their potential."
WHY THIS MATTERS
Q1 and Q5 together lost you 1.5 points to mechanical errors. A 10-second read-back catches both: the misspelling looks wrong when you read slowly, and the comma splice sounds like two separate thoughts crammed together.

Error #2: Explain the HOW, Not Just the WHAT

Q6 and Q7 — you name the topic but stop before the explanation

Eva, on Q6 you correctly identified the challenge Peter Tabichi faces, but you did not explain what made learning difficult. On Q7, you named the lesson but did not explain how someone could apply it.

Q6 — YOUR ANSWER (2 out of 3 weighted points)
"A challenge Peter Tabichi faces is that his students do not have enough resources."

What is missing: You name the challenge (lack of resources) but do not explain what that means for learning specifically. What does it look like when students lack resources? How does it make learning harder?

A FULLER ANSWER
"A challenge Peter Tabichi faces is that his students do not have enough resources. This means they have to share old textbooks and cannot do science experiments, which makes it harder for them to understand lessons that other students learn by doing."
Q7 — YOUR ANSWER (2 out of 3 weighted points)
"One lesson from the passage is that you should never give up on what you believe in."

What is missing: You name the lesson (never give up) but do not explain how someone could apply it in their life or what it would look like in practice.

A FULLER ANSWER
"One lesson from the passage is that you should never give up on what you believe in. This means that if you want to help others, you should keep trying even when things are hard, the way Tabichi kept teaching even when his school had almost no supplies."
THE "SO WHAT?" TEST
After you write your first sentence, ask yourself: "So what? What does this look like in practice?"

• Challenge named? Good. Now explain: what does it make harder?
• Lesson named? Good. Now explain: how would someone use this?

The second sentence answers "so what" and earns you the full points.
Q6 asks about a challenge. Which answer explains what the challenge makes HARDER?
A "Tabichi's students do not have enough resources and that is a big challenge for the school."
B "Tabichi's students do not have enough resources, so they cannot do hands-on experiments. This makes learning harder because students have to imagine scientific ideas instead of seeing them in real life."
WHY THIS MATTERS
Q6 and Q7 together are worth 6 weighted points. You earned 4 out of 6. The missing 2 points are always in that second sentence — the one that explains what the challenge makes harder or how the lesson works in practice.
