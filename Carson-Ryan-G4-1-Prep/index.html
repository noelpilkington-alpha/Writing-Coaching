Lock In: Write the Appositive

Your only miss in the first ten questions — and G4's favorite combine
🔍
Here is the interesting part: on G3.5 Q5 you identified an appositive perfectly — you found “a best-selling story” tucked between its commas and earned full marks. You can already spot them. Writing one yourself is the only missing step, and G4 sentence-combining questions ask for exactly that.

Q4 asked you to combine two ideas using an appositive: “The books are still popular” and “They are known for their golden spines.” Here is what you wrote:

G3.5 Q4 — 1 OUT OF 2
“The books are still popular, sold around the world, and known for their golden spines.”
This is a good sentence — but it is a list of three things about the books, not an appositive. It also added a new fact (“sold around the world”) that was not in either sentence.
WHAT Q4 WAS LOOKING FOR
✓ “The books, known for their golden spines, are still popular.”

The describing phrase is tucked between commas, right after “books” — exactly like “a best-selling story” in the sentence you analyzed one question later.
THE TEST YOU ALREADY KNOW
An appositive is a naming or describing phrase that sits right next to the noun, between commas — and it cannot stand alone as a sentence. Try it: “Known for their golden spines.” Not a sentence — so it is an appositive. “They are known for their golden spines.” A complete sentence — so gluing it on with a comma is not an appositive, it is two sentences colliding.
Which sentence combines the two ideas with an appositive?
A The books are still popular, they are known for their golden spines.
B The books, known for their golden spines, are still popular.
YOUR MOVE ON G4.1
When a question says appositive, do these three things: (1) find the noun the extra information describes, (2) turn the second sentence into a phrase that cannot stand alone, and (3) tuck it right after the noun, between two commas. Then run your test: if the phrase could be its own sentence, it is not an appositive yet.

Watch: The Comma Before “Because”

Four answers in a row — a habit to drop before G4.1
🔍
This did not cost you points on G3.5 — your reasons were so good that all five write-a-sentence answers earned full marks. But the same extra comma appeared in four answers in a row, which makes it a habit, and habits ride along to G4. Two minutes now saves points later.

When because introduces the reason your sentence needs, the sentence runs straight through — no comma in front of it. Here is the habit in your own answers:

G3.5 — THE SAME COMMA, FOUR TIMES
“…stand out in stores, because they make the books sparkle.” (Q6)
“…in a Little Golden Book, because it makes the book theirs forever.” (Q7)
“I would read a book myself, because it's fun to read.” (Q8)
“…became popular is, because of their golden spines.” (Q9 — this one splits the verb from its reason)
STRAIGHT THROUGH — NO COMMA NEEDED
✓ “I would read a book myself because it's fun to read.”
✓ “One reason the Little Golden Books became popular is their golden spines.”

The reason belongs to the sentence, so nothing separates them. In the Q9 sentence, “is” already introduces the reason — it does not need “because” at all.
THE RULE
Because glues a reason onto a sentence, and glue works best with the pieces touching — no comma in front of it. The commas you DO keep: before but and so when they join two complete ideas, and the pair of commas around an appositive.
Which sentence is punctuated correctly?
A I would read a book myself because it's fun to read.
B I would read a book myself, because it's fun to read.
ONE MORE WATCH-WORD: TO vs. TOO
On Q10 you wrote “they are to childish” — that one needs too, the “more than enough” word (too childish, too loud, too fast). To points at something (to the store, to read). When you proofread, every “to” gets a two-second check: could I say “extra” here? Then it is too.

YOUR PARAGRAPH, PROOFREAD

“Jason Taylor put his statues underwater because he wanted nobody to accidentally hurt the coral reefs. There are two places you can see his underwater statues, one near Grenada with 65 statues and another in Cancun, Mexico with almost 500 statues. Also, to see the statues you have to scuba dive, but if you prefer to stay dry you can ride a glass-bottom boat. After the statues are put in the sea, seaweed and fish cover his statues. This does not bother Jason. He even said, “I like it most of all. Each visit is new, because the sea changes the art.” That is why Jason Taylor put his statues underwater.”

Five small fixes: accidentally, Cancun, a period between the two fused ideas, the closing quotation mark, and the comma before “because” in sentence one. Not one idea changed.

Your winning recipe — name it so you can repeat it on G4.1
Open One sentence that answers the question directly (yours: why Taylor put the statues underwater).
Facts Specific evidence with exact numbers and names from the passage (65 statues, almost 500, Grenada, Cancun).
Quote A short quote from the passage — opened AND closed with quotation marks.
Close A closing sentence that restates the main idea (“That is why…”).
SO WHAT? WHAT CHANGES FOR G4.1?
The recipe: nothing. The paragraph is still worth 20 points and this structure is exactly what it wants. The upgrade is the last five minutes: after drafting, read the paragraph back word by word — spelling, periods between complete ideas, and a closing mark on every quote you open. That read-back is the difference between 18 and 20.
