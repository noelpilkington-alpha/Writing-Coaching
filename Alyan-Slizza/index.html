Q3: What's an Appositive?

A short description tucked inside the sentence

The question asked you to rewrite the sentence and add an appositive:

Original
Sally swam to the other seals.
YOU WROTE
Sally swam around to the other seals.

You added the word "around," which changes the sentence a little — but it's not an appositive. An appositive is a short description placed right after a noun, set off by commas. It tells the reader who or what something is.

Tap to see the improved version
Read the explanation above first
WITH AN APPOSITIVE
Sally, the baby seal, swam to the other seals.
THE APPOSITIVE PATTERN
Name, [short description], rest of sentence.

Think of it like a whisper to the reader: "Sally — oh, by the way, she's the baby seal — swam to the other seals."

More examples:
• Anna, the oldest child, spotted the seal first.
• The fishnet, a tangled mess, was wrapped around the pup.

Q2: Expanding a Sentence

"Expand" means add details — not change the sentence type

The question asked you to expand this sentence with details:

Original
Dad freed the pup.
YOU WROTE
When did dad free the pup?

You turned the sentence into a question — but the task was to keep it as a statement and add more information. "Expand" means to make the sentence longer and more detailed by telling how, where, when, or why.

Tap to see the improved version
Read the explanation above first
EXPANDED WITH DETAILS
Dad carefully freed the tiny pup from the tangled fishnet on the beach.
THE EXPANDING TRICK
When you see "expand," ask yourself these questions about the sentence:

• How? — carefully, gently, quickly
• Where? — on the beach, near the water
• When? — that morning, after they found it
• What kind? — tiny pup, tangled fishnet

Add 2-3 of those details into the sentence. Keep it as a statement — don't change it to a question.

Polishing Your Paragraph

18/20 — small fixes for a great paragraph

Your paragraph about Satoshi Tajiri and Pokémon was excellent — strong topic sentence, great transitions, specific details. Here's how to make it even better:

1. Fix the double superlative: "most biggest"

You wrote something like:

YOU WROTE
...the most biggest game...

In English, you only need one way to show "the greatest." Either use "most" OR add "-est" — never both together.

most biggest → biggest
most fastest → fastest
most tallest → tallest
THE RULE
Short words (big, fast, tall) → add -est (biggest, fastest, tallest)
Long words (popular, important) → use most (most popular, most important)

Never combine them: no "most biggest," no "most fastest."

2. Remove the unnecessary comma.

YOU WROTE
...into a game, loved by many.
Tap to see the improved version
Read the explanation above first
SMOOTHER
...into a game loved by many.

When "loved by many" directly describes "game," no comma is needed — it flows as one idea.
