Fix 1: Before, After, and This Shows

Q11 asked how the events change Cody’s opinions. The paragraph told the story of the goal
THE RULE: NAME THE OPINION BEFORE AND AFTER, THEN SAY WHAT THE EVENT SHOWS
A how did it change question is two jobs. Say what Cody thought before and what he thinks after, and point to the event between them. Then This shows plus what the change means. The prompt said opinions, more than one: two change in the story, about Amanda and about Mud. The first sentence names both. Each gets a DETAIL · SHOW from the story and a DETAIL · SO WHAT. The ending adds the two up. The check: could a reader of your paragraph say what Cody thought before, what he thinks after, and which event sits between?

Fix 2: Combine: Keep the Meaning, Match the Joining Word

Q1 changed what the award was. Q5 used a reason word for a result
THE RULE: ONE SENTENCE THAT SAYS EXACTLY WHAT THE TWO SAID
Combine means one sentence, the same facts, no new ones. When the instruction offers because, but or so, name the relationship first: because gives a reason, so gives a result, but gives a contrast. The check: read your sentence against the two originals. Is every fact still true? Is the joining word the relationship between them? And is there one sentence, not two held together by a comma?
YOU WROTE (Q1, 1 OUT OF 2)
“The Science teacher, Peter Tabichi, received the award for the perstigious Global Teacher Prize.” THE MEANING DRIFTED
Sentence 3 says the award was the Global Teacher Prize. Received the award for the Prize makes it a different award given for winning a prize. One letter pair swapped in prestigious too. The commas around Peter Tabichi are right, and they matter on card 5.
SAME FACTS, ONE SENTENCE
At this event, science teacher Peter Tabichi received the prestigious Global Teacher Prize.
The award and the Prize are one thing, as in the original.
YOU WROTE (Q5, 0 OUT OF 2)
“His dedication transformed the school because of his efforts, enrollment has doubled, and more students are now attending college.” A REASON WORD FOR A RESULT, AND A COMMA HOLDING TWO SENTENCES
Sentence 14 is the result of sentence 13: the dedication came first, the doubled enrollment followed. That is so. And after because of his efforts, a whole new sentence begins with only a comma in front of it.
THE RESULT WORD
His dedication has transformed the school, so enrollment has doubled and more students are now attending college.

Three joining words that match, and one that does not:

RESULT: SOSome students had to walk four miles to school, so Tabichi knew the hardships they faced.
REASON: BECAUSETabichi used his own salary to buy food because his students were hungry.
CONTRAST: BUTHe had worked at a private school with ample resources, but the public school had one computer and no reliable internet.
WRONG RELATIONSHIPTabichi used his own salary to buy food, but his students were hungry.
Hunger is the reason for the food, not a contrast to it. Because.
Combine: In 2016 he visited a small public school. It had only one computer. Which sentence keeps the meaning?
AIn 2016 he visited a small public school for its one computer.
BIn 2016 he visited a small public school that had only one computer.
Combine with because, but or so: Amanda found herself surrounded. She passed the ball to Mud. Which joining word matches?
AAmanda found herself surrounded, so she passed the ball to Mud.
BAmanda found herself surrounded because she passed the ball to Mud.
Which is one sentence, not two held by a comma?
AMud darted between two defenders, the Red Team could not catch him.
BMud darted between two defenders, so the Red Team could not catch him.
PRACTICE IT IN YOUR ALPHAWRITE LESSONS
Every combine: say the relationship in your head first, reason, result or contrast, then pick the word. Then read your sentence against the originals for any fact that moved.

Fix 3: An Appositive Renames with a Noun

Q3 now, Q1 on your first test. And you wrote one in Q1 this time without noticing
THE RULE: AN APPOSITIVE IS A NOUN PHRASE THAT RENAMES THE NOUN NEXT TO IT, INSIDE COMMAS
Peter Tabichi, a high school science teacher, is from Kenya. The appositive sits right after the name and says who or what it is. It is not a new sentence, not a who clause, not a list of more facts. The check: swap the appositive in for the noun. A high school science teacher is from Kenya is still a sentence, so it passes.
YOU WROTE (Q3, 1 OUT OF 2)
“Peter Tabichi is a science teacher born from Kenya, Africa, and used to work in private school, but now teaches high school students.” MORE INFORMATION, NO APPOSITIVE
Every fact is true and there are plenty. None of them is a noun phrase set beside his name in commas, so the skill the instruction named is not there.
THE APPOSITIVE
Peter Tabichi, a high school science teacher from Kenya, received the Global Teacher Prize.
Swap test: A high school science teacher from Kenya received the Global Teacher Prize. Passes.
YOU WROTE (SEPTEMBER 2025, G3.1 Q1, 0 OUT OF 2)
“My family owns two dogs.Their names are Lucy and Rocket.” TWO SENTENCES, NO APPOSITIVE
The appositive version is one sentence: My family owns two dogs, Lucy and Rocket.
YOURS, Q1 THIS TEST

Peter Tabichi renames the science teacher, inside commas. That is an appositive, and you wrote it without being asked. Do the same thing when the instruction asks.

Fix 4: Answer, Then Because or So

Q7 and Q9 stopped after the answer. Q6, Q8 and Q10 did not
THE RULE: THE ANSWER, THEN THE REASON, IN ONE SENTENCE
Each one-sentence answer is three points: one for answering the question, one for a reason, explanation or example, one for spelling, capitals and punctuation. The reason lives after a because, a so or a which. The check: cover the first half of your sentence. Is there a why or a how left over?
YOU WROTE (Q7, 2 OUT OF 3)
“If people are having a bad time, you should help them out.” A LESSON, NO HOW
A clear lesson, and the question asked how it applies to your life. Nothing says what helping does.
WITH ITS BECAUSE
Peter Tabichi’s story teaches me to help people who are struggling, because a small help can change what they are able to do, the way his food and supplies changed what his students could learn.
YOU WROTE (Q9, 2 OUT OF 3)
“If I won a award, I will give it to my brothers and sisters.” AN ANSWER, NO WHY
A kind answer that stops before the reason. Also an award, and If I won takes I would.
WITH ITS SO
If I won an award, I would give some of the money to my brothers and sisters so they could buy what they need for school.
