Essay Writing Guide

Six parts. One part per sitting. Plan it, then write it

On the Grade 6 to Grade 8 writing tests, Question 11 is an essay worth 20 of the 30 points, and a pass is 27. This guide teaches the essay the way it is scored: plan first, then an introduction, three body paragraphs and a conclusion, each with named parts you can check before you submit. One practice passage and one essay run through every card, from the plan to the finished draft.

5 points
The parts
An introduction with a thesis, three body paragraphs with topic sentences, a conclusion. Parts 2 to 4.
5 points
Evidence, explained
Facts from the passage in every body paragraph, and a sentence that says what they show. Part 3.
4 + 3 + 3 points
Order, sentences, editing
Transitions and staying on the main idea; varied, formal sentences; spelling and grammar. Parts 1 and 5.
How to use it: one part per sitting. Read the rule, study the model and the common miss on the practice passage, then practice in the AlphaWrite activity each card names. Keep Part 6 open when you write.

The Practice Passage and the Prompt

Part 1 of 6: plan first. Every card in this guide works on this one passage
THE LOT ON BRIDGE STREET
Dani Okafor was fourteen when the grocery store on Bridge Street closed, leaving a fenced lot full of broken glass and weeds across from her apartment. For two years nobody touched it. “Everyone walked past it,” she said later. “I decided to walk into it.”

Her first idea failed. In March 2024 she asked the city to turn the lot into a park, and the answer was a letter explaining that a park would cost about $400,000 and the budget was spent. Her second idea was smaller. She asked the owner of the lot, a company in another state, for permission to plant a garden until the land was sold. It took eleven emails and four months. In July the company said yes, on one condition: the garden had to be gone within thirty days if a buyer appeared.

Dani did not start by planting. She started by asking. She knocked on 60 doors in the two buildings facing the lot and asked each neighbor one question: what would you grow? She wrote the answers on a map of the lot, and the map became the plan. Tomatoes and peppers for the Ruiz family, who had farmed in Guatemala. Mint and sage for Mrs. Adeyemi, who said she had not smelled fresh mint in twenty years. A sandbox corner for the daycare on the ground floor.

Clearing the lot took six Saturdays and 41 bags of trash. A hardware store donated lumber for twelve raised beds after Dani brought the map to its manager. A retired carpenter named Walt Brennan built the beds and then kept coming back; by the end of the summer he was there most mornings before Dani was.

The first harvest, in September 2024, filled nine crates. The daycare children ate cherry tomatoes off the vine. Mrs. Adeyemi made tea. The second summer, 31 households had a bed, and the garden ran a Saturday table where anyone could take vegetables without paying.

The lot was sold in the spring of 2026. The garden had thirty days. In that month the neighbors moved the twelve beds, one by one, to a strip of city land behind the library that the city, this time, said yes to. “The garden was never the lot,” Dani said. “It was the people who showed up on Saturdays.”
THE PROMPT
Explain how Dani Okafor turned the empty lot into a garden and what her story shows about leadership. Use information from the passage to support your ideas. Write an introduction with a strong thesis, three body paragraphs with topic sentences, and a conclusion.

Before anything else, count the parts of the prompt. This one has two: how she turned the lot into a garden, and what her story shows about leadership. Every body paragraph will have to serve both.

PRACTICE IT WHERE IT EARNS XP
Writing MPOs (Multiple Paragraph Outlines). Before the outline, underline the parts of the prompt and count them.

Plan Before You Write

Part 1 of 6: plan first. The hard thinking happens here, not while you type
THE RULE: PLAN FIRST, IN KEY WORDS
Always start by planning the essay. Planning does the hard thinking first and lets you check that every component is there before you write a sentence. Thinking while you type usually produces a weaker essay: components get missed and the logic breaks. The plan is the component list in key words: the thesis (the point, and the three hows), one topic sentence per body paragraph, two or three facts from the passage under each, and the takeaway. The check: can you point to every component in your plan before you write the first sentence?
A COMMON FIRST DRAFT, WRITTEN WITHOUT A PLAN
“Dani Okafor turned an empty lot into a garden and showed leadership. The lot on Bridge Street was empty for two years after the grocery store closed. First she asked the city for a park but they said no. Then she asked the owner and after eleven emails they said yes. She knocked on 60 doors and made a map. They cleared 41 bags of trash and built twelve beds. The first harvest was nine crates. The lot was sold in 2026 and they moved the beds. In conclusion, Dani turned an empty lot into a garden and showed leadership.” THE PASSAGE RETOLD IN ORDER
One block, every fact in the order the passage gave them, no sentence saying what any fact shows, and a last sentence that repeats the first. This is what thinking while typing produces: the facts carry the writer instead of the plan carrying the facts. The second half of the prompt, leadership, is named twice and never explained.
THE PLAN, IN KEY WORDS
THESIS lot to garden by: asking, not waiting / plan from neighbors’ answers / kept it going when land lost → leadership = getting others to show up
BODY 1 asking: city no ($400,000) → owner, 11 emails, 4 months, yes
BODY 2 neighbors’ answers: 60 doors, one question, the map; Ruiz, Adeyemi, daycare; 31 households later
BODY 3 land lost: sold spring 2026, 30 days, 12 beds moved behind library; “never the lot”
TAKEAWAY a leader makes it possible for everyone else to join in
Key words, not sentences. Every component is here before the first sentence exists, and every fact already knows which body paragraph it belongs to.
PRACTICE IT WHERE IT EARNS XP
Writing MPOs (Multiple Paragraph Outlines): the thesis line, three topic lines, key words under each. Then Write an Essay from Prompt turns the outline into sentences.

The Introduction: Three Statements

Part 2 of 6: introduction. General, specific, thesis
THE RULE: GENERAL, THEN SPECIFIC, THEN THESIS
A good introduction has three parts. GENERAL STATEMENT: a true sentence about the idea, before anyone is named. SPECIFIC STATEMENT: the passage’s subject, named, with the fact that sets up the essay. THESIS STATEMENT: the point, and the hows the body paragraphs will prove. The check: count three sentences, and read them as a zoom: the world, then this story, then your claim about it.
A COMMON MISS: THE THESIS ALONE
“Dani Okafor turned an empty lot into a garden and showed leadership. We can learn a lot from her.” NO WAY IN, NO HOWS
Two sentences that jump straight to the claim. The reader has no idea where the story is, and the thesis does not say what three things the essay will prove.
THREE STATEMENTS
GENERAL STATEMENTMost neighborhoods have a place everyone complains about and nobody touches. SPECIFIC STATEMENTOn Bridge Street, that place was a fenced lot full of glass and weeds, and the person who finally walked into it was a fourteen-year-old named Dani Okafor. THESIS STATEMENTDani turned the lot into a garden by asking instead of waiting, by building the plan out of her neighbors’ answers, and by keeping the garden alive when the land was taken away, and her story shows that leadership is mostly the work of getting other people to show up.
The world, then this lot, then the claim with its three hows and the second half of the prompt.

Three general statements that could open this essay, and one that is already specific:

GENERALEmpty places tend to stay empty until one person decides they are a problem worth solving.
GENERALLeadership is usually pictured as giving orders, but it more often looks like asking questions.
GENERALSome of the biggest changes in a neighborhood start with a person too young to vote.
SPECIFIC, NOT GENERALDani Okafor knocked on 60 doors and asked her neighbors what they would grow.
A fact about Dani. True, and it belongs in the second sentence or a body paragraph, not the first.
PRACTICE IT WHERE IT EARNS XP
Write an Essay from Prompt. Write the thesis first, then the two sentences in front of it: general, then specific.

The Thesis Names the Body Paragraphs

Part 2 of 6: introduction. The point, and the hows the essay will prove
THE RULE: THE POINT, THEN THE HOWS
A thesis does two jobs. POINT: it answers the prompt. HOW: it names the two or three things the essay will prove, one per body paragraph, in the order they will come. When the prompt has two halves, the thesis answers both. The check: underline the hows in your thesis and count them. That number is your number of body paragraphs, and each body paragraph’s topic sentence has to link back to one of them.
THE THESIS, WITH ITS PARTS MARKED
POINTDani turned the lot into a garden HOW 1by asking instead of waiting, HOW 2by building the plan out of her neighbors’ answers, HOW 3and by keeping the garden alive when the land was taken away, SECOND HALFand her story shows that leadership is mostly the work of getting other people to show up.
Three hows, three body paragraphs, in that order. The leadership half is answered here and will be answered again in every body paragraph’s explanation.
A COMMON MISS: A POINT WITH NO HOWS
“Dani Okafor showed great leadership in many ways and turned the lot into something special.” MANY WAYS NAMES NONE
A true claim and no plan. The writer does not know what the three body paragraphs are about, and neither does the reader.

Three theses with a point and hows, and one with only a point:

POINT + 3 HOWSDani built the garden by getting permission, by designing it with her neighbors, and by moving it when the lot was sold, and each step shows leadership as service.
POINT + 2 HOWSThe garden survived the sale of the lot because the plan belonged to the neighbors and because the beds could be carried, which shows that what a leader builds should outlast the leader.
POINT + 3 HOWSDani’s leadership shows up in three questions she asked: may I, what would you grow, and where can it go now.
POINT ONLYDani Okafor is a great example of a young leader.
No hows, so no body paragraphs are named.
PRACTICE IT WHERE IT EARNS XP
Writing MPOs (Multiple Paragraph Outlines): the thesis line is the point and the hows. Check that each topic line matches one how, in order.

The Topic Sentence Links Back

Part 3 of 6: body paragraphs. Each one must link back to the thesis statement
THE RULE: EVERY BODY PARAGRAPH LINKS BACK TO THE THESIS
Each body paragraph has four parts: a TOPIC SENTENCE, SUPPORTING DETAILS from the passage, an EXPLANATION of what they tell us, and a CONCLUDING SENTENCE. The topic sentence picks up one how from the thesis, in order, and says it as the point of this body paragraph. The check: read the thesis, then each topic sentence. Can you point from each topic sentence to the how it came from? A body paragraph that cannot point back does not belong.
THE THESIS, THEN THE THREE TOPIC SENTENCES
THESIS…by asking instead of waiting, by building the plan out of her neighbors’ answers, and by keeping the garden alive when the land was taken away…
TOPIC SENTENCE 1Dani’s first move was to ask, and to keep asking after the first answer was no.
TOPIC SENTENCE 2Her second move was to let the neighbors design the garden.
TOPIC SENTENCE 3Her last move was to keep the garden going when the lot was sold.
Three hows, three topic sentences, same order. Each one is a point, not a fact: it says what this body paragraph will prove.
A COMMON MISS: A FACT WHERE THE TOPIC SENTENCE GOES
“In March 2024 Dani asked the city to turn the lot into a park.” THE FIRST FACT, NOT THE POINT
True, from the passage, and the body paragraph now has no sentence saying which how it proves. The facts carry the writer again.
PRACTICE IT WHERE IT EARNS XP
Writing SPOs (Single Paragraph Outlines) for each body paragraph: the topic sentence is the how, written as a point.

Supporting Details from the Passage

Part 3 of 6: body paragraphs. Facts you can point to, quoted short or paraphrased with the fact kept
THE RULE: ONE OR TWO DETAILS A READER CAN FIND ON THE PAGE
Supporting details come from the passage: a number, a name, a date, or a short exact phrase in quotation marks. Two ways to bring one in. Quote: two to eight exact words, in quotation marks, introduced with the passage says or Dani said. Paraphrase: your own words, keeping the specific fact. The check: for each detail, put your finger on it in the passage. If you cannot, it is your opinion. If you copied three sentences, it is filler, not evidence.

Three details that belong under the first how, and one that does not:

PARAPHRASE, FACT KEPTThe city turned down a park because one would cost about $400,000.
PARAPHRASE, FACT KEPTGetting the owner’s permission took eleven emails and four months.
SHORT EXACT QUOTEDani said that everyone walked past the lot and “I decided to walk into it.”
OPINIONDani must have felt frustrated when the city said no.
Probably true, and nowhere on the page. Not a supporting detail.
A COMMON MISS: THREE SENTENCES COPIED
“According to the text, “Her first idea failed. In March 2024 she asked the city to turn the lot into a park, and the answer was a letter explaining that a park would cost about $400,000 and the budget was spent. Her second idea was smaller.” This shows how she was a leader.” THREE SENTENCES COPIED, THEN NOTHING
A long quote fills space without showing understanding, and the sentence after it explains nothing. Take the number, say it in your own words, and spend the saved words on the explanation.
PRACTICE IT WHERE IT EARNS XP
Writing SPOs: the detail lines are key words you can point to in the passage. In the essay, each line becomes one paraphrase or one short quote.

Explanation: Why Do They Matter? What Do They Tell Us?

Part 3 of 6: body paragraphs. After the details, the sentence that earns the points
THE RULE: EVERY DETAIL GETS EXPLAINED, AND THE EXPLANATION ANSWERS THE PROMPT
After the supporting details comes the EXPLANATION: why do these details matter? What do they tell us? This shows is the usual way in. When the prompt has two halves, the explanation is where the second half gets answered in every body paragraph. Two placements both work: an explanation after each detail, or one synthesized explanation after all the details. The check: every detail has been explained somewhere, and a reader can tell which detail each explanation belongs to.
A COMMON MISS: FACTS, THEN A STOP
“Dani asked the city for a park and the answer was a letter saying it would cost about $400,000. Then she asked the owner, which took eleven emails and four months. In July the company said yes.” THREE DETAILS, NO EXPLANATION
Three good details and nothing after them. The reader is left to guess what the eleven emails show about leadership, and the second half of the prompt goes unanswered in this body paragraph.
PLACEMENT 1: ONE EXPLANATION AFTER ALL THE DETAILS
SUPPORTING DETAILSWhen the city turned down a park because one would cost about $400,000, she wrote to the lot’s owner instead, a company in another state, and it took eleven emails and four months to get a yes. EXPLANATIONThis shows that the garden began as persistence rather than planting: the eleven emails did more than the shovels. It also shows the first thing a leader does, which is to find the person who can say yes.
PLACEMENT 2: AN EXPLANATION AFTER EACH DETAIL
DETAILThe city turned down a park because one would cost about $400,000. EXPLANATIONThis shows that the obvious route was closed before she started. DETAILGetting the owner’s permission took eleven emails and four months. EXPLANATIONThis tells us that her leadership began as patience with people who had every reason to ignore her.
Either placement earns the points. What loses them is a detail with no explanation anywhere.
PRACTICE IT WHERE IT EARNS XP
Write a Paragraph from Prompt: after the details, ask the two questions out loud, why do they matter, what do they tell us, and write the answer as the explanation.

The Body Paragraph’s Concluding Sentence

Part 3 of 6: body paragraphs. The fourth part, and the whole body paragraph in one place
THE RULE: CLOSE THE BODY PARAGRAPH BEFORE YOU LEAVE IT
Each body paragraph ends with a CONCLUDING SENTENCE: one sentence that lands the point of this body paragraph, in the light of the details and the explanation. Not the topic sentence again, and not the next body paragraph’s first fact. The check: read the topic sentence and the concluding sentence of a body paragraph together. The second should sound like the first after the proof has been given.
TOPIC SENTENCE AND CONCLUDING SENTENCE, SIDE BY SIDE
TOPIC SENTENCEDani’s first move was to ask, and to keep asking after the first answer was no. CONCLUDING SENTENCEBefore a single seed went in, Dani had already done the hardest work of the project with a keyboard.
Same point, after the eleven emails have been told. The keyboard line could not have been written before the details.
A COMMON MISS: NO CLOSE, STRAIGHT INTO THE NEXT FACTS
“…It also shows that a leader finds the person who can say yes. She knocked on 60 doors and asked what people would grow.” BODY 2 BEGINS BEFORE BODY 1 ENDS
The explanation runs straight into the next body paragraph’s first detail. One closing sentence, then a new line, keeps the two apart.
BODY PARAGRAPH 1, ASSEMBLED
TOPIC SENTENCEDani’s first move was to ask, and to keep asking after the first answer was no. SUPPORTING DETAILSWhen the city turned down a park because one would cost about $400,000, she wrote to the lot’s owner instead, a company in another state, and it took eleven emails and four months to get a yes. EXPLANATIONThis shows that the garden began as persistence rather than planting, and it tells us the first thing a leader does: find the person who can say yes. CONCLUDING SENTENCEBefore a single seed went in, Dani had already done the hardest work of the project with a keyboard.
Four parts, four jobs. Repeat for body 2 and body 3 and the evidence points are earned.
PRACTICE IT WHERE IT EARNS XP
Writing SPOs, then Write a Paragraph from Prompt: topic sentence, two details, explanation, concluding sentence. Count four before you move on.

The Conclusion Inverts the Introduction

Part 4 of 6: conclusion. Thesis synthesized, specific, general
THE RULE: THE INTRODUCTION, BACKWARDS, WITH SOMETHING EARNED
The conclusion has the introduction’s three parts in reverse. THESIS STATEMENT, SYNTHESIZED: not the thesis repeated, but a sentence that says how the essay addressed it, what the three body paragraphs add up to. SPECIFIC STATEMENT: back to the passage, one concrete image. GENERAL STATEMENT: what you want your audience to take away. The check: could your first conclusion sentence have been written before the body paragraphs? Then it is the thesis again, not a synthesis.
A COMMON MISS: THE INTRODUCTION AGAIN
“In conclusion, Dani Okafor turned an empty lot into a garden by asking, by using her neighbors’ ideas, and by moving the garden, and she showed leadership. We can learn a lot from her.” THE THESIS REPEATED, NOTHING EARNED
Every word of this could have been written before the body paragraphs existed. That is the tell. The three body paragraphs proved something; the conclusion is where you say what.
THREE MOVES OUT
THESIS, SYNTHESIZEDAsking, listening, and letting go: the three moves that turned a lot into a garden were all ways of getting other people to act. SPECIFIC STATEMENTThe twelve beds behind the library are tended today by people Dani knocked on doors to meet, and a retired carpenter is there most mornings before she is. GENERAL STATEMENTThe lesson of Bridge Street is that a leader’s job is not to do the work alone but to make it possible for everyone else to join in.
The three hows named as one idea, one image from the passage, then the takeaway.
PRACTICE IT WHERE IT EARNS XP
Write an Essay from Prompt: write the conclusion last. Ask what the three body paragraphs proved together, then one image, then what the reader should carry away.

Transitions Show the Reader the Order

Part 5 of 6: the rubric’s other seven points. Four of them are for staying organized
THE RULE: A TRANSITION TELLS THE READER WHAT KIND OF SENTENCE IS COMING
Four points of the essay are for order and staying on the main idea. Transitions do that work: time and sequence (first, after that, by the second summer), illustration (for example, the passage says), change of direction (but, however, instead), emphasis (most of all, above all), and conclusion (as a result, in the end). The check: the first words of each body paragraph and of the conclusion tell the reader where they are. And every sentence serves the thesis: cover the thesis, read the sentence, ask whether it proves a how. If not, cut it.
A COMMON MISS: FACTS WITH NO SIGNPOSTS
“She asked the city. The city said no. She asked the owner. The owner said yes. She knocked on 60 doors. She made a map.” SIX SENTENCES, NO ORDER WORDS, ONE SHAPE
Every fact is correct and the reader does the sorting. One transition per turn tells them which are first, which change direction, and which follow from which.
THE SAME FACTS, WITH SIGNPOSTS
First, she asked the city for a park; but the answer was a $400,000 no. Instead, she asked the lot’s owner, and after eleven emails the answer was yes. Then, before planting anything, she knocked on 60 doors, and as a result the map of the garden was drawn by the people who would use it.
PRACTICE IT WHERE IT EARNS XP
Writing Transition Outlines: one transition at the start of each body paragraph and the conclusion, chosen for the job it does.

Vary the Sentences, Then Edit

Part 5 of 6: the rubric’s other seven points. Three for sentences, three for editing
THE RULE: ONE LONG, ONE SHORT; THEN A FINGER ON THE KEY WORD
Three points are for varied, formal sentences: join two short facts with because, but, so, when or although, open some sentences with the joining word, use a naming phrase between commas, and let one short sentence land after a long one. Three points are for editing. The check: copy the key word of the prompt from the prompt itself, never from memory; then read the whole essay back once with a finger on each word.
A COMMON MISS: ONE SHAPE, AND THE KEY WORD WRONG
“Dani showed leadship. She asked the owner. The owner said yes. She made a garden. The garden was good.” FIVE SENTENCES, ONE SHAPE, THE PROMPT’S WORD MISSPELLED
Subject, verb, object, five times. Correct, and the three sentence points go to variety. And the word the whole essay is about is spelled for you in the prompt.
THE SAME FACTS, THREE SHAPES
Although the city had said no, Dani wrote to the owner, a company in another state, until it said yes. The garden that followed fed 31 households. That is leadership.

Three ways to vary a sentence, and one that only lengthens it:

OPEN WITH THE JOINING WORDWhen the lot was sold, the neighbors had thirty days.
A NAMING PHRASE BETWEEN COMMASWalt Brennan, a retired carpenter, built the twelve beds and kept coming back.
A SHORT ONE AFTER A LONG ONEIn one month the neighbors carried every bed to a strip of land behind the library. The garden survived.
ONLY LONGERDani asked the owner and the owner said yes and she made a map and the neighbors helped and the garden grew.
Five facts and four ands. Longer, not varied.
PRACTICE IT WHERE IT EARNS XP
Sentence Combining and Kernel Expansion: join two facts a day with a different joining word. Before every submit, copy the key word from the prompt and read the essay back with a finger on each word.
