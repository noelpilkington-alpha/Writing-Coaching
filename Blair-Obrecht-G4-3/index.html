Question 2 Fix — Unscramble Sentences

Let’s review this together.

You scored 1/2 on this question — partial credit.

The question asked you to fix the word order in this sentence:

“Noah’s new little pets seemed to be on this first day mostly eyes and tails.”

Which sentence has the best word order?
A Mostly eyes and tails, were all Noah's new little pets seemed on their first day.
B On their first day, Noah's new little pets seemed to be mostly eyes and tails.
YOU WROTE
“Mostly eyes and tails, were all Noah's new little pets seemed on their first day.”
HOW TO UNSCRAMBLE A SENTENCE
Step 1: Find the subject (who or what the sentence is about) — here it is “Noah’s new little pets.”
Step 2: Find the verb (what they did) — “seemed to be.”
Step 3: Keep subject + verb together, then place the extra details before or after.
See the read-aloud test
Read your rearranged sentence out loud. If it sounds awkward or the meaning is hard to follow, the subject and verb probably got separated. Try keeping them next to each other and moving the descriptive part to the beginning or end.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
When words are in the wrong order, even a correct idea can be hard to understand. Keeping the subject and verb together makes your writing clear and easy to read.

Question 3 Fix — Appositives

Knowing the difference between an appositive and other phrases

You scored 1/2 on this question — partial credit.

The question asked you to rewrite this sentence with an appositive that gives more information about Noah’s mom:

“He asked his mom for some lettuce.”

Which sentence contains an appositive?
A He asked his mom, looking confused, for some lettuce.
B He asked his mom, a vegetable lover, for some lettuce.
YOU WROTE
“He asked his mom, looking confused, for some lettuce.”
💡 Interesting: you actually used an appositive correctly in Question 8 (“A seed, germinating, will become a beautiful flower”). The issue here was recognizing the difference between an appositive and a participle phrase when the question asked for one specifically.
APPOSITIVE vs. PARTICIPLE PHRASE
Appositive = a noun phrase that renames another noun. Ask: “Does this phrase tell me who or what the noun is?”
• “His mom, a great cook, made dinner.” (“a great cook” = who she is)

Participle phrase = a phrase starting with an -ing or -ed verb that describes an action.
• “His mom, looking confused, asked a question.” (“looking confused” = what she was doing)
Quick test to tell them apart
Try replacing the phrase with “who is...” — if it works with a noun (“who is a great cook”), it is an appositive. If it works with an action (“who is looking confused”), it is a participle phrase. The question asked for an appositive, so you need the noun version.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
This question tests whether you know what an appositive is, not just whether you can write a good sentence. When the question says “add an appositive,” it means: add a noun phrase that renames or identifies the noun. Using a different kind of phrase — even a good one — will cost you points.

Many longer words are just a base word with a suffix. If you can spell the base word, you can spell the longer word:

REWRITTEN VERSION

Yacouba Sawadogo used his determination and knowledge of old farming methods to bring plants back to the dry desert land. According to the passage, he dug holes in the ground and filled them with compost, using a technique that farmers had used long ago. Even though the villagers did not believe in his methods at first, Yacouba kept going. Over time, green grass started appearing, and the villagers changed their minds. Now the village is full of healthy trees and green grass, all thanks to Yacouba Sawadogo’s refusal to give up.

Notice: the topic sentence answers HOW (determination + old methods), the evidence is specific and explained, and the closing connects back to the main idea.

THE EVIDENCE SANDWICH
1. Set it up: Write a sentence that introduces your evidence.
2. Evidence: Use specific details from the passage (quoted or paraphrased).
3. Explain: Tell the reader why this evidence matters or what it shows.
