#2 Fix: One Idea, One Sentence

Q3 — 0 out of 2  ·  Q10 — 1 out of 3

Ozzie, a comma is not strong enough to hold two complete sentences together. When you have two complete thoughts, the first one needs a period, and the second one starts fresh with a capital letter. Question 3 is where this cost you the most:

QUESTION 3 — 0 OUT OF 2
“Garett Morgan built an early traffic signal, he believed there had to be a safer way for evreyone to share the road, Morgan’s idea helped save lives and reduce crashes.”

Those two red commas are each holding together two complete sentences. Read them out loud and you can hear where each one wants to stop.

Which version is punctuated correctly?
AGarrett Morgan built an early traffic signal, he believed there had to be a safer way to share the road.
BGarrett Morgan built an early traffic signal. He believed there had to be a safer way to share the road.
BUT QUESTION 3 WANTED SOMETHING ELSE TOO
Question 3 asked you to combine the two sentences by adding an appositive — a naming phrase that renames the noun right before it, tucked between commas. We worked on this on your G4.5 card, so here it is once more:
WHAT QUESTION 3 WAS LOOKING FOR
“Garrett Morgan built an early traffic signal, an idea that helped save lives and reduce crashes.”

See how the phrase an idea that helped save lives and reduce crashes sits right after signal and renames it? That is an appositive. It is one sentence, not three, and it does exactly what the question asked.

QUESTION 10 — 1 OUT OF 3

ONE CLEAR SENTENCE INSTEAD

Question 10 asks for one complete sentence that includes an explanation. Your idea was good — the teleporting invention is a real answer. It just needed to come out as one clean sentence with the reason attached.

First, the big one. The question asked why inventors and problem-solvers are important to communities. Your paragraph opens with a question of its own — “What do inventors and problem-solvers do?” — and then tells us what they are. It never comes out and states why they matter. Second. Your traffic light example is genuinely good — it is real evidence straight from the passage. But you retell it and then move on. You never say what it proves. Evidence earns its points when you explain what it shows.

YOUR PARAGRAPH, REBUILT (closer to 19/20)

“Inventors and problem-solvers are important to communities because they notice everyday problems and build solutions that keep people safe. Inventors and problem-solvers are people who find holes in communities. For example, back in the 1900s there were a lot of crashes, so a man named Garrett Morgan had an idea to create a system. That system is now known as the traffic light. His invention proves that one person paying attention to a daily problem can protect thousands of people who never even learn his name. Every one of these inventors and problem-solvers is trying to help communities be better. That is why communities need them: without someone willing to fix what is broken, the same problem repeats year after year. In conclusion, inventors and problem-solvers are more important than you think.”

Look at what changed. Your facts are all still there — the holes in communities, the crashes, Garrett Morgan, the traffic light, everyone trying to make communities better. What got added is one sentence at the top that answers the question, and two sentences that say what your evidence proves.

SO WHAT?
Two moves are worth about five points on every paragraph you write. Move 1: make your first sentence answer the question directly — if the prompt asks why, your first sentence starts with the answer. Move 2: after any example, add one sentence beginning “This shows that…” or “This proves that…” You already find good evidence. Now tell the reader what it means.
