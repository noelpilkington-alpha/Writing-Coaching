PACING — A ROUGH PLAN
You have 60 minutes. A reasonable time plan:
• 10 minutes — read the passage carefully (twice if you can)
• 15 minutes — Questions 1–10 (about 1.5 minutes each)
• 5 minutes — plan your Q11 paragraph (more on this in Card 5)
• 20 minutes — write Q11
• 10 minutes — proofread everything using the rubric

Don't submit early. If you finish at 30 minutes, use the other 30 to reread, rewrite, and catch mistakes. Most of the points you lose on a first test come from rushing past a slip you could have caught.

Read the Passage & Questions Carefully

The #1 habit that separates high and low scores

Alex, every Alpha Writing test starts with a passage — usually a short article or story — followed by 11 questions that ask you to edit sentences, answer short questions, and finally write a paragraph based on that passage.

Here's the single biggest habit that separates high scores from low scores: reading the passage and every question carefully, more than once. Most of the mistakes you can lose points to on this test come from missing a small word in the question, not from weak writing.

You open the test. The passage is on screen with 11 questions below. What's the best first move?
A Scroll down to Question 1 and start answering — you'll read the passage when you need it.
B Read the whole passage first — slowly — before touching Question 1.
How to read the passage (4 steps)
1 First pass — for the story / main idea. Read once through at a normal pace. What is the article about? Who or what is the passage describing?
2 Second pass — for specific details. Read again, this time paying attention to names, places, dates, numbers, and anything a character did or said. These are the details you'll need for Q11 evidence.
3 Keep the passage open. The passage panel stays on screen while you answer. Every time a question asks about the article, re-read the relevant part before you answer — don't rely on memory.
4 Before Q11 — skim once more. Before you start planning your paragraph, skim the passage one more time. You'll need the specific facts fresh in your head.
How to read the QUESTIONS (don't skip this!)
READ TWICE Read every question at least twice before you start writing your answer. Your first read is for the big idea. Your second read is to catch the small details that change your answer completely.
LOOK FOR TWO-PART PROMPTS A two-part prompt has the word "and" or a second question mark inside the prompt. Examples: "What is one rule? Why is it important?" or "Would you…? Why or why not?" If the question has two parts, your one-sentence answer needs to cover both parts (usually by joining them with "because").
NOTICE THE VERB The question verb tells you exactly what to do: rewrite, combine, expand, change, explain, describe, identify. A "rewrite as a question" prompt wants a full new sentence ending in a question mark — not just one word.
🔎 Common slip that costs easy points: the prompt says "rewrite this sentence as a question" and a student writes a perfectly good question without the question mark at the end. Or the prompt says "change the verb to be stronger" and the student changes an adjective instead. Slow down. Find what the prompt is actually asking. Then write.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
At least 3–5 points are usually on the table just from reading the question more carefully — no new writing skill required. If you do nothing else from this guide, read every question twice before answering. That habit alone can move your score by 10%.

What Each Question Type Is Worth

Use the rubrics as your target — and as your proofreading checklist

Every question on the test is graded against a rubric — a list of what a full-credit answer looks like. If you know the rubric before you answer, you can aim straight at full credit. Later, you can also use the rubric as your proofreading checklist (see Card 6).

Questions 1–5 rubric — Editing tasks (2 points each)
2 / 2 Full credit. You rewrite the whole sentence following the exact instruction (e.g., "change the verb to be stronger," "rewrite as a question," "combine using 'since'"). The sentence is complete, correctly punctuated, and capitalized. Spelling is correct.
1 / 2 Partial credit. You follow the instruction mostly but miss one element — no question mark on an interrogative rewrite, missing comma in an appositive, a spelling slip, or a word the prompt asked you to change but you left alone.
0 / 2 No credit. You answered a different task (changed the wrong word, wrote a fragment, or didn't rewrite the full sentence).
Questions 6–10 rubric — Sentence writing (3 points each)
3 / 3 Full credit. One complete sentence that answers the question AND includes a reason, explanation, or example tied to the passage — usually joined with the word "because." Correct punctuation, capitalization, and spelling.
2 / 3 Partial credit. You answered with one sentence but the reason is thin, general, or doesn't actually tie back to the passage (example: "No, because I don't like it" when the question is about a character's choice).
1 / 3 Low partial credit. Very short response — you answered the question but gave no reason at all, OR answered only one part of a two-part prompt.
0 / 3 No credit. A one-word answer ("Yes." / "No.") or an off-topic sentence.
Question 11 rubric — Paragraph (20 points)
17–20 / 20 Strong paragraph. Clear topic sentence, 2–3 body sentences each with a specific detail from the passage (quoted or paraphrased) AND an explanation of what the detail shows. A closing sentence. Good spelling, punctuation, grammar.
13–16 / 20 Solid paragraph. Topic sentence and closing are there. Body sentences make general claims but either miss specific passage details or don't fully explain what the details show. Some spelling or grammar slips.
8–12 / 20 Partial paragraph. Structure is shaky — maybe no topic sentence, or only one body idea, or no specific details at all. Reads more like a short response than a paragraph.
0–7 / 20 Low credit. One or two sentences total, no structure, no passage details. A quote from somewhere else in place of a real paragraph.
📌 The big takeaway from the rubrics: every full-credit answer has three ingredients — answer the question exactly + use a specific detail from the passage + check spelling and punctuation. If you hit all three on every question, you've hit 90%+.

Plan Q11 With a Single Paragraph Outline

30–60 seconds of planning is the single biggest lever on your score

Alex, here's the secret of the strongest G3 paragraph writers: they never start writing Q11 without a plan. They spend 30–60 seconds building a Single Paragraph Outline (SPO) first. And then the paragraph almost writes itself.

The SPO is a 4-row map of your paragraph. It lives at the top of the answer box on the test — you type the outline, write your paragraph underneath, and delete the outline before you submit.

You read the Q11 prompt. The clock says you have 25 minutes left. What do you do FIRST?
A Start writing the paragraph. Fix it as you go.
B Type a quick Paragraph Outline (SPO) at the top of the answer box: topic, 2–3 body points with details from the passage, closing. Then write.
The Single Paragraph Outline (SPO) — 4 rows
TOPIC Topic sentence. One sentence that states the main idea of your paragraph — it answers the prompt directly. Example: "Modern sports became popular because people wrote down the rules and schools began holding matches."
BODY 1 First point + specific passage detail + short explanation. Use the passage's exact facts — a name, place, date, number, or something a character said or did. Quote a short phrase ("like this") OR paraphrase in your own words while keeping the fact. Add one line saying what it shows.
BODY 2 Second point + a different specific passage detail + short explanation. Same structure — new detail. Two details per paragraph is the target for G3.
CLOSE Closing sentence. One sentence that wraps up your main idea — not a copy of your topic sentence, but a natural ending. Example: "All of these changes are how sports became the games we play today."
HOW TO USE AN SPO ON A DIGITAL TEST
Step 1: In the answer box, type your outline at the very top in short form. It might look like:
TOPIC: modern sports — rules + schools
B1: rulebooks written down — stopped fighting
B2: schools held matches — 1800s Britain
CLOSE: that's how sports became popular today

Step 2: Skip a line and write your full paragraph underneath, using the outline as a map.
Step 3: Before you submit, delete the outline at the top so only your polished paragraph remains.
How to QUOTE from the passage (short, exact, introduced)
DO Pull a short exact phrase (2–8 words) and put it in "double quotation marks." Introduce it with a phrase like "The article says…" or "The passage explains…" so the grader sees it's the author's words.
DO NOT Copy a whole sentence or paragraph. And never drop a quote without explaining what it shows — always add one sentence after the quote saying why it matters.
How to PARAPHRASE from the passage (own words + specific fact)
DO Put the passage's idea in your own words while keeping the specific fact — a name, place, date, number, or action. "Schools in Britain wrote down cricket rules in the 1800s" has the fact and is in your own words.
DO NOT Copy word-for-word without quotation marks — that's not paraphrasing and can lose points. And don't drop the specific fact — "people played sports" is too general to count as evidence.

MODEL SPO

B2: schools held matches — 1800s Britain

↓ Paragraph that follows from this SPO:

Modern sports became popular because people wrote down the rules and schools began holding organized matches. The article says that people "fought about rules" until leaders wrote the rules down in official rulebooks, which stopped the arguments and let games be fair. By the late 1800s, schools in Britain held cricket matches with written rules, and winning gave players respect. All of these changes together are how sports became the games we play today.

SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q11 is worth 20 out of 45 points — almost half your whole score. An SPO takes 60 seconds and pulls your paragraph from "okay" (12–14/20) to "strong" (17+/20). That's 3–5 raw points. Plan first. Write second.

Proofread Before You Submit

Use the rubric as your checklist — easiest points you'll gain

Alex, after you've written every answer, do not submit yet. Go back to the top and read every answer once more. This is where most students lose the easiest points — simple slips that a proofread would catch.

The best way to proofread is to use the rubric as your checklist. For each question type, you already know what the grader is looking for (from Card 4). So you can check your answer against the exact list.

Your CUPS + Rubric Proofread (for every answer)
C Capitalization: Every sentence starts with a capital letter. The word I is always capital. Proper nouns (names of people, places) are capital.
U Understanding the prompt: Did you answer the exact task the prompt asked for? If the prompt said "rewrite as a question," does your sentence actually end with a ? If it said "change the verb," did you change the verb (not an adjective)? Re-read the prompt one more time.
P Punctuation: Every sentence ends with ., ?, or !. Questions MUST end with ? — this is a common way to lose 1 point on Q1–Q5. Appositives need commas on BOTH sides ("My dog, Lucy, is friendly").
S Spelling + Structure: Re-read long words slowly. Every sentence is a complete thought (not a fragment). Q6–Q10 is one full sentence — not just a word or two.
The prompt says: "Change this sentence into a question: Anna named the seal Sally." A student writes: "Did Anna name the seal Sally." Is this full credit?
A Yes — the words are a question, so it's full credit.
B Partial credit — the punctuation is wrong. A question needs a ?, not a .
Your Question-by-Question proofread map
Q1–Q5 Did you rewrite the whole sentence? Right punctuation at the end? Did you change what the prompt asked you to change (verb vs adjective vs sentence type)? Commas on BOTH sides of an appositive?
Q6–Q10 Did you write one complete sentence? Did you include a reason, explanation, or example using "because" and tying back to the passage? For two-part prompts ("What… and why?") — does your sentence cover BOTH parts?
Q11 Do you have a topic sentence? At least 2 body sentences with specific passage details (quoted or paraphrased)? A closing? Did you remember to delete your SPO from the top of the answer box? Read it out loud in your head — does it flow?
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Students who submit early leave 5–10 points on the table on a typical test. A careful 10-minute proofread using the rubric usually adds 5+ points to your score — zero new writing skill needed. Never submit until you've proofread every answer.
