PRE-TEST PREP — G3.1 COMING UP
Your first writing test is coming. Nothing on it is a trick, and nothing on it is new — it is skills you already use, organized into 11 questions. This card is your map.

Bailey, here is the honest truth about first writing tests: the students who struggle on them almost never struggle because of writing. They struggle because they did not know how the test works — they raced through it, skipped a hard question, or wrote two quick lines where twenty points were waiting. Every one of those mistakes is avoidable before you ever see the first question.

That is what this card set is for. No scores, no grades, no history — just the five things that separate a strong first test from a rough one. Students who walk in knowing these five things routinely score 10 or more points higher than students who learn them the hard way.

What G3.1 Looks Like

11 questions · 45 points · one question is worth almost half

Bailey, the test has three parts, and they are not worth the same. Knowing where the points live changes how you spend your effort:

Questions 1–5: Sentence Editing — 10 points
Fix a fragment, combine two sentences, turn a statement into a question, add an appositive, choose a connecting word. Each is worth 2 points. Short, precise work.
Questions 6–10: Write-a-Sentence — 15 points
Each asks a question about the passage and wants one complete sentence that includes a reason, explanation, or example. Each is worth 3 points.
Question 11: The Paragraph — 20 points
One well-organized paragraph using facts from the passage. Twenty of the 45 points live in this one box — almost half your entire score.
What the grading checks
Clear central idea, organized writing, developed ideas, evidence from the passage, and correct spelling, capitalization, punctuation, and grammar.
🔍
The single most important fact on this card: Question 11 is worth as much as Questions 1 through 7 combined. Most first-time students spend their energy evenly across all 11 questions and leave the paragraph thin. Plan from the start to give the paragraph your best, longest effort.

And one more thing worth knowing going in: 90% or higher is a pass. That is the line this whole testing loop is aiming for — not on the first try necessarily, but every test tells us exactly what to work on next.

Your Game Plan

The order of operations — and the one rule that costs the most points
STEP 1: READ THE PASSAGE TWICE
First read: understand the story or article. Second read: collect the names, numbers, and facts you will use in your answers. The questions all come from the passage — the second read is where the answers hide.
STEP 2: WORK EVERY QUESTION WITH THE 4-STEP PROCESS
Read the question twice — note exactly what it asks for. Plan your answer in your head before typing. Draft it in the box. Revise — reread what you typed before moving on. There is no prize for finishing fast, and there is always plenty of test time available. Aim to give the whole test at least 40 focused minutes.
STEP 3: NEVER LEAVE A BOX BLANK — EVER
This is the most expensive first-test mistake there is. A blank box scores zero, every time. A rough attempt can earn partial credit — and often does. In this testing group, a student once skipped just one question on a first test and it was the difference between a good score and a great one.
You hit a question you are not sure about. What is the better move?
A Skip it and keep moving — better to not answer than to answer wrong.
B Write my best attempt now, and come back to improve it if I have time.
STEP 4: THE FINAL PASS
After your last answer, go back to Question 1 and read every answer once, slowly, one word at a time. You are checking three things: no box is blank, every sentence ends with a period or question mark, and the tricky words (their/there, to/too, because) came out right. This pass takes about five minutes and it protects points on every single question.

The “Because” Habit

Questions 6–10 — the third point on every short answer

Bailey, questions 6 through 10 all follow the same pattern: they ask you something about the passage — or about your own opinion — and they want one complete sentence. Here is the part most first-timers miss: the question almost always says “explain”, and that word is worth a point all by itself.

🔍
The pattern across this whole testing group: answers that state an opinion but never say why earn 2 out of 3, over and over. Answers that carry their reason with them earn 3 out of 3. Same students, same test — the “because” half of the sentence is the difference.
A 2-OUT-OF-3 ANSWER (real pattern, no reason attached)
“Yes, I would have helped the seal.”
A 3-OUT-OF-3 ANSWER (reason on board)
✓ “Yes, I would have helped the seal because it was tangled in the net and could not free itself.”
The question asks: “Would you like to visit the rainforest? Explain your answer in one sentence.” Which answer earns full marks?
A I would love to visit the rainforest because I have never seen animals like that in real life.
B I would love to visit the rainforest.
THE TWO-PART CHECK
Before you submit any Question 6–10 answer, ask: (1) Did I answer the question? (2) Did I say why — with a because, a reason, or an example? One sentence is enough. It just has to carry both halves.

The Paragraph Recipe

Question 11 — 20 points, one box, one repeatable recipe

Bailey, the paragraph question will show you an article, then ask you to explain something about it — how a person solved a problem, why something matters, how something works. The single most important move: your first sentence should answer the question directly. Not background, not “this paragraph is about…” — the answer itself.

The recipe — four parts, in order
Open One sentence that answers the question directly. If the prompt asks why, your first sentence starts with the answer.
Facts Two or three specific facts from the article — names, numbers, and details beat vague words every time.
Explain After your facts, one sentence that starts “This shows that…” and says what the facts prove.
Close A closing sentence that restates your main idea in new words.
Tap to see the recipe in action
A full-credit paragraph, built one part at a time
THE RECIPE, ASSEMBLED (example: “Why do gardens help a neighborhood?”)

[Open] Community gardens help a neighborhood because they turn empty space into food and friendships. [Facts] The article says one garden grew 300 pounds of vegetables in a single summer, and neighbors who had never met worked on it side by side. [Explain] This shows that a garden does more than grow food. It brings people together who would otherwise stay strangers. [Close] That is why a simple patch of dirt can make a whole neighborhood stronger.

Answer first. Real facts. “This shows that…” Then close. Every full-credit paragraph in this testing group has this shape.

PLAN IT BEFORE YOU DRAFT IT
Before writing the real paragraph, type a quick four-line plan at the top of the answer box — your answer, your two facts, your “this shows”, your closer. Then write the full paragraph below it and delete the plan before you submit. Planning inside the box takes one minute and it is the difference between a paragraph that wanders and one that scores.

The Grading Checklist Is Printed on the Test

Question 11 tells you exactly how it will be graded — most students never read it

Bailey, here is a secret hiding in plain sight: the paragraph question does not just ask you to write — it hands you the grading checklist, right there in the prompt. Every G3 test prints this above the answer box:

WHAT THE Q11 PROMPT ACTUALLY SAYS
Your paragraph should:
• clearly state your central idea
• organize your writing
• develop your ideas in detail
• use evidence from the selection in your response
• use correct spelling, capitalization, punctuation, and grammar

Make sure to: review the selection · plan your response · write your response · revise and edit your response

Look familiar? It should — this is the recipe you just learned, written by the people who grade the test. “State your central idea” is your answer-first opener. “Use evidence from the selection” is your facts. “Develop your ideas” is your “This shows that…” sentence. And “review, plan, write, revise” is the 4-step process from your game plan. The recipe is not a trick — it is the rubric in disguise.

🔍
How to use it, twice: read the checklist before you write — it is your plan skeleton. Then read it again after you write — five lines, five checks, one point-protecting minute. If a line is not true of your paragraph yet, fix it before you submit.
The rubric says “use evidence from the selection in your response.” Which sentence actually does that?
A The garden was really nice and lots of people liked it.
B The article says one garden grew 300 pounds of vegetables in a single summer.
THE SHORT ANSWERS HAVE MINI-RUBRICS TOO
Questions 6–10 do the same thing in miniature. When a question says “in one sentence”, that is a graded requirement — one sentence, not three. When it says “explain”, the reason is graded — that is your “because.” The instructions inside each question are never decoration. They are the checklist the grading uses, handed to you for free.

Your Checklist for Test Day

Six habits — print it, keep it, use it
0 of 7 checked
Read the passage twice — collect names, numbers, and facts on the second pass
Never leave a box blank — an attempt can earn partial credit, a blank never can
Questions 6–10: one sentence that answers AND explains — carry the “because”
Question 11: use the recipe — answer first, facts, “this shows”, close — and give it your best effort, it is 20 points
Plan the paragraph in the answer box — type a four-line plan, write from it, delete it before submitting
Final pass: reread every answer word by word — blanks, periods, their/there, to/too, because
Before submitting Question 11: check it line by line against the “Your paragraph should” list printed in the prompt — five lines, five checks
