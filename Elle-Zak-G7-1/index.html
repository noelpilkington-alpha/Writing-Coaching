Fifteen of Ninety Minutes

Not a card about slowing down — a card about what five more minutes buys

Let’s be precise about the speed, because it is genuinely a strength. Fifteen minutes produced a 450-word, five-paragraph essay that scored 18 out of 20. Nobody should tell you to write slower. But a 90-minute window with 75 minutes unused means one thing was skipped entirely: the read-back. And on this test the read-back had a very short, very specific list waiting for it.

“Solar Panels help with all of this.”   “Solar Panels suck up light from the sun…”   “Similar to how Solar Panels work for houses…”   “Now that Solar Panels have arrived…”
Nine times in the essay, solar panels gets capital letters. It is a common noun, like light bulbs or rooftops — no capitals unless it starts a sentence. You wrote it correctly in your very first sentence, so this is a habit that crept in after the introduction and never got checked. HOW TO SPEND THE FIVE MINUTES
Write at your speed. Then, before submitting, do two passes. Pass one, multiple choice: re-read each question and underline the key word in your head — logically, benefits, best, NOT — and confirm your pick answers that word. Pass two, essay: read it once as if a stranger wrote it, looking only for capitals and spelling. On this test, those two passes were worth a point on the multiple choice and very likely a point on the essay. That is the pass.

#1 Fix: The Key Word in the Question

Q3 and Q7 — two true answers to two questions that were not asked

Both of your multiple-choice misses have the same shape. The option you chose was correct English and a true statement. It just was not the answer to the word the question turned on. Tap through:

Tap: Question 3
The key word was “logically”
Q3 — “WHICH SENTENCE COMBINES THE IDEAS LOGICALLY?”
Idea 1: Children could study in the evenings.   Idea 2: They could see better.

You chose: “Children could study in the evenings; they could see better.”
That sentence is grammatically perfect — a semicolon can join two complete sentences. But a semicolon says nothing about how the ideas relate. It just sets them side by side. The question asked for the logical combination, and these two ideas have a logic: one causes the other. Seeing better is why they could study. The correct option names that: “Because they could see better, children could study in the evenings.” Same two ideas, plus the relationship between them.
Tap: Question 7
The key word was “benefits”
Q7 — “WHICH SENTENCE BEST MATCHES THE TOPIC: BENEFITS FOR LOCAL BUSINESSES…?”
You chose: “Businesses could stay open for longer.”
Correct: “The extra hours gave customers more time to shop.”
Your pick is true, and it is in the passage. But staying open longer is the change — the thing that became possible. The topic asked for the benefit: what the change produced. Longer hours only help a business if customers use them, and the correct option says exactly that. You know this distinction cold — your own essay says shops “can stay open later and bring in more money.” The essay named the benefit. The multiple choice picked the step before it.

Here is what those two have in common. In each case, one word in the question — logically, benefits — was doing all the work, and the tempting wrong option is the one that is true but does not answer that word. Multiple-choice writers build the distractors this way on purpose. At speed, the true-sounding option wins.

THE FIVE-SECOND HABIT
Before you look at the options, find the word in the question that a wrong answer would ignore. Logically. Benefits. Best introduces. Does NOT belong. Say it to yourself. Then pick the option that answers that word, not the option that is merely true. You already do this on Q6, Q8 and Q10, which you got right — each of those hinges on a word like best or NOT. Do it on all ten.
Which sentence combines these ideas logically? Idea 1: The clinic could store vaccines overnight. Idea 2: The clinic had power after dark.
AThe clinic had power after dark; it could store vaccines overnight.
BBecause the clinic had power after dark, it could store vaccines overnight.

#2 Fix: Formal Sentences, Passage Facts

Where the essay’s last two points most likely live

An 18 out of 20 essay does not have a weakness so much as a finish. The rubric has two lines your essay brushes against: “use clear, formal language” and “support each point with facts from the passage.”

Formal language first. Look at how three sections of your essay open:

YOUR TOPIC SENTENCES
“First, the children.”   “What about shops?”   “Finally, the medical clinic.”
Two of these have no verb, and one is a question. They are punchy, and in a magazine they would work. On an expository essay graded for formal language, a topic sentence is expected to be a complete statement that makes a claim: “First, the panels changed what the village’s children could do after dark.” Same energy, full sentence. The same goes for “solar panels suck up light” (absorb) and “bitten by a poisonous thing” (a venomous snake).

Now the facts. Your first body paragraph explains eye strain in real detail — retinas, headaches, lower back and shoulder pain, fatigue. That is interesting, and none of it is in the passage. Meanwhile the passage handed you specifics you left unused:

Facts from the passage that did not make it into the essay
Clinic“The clinic now stores vaccines safely overnight” — you reasoned your way toward this, but the passage states it
Shops“The extra hours gave customers more time to shop” — the benefit, in the passage’s own words

THE RULE FOR THE LAST TWO POINTS
Outside knowledge is a bonus after the passage’s facts are in, never instead of them. For each body paragraph: one specific fact quoted or closely paraphrased from the text, then your explanation of what it shows. And every topic sentence a complete, formal statement. That is the difference between 18 and 20 — and with the multiple choice fixed, between 87 and the mid-nineties.
Which works as the topic sentence of a body paragraph in a formal expository essay?
AFinally, the medical clinic.
BFinally, the medical clinic gained the ability to care for patients at any hour.

Your Essay: 18 out of 20

Five paragraphs in fifteen minutes — and what the twenty-point version looks like

The prompt: explain how the solar panel project improved life in the village, describing at least three ways, with details from the text. Here is your essay:

INTRODUCTION
Solar panels are a wonderful alternative to electricity. In the passage, the small village once went without electricity. Children strained their eyes studying, shops closed earlier before it got dark, and a small medical clinic operated until sunset. Solar panels can help with all of this.

BODY 1
First, the children. Many children don’t get home or don’t have time to study until late at night. If children study when it’s dark, they can strain their eyes. This can be very bad for them. When you strain your eyes, you temporarily damage your retinas. This can cause headaches, lower back and shoulder pain, difficulty concentrating, and even fatique. Solar Panels help with all of this. Solar Panels suck up light from the sun during the day, store it, and then have fresh energy for nighttime. This way, students don’t have to strain their eyes to study.

BODY 2
What about shops? If shops have to close at sundown, they can’t make as much money. This inhibts their ability to be able to pay rent and stay open. Similar to how Solar Panels work for houses, they get light during the day so shops can run throughout most of the night and bring in more money. Now that Solar Panels have arrived in this rural town, shops can make more money and stay open later without worrying about lamps or candles.

BODY 3
Finally, the medical clinic. People need around the clock medical care. What if someone was bitten by a poisonous thing in their house? If there wasn’t electric or air conditioning to house the vaccines and antivenom, then how could that person get help? Solar Panels help here too. The same way they work for houses and shops, Solar Panels suck up energy and light when the sun is out and then store it so it can be used during the night when there is no light. This means that now people can get around the clock care at the medical clinic without having to worry about what time it is.

CONCLUSION
Today, the village is a model and example of what can happen when an innovation comes to help. Students don’t have to strain their eyes to study, shops can stay open past dark and make more money, and the medical clinic can help everyone no matter what time it is. This shift from candles and lamps to Solar Panels not only improved the daily aspect, but the overall community in this town.
Five paragraphs, all present Thesis previews the three areas Every body explains the mechanism Conclusion synthesizes, not repeats Outside facts instead of passage facts Fragment topic sentences · informal verbs “Solar Panels” ×9 · fatique · inhibts

What earned the 18. The introduction names the three problems in one sentence, and the three body paragraphs take them in that exact order. Each body says how the panels change things — store energy by day, use it by night — instead of just asserting that they help. And the conclusion does the hardest thing: it steps back and says what the whole story means. That is an essay with a spine, written faster than most people outline.

The last two points are the ones cards 3 and 5 named. The evidence is mostly your own reasoning rather than the passage’s facts. Three topic sentences are not sentences. The editing line takes a hit from nine stray capitals and two misspellings. None of that is structural. All of it is a read-back.

Your structure and your point — a formal topic sentence and the passage’s own fact
BODY 3, POLISHED

“Finally, the medical clinic gained the ability to care for patients at any hour. Before the panels, the clinic operated only until sunset, which meant an emergency at night — a venomous snakebite, for example — had nowhere to go. The passage notes that the clinic now stores vaccines safely overnight, something that was impossible without reliable power. Solar panels absorb energy while the sun is out and store it for use after dark, so the medicine stays cold and the doors stay open. This means the village no longer has to hope that illness keeps daytime hours.”

Same section, same argument, same place in the essay. What changed: the topic sentence became a sentence, the passage’s own fact about the vaccines took the place of a rhetorical question, suck up became absorb, and the capitals came off. That is the finish the rubric is paying for.
