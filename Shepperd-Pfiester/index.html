Q3: Declarative vs. Imperative

You know what an imperative is — but you wrote one when asked for a declarative

Q3 asked you to change an interrogative sentence into a declarative sentence. Here's what you wrote:

YOUR ANSWER (IMPERATIVE)
"Help elderly people travel independently with self-driving cars."

That's a command — it tells someone to do something. That makes it imperative, not declarative. You actually proved you know what an imperative is on Q2 (which you got right!). The issue is mixing up which type was being asked for.

Tap to see the improved version
Read the explanation above first
CORRECT ANSWER (DECLARATIVE)
"Self-driving cars would help elderly people travel independently."

A declarative sentence is a statement — it tells the reader something. It has a subject ("Self-driving cars") + verb ("would help") and ends with a period.

Sentence Type What It Does Example
Declarative Makes a statement (TELLS) "Self-driving cars would help elderly people."
Imperative Gives a command (COMMANDS) "Help elderly people travel independently."
Interrogative Asks a question (ASKS) "Would self-driving cars help elderly people?"
Exclamatory Shows strong feeling (EXCLAIMS) "Self-driving cars would change everything!"
TIP
Declarative = tells. Imperative = commands. If your sentence starts with a verb and has no visible subject ("Help..."), it's imperative. A declarative always has a clear subject doing or being something.

Q6: "If" vs. "Unless"

These are both subordinating conjunctions — but they create opposite conditions

The sentence: "Self-driving cars could transform transportation. ______ the technology fails, difficult questions arise about safety and trust."

YOUR ANSWER
"Unless the technology fails, difficult questions arise about safety and trust."
Tap to see the improved version
Read the explanation above first
CORRECT ANSWER
"If the technology fails, difficult questions arise about safety and trust."

Both "if" and "unless" are subordinating conjunctions, but they create opposite meanings:

Word Meaning What the sentence says
"If" In the event that When the tech fails → questions arise. (Makes sense!)
"Unless" Except if / Only if NOT Questions arise EXCEPT when the tech fails. (Reversed logic!)

"Unless" means "except if" — so "unless the technology fails" would mean questions arise in every situation except when the tech fails. That's the opposite of what the passage means.

TIP
When choosing between "if" and "unless," try replacing the word with its meaning. "If" = "in the event that." "Unless" = "except if." Plug both into the sentence and see which one makes logical sense with the rest of the paragraph.

Organize Benefits and Concerns Separately

One main point per paragraph

Your P3 mixes benefits and concerns into one paragraph:

Your Paragraph 3 (Mixed)
The tech also helps people who are not able to drive. Elderly people who are not allowed to drive themselves or others would be able to travel indepentently again. People who have disabilites could have more freedom, and overall it would solve many issues. Some people have concerns, such as what if the tech fails, or what if people get accidents. Benefits and concerns should be in separate paragraphs. Here's the essay plan:

Essay Outline
INTRO Hook → Thesis: "It is important for readers to understand both the benefits and concerns of self-driving cars because..."
BP1 Benefits — E1: saving lives / preventing distracted driving → X1: why this matters → E2: helping elderly / disabled people → X2: why this matters → CS
BP2 Concerns — E1: technology failures / accidents → X1: why this matters → E2: legal questions / job losses → X2: why this matters → CS
BP3 Why understanding BOTH matters — informed decisions, balanced view, technology still being tested
CONC Restate thesis → final thought about why this understanding matters for the future

BP = Body Paragraph • E = Evidence • X = Explanation • TS = Topic Sentence • CS = Concluding Sentence

Body paragraph pattern: TS → E1 → X1 → E2 → X2 → CS

Your P1 should be a short intro (hook + thesis), not a 7-sentence retelling of how sensors work. Your P5 should restate your thesis and summarize your main points, not add new facts about testing cities.

WHY THIS MATTERS
When benefits and concerns are in the same paragraph, neither one gets fully developed. Separating them gives you room to include evidence from the passage and explain what it shows — which is exactly what earns points on the essay.
