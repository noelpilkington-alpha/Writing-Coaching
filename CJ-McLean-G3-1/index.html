Eighteen Minutes

Eleven questions, one of them a whole paragraph — and what that speed cost

Eleven questions in 18 minutes works out to a little over a minute and a half each, and one of those eleven was a full paragraph. So the paragraph and the ten questions before it were all done at roughly the pace of a text message.

Here is why that matters, and it is not a lecture about being careful. It is that the instructions on this test tell you which move to make, and reading fast is exactly how you miss the one word that says which. Look at two of them.

QUESTION 4 — 1 OUT OF 2
The instruction: “Use because, but, or so to combine sentences 8 and 9 into one sentence.”

You wrote: “Secondly, dogs might growl and bite when nervous or tired.”
Read that sentence on its own and it is good. Two ideas, joined smoothly, meaning intact. You did the whole job. But the instruction named three words you could use, and “when” is not one of them. Swap in because and it is worth full marks: “Secondly, dogs might growl or bite because they are nervous or tired.” One word.
QUESTION 2 — 1 OUT OF 2
The instruction: “Rewrite sentence 4 as a question.”  Sentence 4: “There is one place we don’t take the dogs.”

You wrote: “What is the one place we never take our dogs?”
This is a real question, properly built, with a question mark. Most of the work is right. But a rewrite has to say the same thing in a new form, and yours asks something new: the original tells you there is one such place, and yours asks which place it is. Same fact, different job.
THE MOVE FOR TURNING A STATEMENT INTO A QUESTION
Take the verb and move it to the front. Nothing else has to change.

“There is one place we don’t take the dogs.”  →  “Is there one place we don’t take the dogs?”
Two words traded places. The question asks about exactly the fact the sentence stated, which is what “rewrite it as a question” means.
THE ONE HABIT THAT FIXES ALL OF THIS
Before you answer, find the word in the instruction that names the move and say it back to yourself: appositive. as a question. because, but, or so. one sentence. Then answer. Your writing was good enough for full marks on all three of these questions — the only thing missing was three seconds of reading. Slowing down on the instruction is not the same as working slower. It is the fastest three points on this test.

#2 Fix: What Is an Appositive?

Q1 — you were one comma away

Question 1 gave you two sentences and asked you to rewrite them as one sentence that includes an appositive.

The two sentences to fold into one:
“My family has two really cute dogs.”
“Their names are Lucy and Rocket.”
QUESTION 1 — 1 OUT OF 2
“My family has 2 dogs named Lucy and Rocket.”

This is close. You did the part that trips most people up: you turned two sentences into one, smoothly, and the meaning survived. That is the point you earned.

The point you missed is the word appositive. An appositive is a short naming phrase that sits between commas and renames the noun right in front of it. Your version describes the dogs using the word “named,” which does a similar job but is not an appositive.

THE FIX — ADD ONE COMMA, DROP ONE WORD
“My family has two really cute dogs, Lucy and Rocket.”
The comma replaces “named,” and now Lucy and Rocket is renaming dogs. That highlighted piece is the appositive. Also worth doing: write two as a word rather than the numeral 2.
THE APPOSITIVE PATTERN, AND HOW TO CHECK IT
[noun], [naming phrase], [rest of the sentence].

The check: take the naming phrase out. If what is left is still a complete sentence, you built it right. “My family has two really cute dogs” — still a sentence. Correct.
Which sentence uses an appositive?
AMy family has two dogs and their names are Lucy and Rocket.
BMy family has two dogs, Lucy and Rocket.

Your Paragraph: 15 out of 20

The structure is right — it stops before it develops

The question: Explain the ways that soil is important to Earth. Here is what you wrote:

“Soil helps the earth. 1( It is home to a lot of animals like worms. 2( It gives life to the world like plants. 3( there are also a lot of differant kinds of soil like sandy soil, smooth soil, and silt soil.”
Central idea in the very first sentence Three separate ways, not a retell Real examples from the article Numbers left in place of transition words Each reason stops after one sentence Lowercase start · differant

The frame here is genuinely good, and that is why this is 15 and not 8. “Soil helps the earth” is a real central idea, and you put it first, which is where it belongs. Then you gave three different ways soil matters instead of retelling the article in order — that is explaining, and it is what the question asked for. Worms, plants, and the kinds of soil all come from the selection.

So where are the five points? Two things, and neither is about your ideas.

One: the 1( 2( 3( markers are still on the page. You were numbering your reasons, which is smart thinking — but in a paragraph the numbers come out and words go in their place: First, Second, Finally. Same job, and it reads like writing instead of a list.

Two: every reason is exactly one sentence long and then moves on. The question asked you to develop your ideas in detail, so each reason wants one more sentence saying how it works. You know these details — they are in the article you just read.

Tap to see your paragraph developed
Your three reasons, in your order — nothing swapped out
YOUR PARAGRAPH, DEVELOPED

“Soil helps Earth in many ways. First, it is home to a lot of animals like worms. Worms dig tunnels that let air and water reach the roots of plants. Second, it gives life to the world like plants. Fallen leaves, twigs, and dead bugs rot in the soil and turn into humus, which is food for plants. Finally, there are also a lot of different kinds of soil, like sandy soil, smooth soil, and silt soil. Each kind holds water in its own way, so different plants can grow in different places. Without soil, none of this life could get started.”

Your three reasons are all still there, in the order you chose. What got added: three transition words in place of the numbers, one detail sentence per reason, and a closing line. That is the difference between 15 and 20.

THE MOVE TO PRACTISE
After you write a reason, ask “how does that work?” and write one more sentence answering it. Soil is home to worms — how does that help? Worms dig tunnels that let air and water down to the roots. That second sentence is where the points live, and you have read the article, so you already have the answer.
