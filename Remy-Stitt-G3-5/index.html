#1 Fix: Where One Sentence Ends

Q6 — two complete sentences with nothing between them

Question 6 asked why the golden spines helped the books stand out. Your idea was right. Read the sentence out loud and notice where you have to take a breath:

Q6 — 2 OUT OF 3
“The books are different because of the golden spines this causes them to stick out more.”

There are two complete sentences here. The books are different because of the golden spines. Full stop. This causes them to stick out more. Full stop. They are pushed together with nothing at the join — no period, no comma-and-connector — so the reader hits “spines this causes” and has to back up. That is what cost the point.

TWO WAYS TO FIX IT
“The books are different because of the golden spines. This causes them to stand out more.”
“The golden spines make the books different, so they stand out more on the shelf.”
A period and a new sentence, or a comma with a connecting word. Either one tells the reader where the first thought ends.

The same thing happened on Q8, where it did not cost you: “Listening to a book is better because it gives you more time you can cook, shower, and drive while listening.” Two thoughts fused at “time you can.” It kept full marks because the reason was so clear — but it is the same join.

THE CHECK
Read the answer back and find the spot where your voice naturally stops. If what comes before the stop is a complete sentence and what comes after is another one, put a period there. Two clean sentences always beat one fused one.
Which version is punctuated correctly?
AThe books are different because of the golden spines this causes them to stand out.
BThe books are different because of the golden spines. This causes them to stand out.

#2 Fix: An Appositive Is a Noun

Q4 — you can spot one; now build one — plus one point on Q2

Here is a small puzzle from your test. On Q5 you were asked to find the appositive in a sentence, and you nailed it: “a best-selling story.” One question earlier, Q4 asked you to write one:

Q4 — 1 OUT OF 2 — “COMBINE THE IDEAS BY USING AN APPOSITIVE”
Ideas: The books are still popular.  +  They are known for their golden spines.

You wrote: “The books, very popular, are known for their golden spines.”

The structure is right — commas in place, sentence complete, both ideas combined. That is the point you earned. But look at what you found on Q5: a best-selling story. That is a noun phrase — it renames the book. “Very popular” is an adjective phrase — it describes the books but does not rename them. An appositive is always a noun that could stand in for the thing it follows.

THE APPOSITIVE VERSION
“The books, a still-popular series, are known for their golden spines.”
“A still-popular series” renames the books. Cover the original noun and the appositive can take its place: A still-popular series is known for its golden spines. That is the test.

And Q2, the unscramble. The words were: puppy / little / poky / the / popular / very / was. You wrote “The very little popular puppy was poky.” Every word used, capital and period in place — but the meaning came out backwards. The passage is about a book called The Poky Little Puppy, so those three words are a name and travel together: “The poky little puppy was very popular.” When an unscramble contains a title from the passage, the title is the anchor; build the sentence around it.

Which sentence uses an appositive?
AThe books, very popular, are known for their golden spines.
BThe books, a still-popular series, are known for their golden spines.

The thinking is right, and it is the hard part. Sentence two answers the exact question asked — he put the art underwater so coral reefs are not destroyed — and sentence three explains the mechanism: divers looking at statues spend less time on the reef. That is a chain of reasoning, and it is why this is 16 and not 12.

So where are the four points? A reader could not tell from this paragraph that you read the passage. It never says the statues are life-sized cement figures, that they sit on the ocean floor near Cancun, Mexico, or that coral and sea plants grow on them until the art becomes a living reef. Those are the passage’s facts, and “use evidence from the selection” is a rubric line worth points. Instead the paragraph spends two sentences on Jason being famous, which the passage does not discuss. You did the citing move perfectly on Q9 — two passage facts in one sentence. The paragraph needed the same.

YOUR PARAGRAPH, DEVELOPED

“Jason deCaires Taylor is an artist who decided to show his art in a different way. The passage explains that he placed life-sized cement statues on the ocean floor near Cancun, Mexico, so that coral reefs are not destroyed. If people dive to look at his statues, they spend less time on the natural reefs, which gives the fragile coral a chance to recover. Over time, coral and sea plants grow on the statues themselves, so the art slowly turns into a new reef and a home for sea life. That is why Jason chose the sea as his museum: his sculptures protect the reef and become part of it.”

Your answer to the question is untouched — it is still sentence two, still your reason, still your divers. What got added: where the statues are and what they are made of, what happens to them over time, and a closing that ties the reason to the result. “Famous” came out because the passage never says it.

SO WHAT?
The four paragraph points are the same every time: two specific details from the passage, each followed by what it shows. You have the reasoning already. The details are sitting in the text, and finding them is exactly what the unused test time is for.
