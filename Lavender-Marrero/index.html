Stop the Comma Splice

Q7, Q8, Q9, Q10 — The same mistake four times

A comma splice happens when you join two complete sentences with just a comma. You did this on four different questions. Let's look at each one:

Q7 — Describe a change you see in spring:

YOU WROTE
One change I see at the start of spring, is more plants growning.

This one actually has an unnecessary comma between the subject and verb, plus a spelling error ("growning" should be growing). The comma breaks the sentence instead of helping it.

FIXED
One change I see at the start of spring is more plants growing.

Q8 — Describe a unique plant or flower:

YOU WROTE
A unique flower I have seen is a hibiscus flower, I think the hibiscus flower is unique because it has many different colors.

Two complete sentences joined by just a comma. Each side can stand alone.

Tap to see the improved version
Read the explanation above first
FIXED
A unique flower I have seen is a hibiscus flower. I think it is unique because it has many different colors.
THE COMMA SPLICE FIX
A comma alone can never join two complete sentences. You have three choices:

1. Use a period and start a new sentence.
2. Add a conjunction: , and / , but / , so
3. Use a semicolon (;) if the ideas are closely related.

Quick test: Cover the comma. Can each side stand alone as a sentence? If yes, you need more than a comma.

YOU MADE THIS MISTAKE 4 TIMES — HERE'S HOW TO CATCH IT
Before you turn in your test, read every sentence out loud. When you hit a comma, pause and ask: "Is there a complete sentence on EACH side of this comma?"

If yes → you have a comma splice. Fix it with a period or add a conjunction (and, but, so).
If no → the comma is fine. Keep going.

COMBINING RULE: KEEP EVERY DETAIL
When the question says "combine," make sure your new sentence includes all the information from both original sentences. Before you move on, compare your answer to the originals — did you leave anything out?
Why does this matter?
When you combine sentences, the goal is to connect ideas without losing any meaning. If the original says "meadows do too," that emphasis on "also" matters. Using "and" to connect the ideas keeps everything intact.
