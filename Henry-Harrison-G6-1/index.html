Develop with Passage Evidence

Q11 — 16 out of 20 — good thesis, body paragraphs need specific facts from Wilma's story

Henry, your essay opens strong — you have a clear thesis about perseverance and what we can learn from Wilma Rudolph. But in the body paragraphs, you make claims without always backing them up with a specific detail from the passage and then explaining what that detail shows. The rubric rewards essays that go beyond naming an idea and actually prove it with evidence.

WHAT A THIN BODY PARAGRAPH SOUNDS LIKE
"Wilma showed perseverance and what we can learn from her story is that if you work for something you will eventually get it."

This states the lesson (perseverance pays off) but never names a specific fact from Wilma's life or explains what that fact shows. The grader reads it and thinks: "What specific thing did Wilma do? How does that prove your point?"

THE DETAIL + EXPLANATION PATTERN
Every body paragraph needs this sequence:

1. Claim: State the lesson or quality Wilma demonstrated.
2. Detail: Name a specific fact from the passage (a real event, a number, a quote).
3. Explanation: Explain what that fact shows about perseverance or why it matters.

The detail is the what. The explanation is the so what. You need both.
SAME IDEA, WITH DETAIL + EXPLANATION
"Wilma showed perseverance by refusing to give up even when her body was against her. [Detail] As a child, she was diagnosed with polio and told she might never walk normally again, yet she did physical therapy for years until she could walk without a brace by age 12. [Explanation] This shows that perseverance is not about one big moment of effort — it is about choosing to keep working day after day, even when progress is slow and the goal feels impossible."
Which body paragraph uses the Detail + Explanation pattern correctly?
A Wilma Rudolph showed that hard work pays off. She worked very hard her whole life and never gave up. This teaches us that we should also never give up on our dreams no matter what.
B Wilma Rudolph showed that perseverance can overcome what seems impossible. After being told she might never walk normally because of polio, she not only learned to walk again but went on to win three gold medals at the 1960 Olympics. This proves that refusing to accept limitations, combined with years of effort, can take a person further than anyone predicted.
A QUICK CHECK BEFORE YOU MOVE ON
After writing each body paragraph, ask yourself two questions:

• Did I name a specific fact from the passage? (a name, a number, an event)
• Did I write a sentence that explains what that fact shows or proves?

If either answer is no, add a sentence before moving to the next paragraph. One detail + one explanation is worth more than three vague claims.

Cite a Specific Fact + Explain What It Shows

The skill that turns a 16/20 essay into an 18/20 essay

Henry, the Wilma Rudolph passage is full of specific facts you can use as evidence. The key is picking one fact per body paragraph and then writing a sentence that explains what that fact shows about perseverance.

FACTS FROM THE PASSAGE YOU COULD CITE
• Diagnosed with polio at age 4 — told she might never walk normally
• Did physical therapy for years, walked without a brace by age 12
• Became a basketball star in high school despite her childhood illness
• Won three gold medals at the 1960 Rome Olympics
• Was the fastest woman in the world after doctors said she might not walk

Each of these facts is a detail. Your job is to pick one, state it, and then explain what it proves.
Which explanation sentence best connects Wilma's fact to the theme of perseverance?
A "This shows that she worked really hard and never gave up, which is inspiring."
B "This proves that perseverance is not just about enduring pain. It is about refusing to let other people's predictions define what you can become."
THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN RESTATING AND EXPLAINING
Restating = saying the same idea again in different words ("she worked hard and that is inspiring").
Explaining = telling the reader what the fact PROVES or what bigger truth it connects to ("perseverance means refusing to let predictions define you").

The test: if your explanation sentence could apply to ANY person who worked hard, it is too vague. A good explanation is specific to THIS fact about THIS person.

Proofread Pass — Catch Slips Before Submitting

Two small errors that cost points: a typo and a cut-off ending

Henry, your essay had two proofreading issues that cost points on the mechanics row of the rubric. These are not ideas problems — they are rushed-ending problems that a slow read-back before submitting would catch.

SLIP #2: CUT-OFF ENDING
"...you will eventua..." — the essay ends mid-word. You ran out of time or accidentally submitted before finishing your thought.

Both of these happened at the very end of your essay, which suggests you were rushing to finish. You had time (1h 10m of 90 minutes) but may not have saved enough for a final read-through.

THE SLOW READ-BACK BEFORE SUBMIT
Before you hit submit, read your last two sentences out loud (in your head). Slowly. Word by word.

Ask yourself:
• Does every sentence end with a period?
• Does every word look complete (not cut off)?
• Did I type the word I meant? ("is" vs "if" are one letter apart on the keyboard)

This 60-second check catches exactly the kind of errors that cost you points on G6.1.
Which version has caught both proofreading errors?

BUILDING THE HABIT
You finished your essay with 20 minutes to spare. That is plenty of time for a read-back. The habit to build: when you finish your last body paragraph, stop writing new ideas and switch to proofreading mode. Read your conclusion and last paragraph slowly, checking for complete words and correct small words (is/if, or/of, the/they). One minute of slow reading saves 2–3 rubric points.
