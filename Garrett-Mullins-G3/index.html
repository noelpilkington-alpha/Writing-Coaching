What Makes a Complete Sentence?

Mini-Lesson 1 — The most important rule in writing

A complete sentence needs three things:

The 3 Parts of a Complete Sentence
Subject Who or what the sentence is about
Verb What the subject does or is
Complete Thought The sentence makes sense on its own — a reader understands the full idea

These are all from your tests. Can you tell which one is a complete sentence?

Which one is a complete sentence?
A "To give to kids in need."
B "Your family has two dogs."

Now try another one from your tests:

Which one makes sense as a complete sentence?
A "Pick up wore dogs poop."
B "Dogs are not able to go to restaurants."
WATCH HOW TO FIX A FRAGMENT
Your test answer was: "To give to kids in need."

"Wait — 'To give to kids in need.' Who is giving? I need to add a subject."

Fixed: "Fans give teddy bears to kids in need."

The fix was simple: add a subject (Fans) and make the verb match (give). Now it's a complete sentence.
THE SENTENCE CHECK
Before you submit any answer, ask yourself:
1. Who or what is this about? (subject)
2. What do they do? (verb)
3. Does it make sense if I say it out loud? (complete thought)

If you can't find the subject, your sentence isn't complete yet.

On your writing tests, almost every question asks you to write a complete sentence. If your answer has a subject, a verb, and a complete thought, you're already earning points.

Which answer would earn more points on a test?
A "ijniyndsa iO"
B "The ise was cuverd with sno."

Here are two strategies for when you know what you want to say but can't spell the word:

Strategy 1: Say It Slow
Step 1 Say the word out loud, slowly: "res — taw — ronts"
Step 2 Write down the sounds you hear: "restrints"
Step 3 Keep going! A reader can tell "restrints" means "restaurants"
Strategy 2: Swap for a Word You Know
Hard "restaurants" → swap it for: "places to eat"
Hard "people" → swap it for: "kids" or "they"
Hard "because" → swap it for: "so"
Rule If you can't spell it, use a simpler word that means the same thing

Read the Question, Answer the Question

Mini-Lesson 3 — Make sure your answer matches what was asked

On your tests, several questions were marked wrong not because you didn't know the answer, but because your answer didn't match what the question asked.

Look at this example from your G3.1 test:

THE QUESTION ASKED
"How would YOU FEEL if a dog walked up to you? Write one sentence to explain."
YOUR ANSWER
"Your family has two dogs."

The question asked about your feelings — but your answer talks about someone's family. It doesn't answer what was asked.

Which answer actually matches the question "How would you feel if a dog walked up to you?"
A "Your family has two dogs."
B "I would feel happy because dogs are friendly and fun to pet."

Here's another one from your G3.2 test:

THE QUESTION ASKED
"Would you like to attend a Teddy Bear Toss game? Write one complete sentence."
YOUR ANSWER
"yes"

"Yes" answers the question, but it's not a complete sentence and it doesn't explain why. The question asked for a sentence.

THE 3-STEP STRATEGY
1. Find the key words in the question (feel, explain, write one sentence)
2. Start your answer by restating the question: "I would feel [feeling] because [reason]."
3. Check: Does my answer match what they asked?
See how this works with the Teddy Bear question
Question: "Would you like to attend a Teddy Bear Toss game?"
Key words: would you like, attend
Start by restating: "I would like to attend a Teddy Bear Toss game because..."
Full answer: "I would like to attend a Teddy Bear Toss game because it sounds fun to throw teddy bears on the ice."

On your tests, 3 questions were marked wrong because your answer didn't match the question. This one fix alone could earn you more points next time.

How to Use AlphaWrite on Your Own

Mini-Lesson 4 — You don't need a coaching call for every activity

Right now, you need a coach on the phone to get through most AlphaWrite activities. That's okay for now — but these four steps will help you work through activities on your own.

1 Read the tutorial first — slowly
Before you start answering questions, read the lesson at the top of the activity. Read it slowly. If a word is hard, skip it and keep going. You're looking for two things: the rule (what you're supposed to do) and the example (what a correct answer looks like). Focus on the example — it shows you the pattern to follow.
2 Say the rule out loud
After reading, say out loud: "The rule is [what I just learned]. The example shows [how it works]." Saying it out loud helps you remember it. If you can say it, you understand it.
3 When you get an answer wrong, don't just click "try again"
Stop and look at the feedback:
  • Find the correct answer — read it slowly, out loud if it helps
  • Put your answer and the correct answer side by side in your head
  • Ask yourself: "What's different?" Look for what they added or changed
4 Use the rule on the next question
Before answering the next question, think: "Does my answer follow the rule I just learned?" This is how you stop making the same mistake twice.
You got an appositive question wrong. The feedback says your answer was "is a good game but it" instead of a noun phrase like "a popular video game." What should you do next?
A Click "try again" right away and guess a different answer.
B Look at the correct answer ("a popular video game") and ask: "What's different? The correct one is a short description — not a whole sentence."
EXAMPLE: LEARNING FROM FEEDBACK
You wrote "is a good game but it" as an appositive.
The correct answer was "a popular video game."

"Hmm, my answer has a verb ('is') and a conjunction ('but'). The correct answer is just a short description — 'a popular video game.' An appositive is like a nickname or description, not a new sentence."

Next question: Now you know — appositives are short descriptions like "a third grader" or "a big brown dog," not full sentences.
THE GOAL
The goal isn't to get every question right on the first try. The goal is to learn from the feedback so you get it right the next time — without needing someone on the phone to explain it.

Proofread Before You Submit

Mini-Lesson 5 — The CUPS Check

Before you click Submit on any answer — on a test or in AlphaWrite — run the CUPS Check. It takes about 10 seconds.

C
Capitalization
Does it start with a capital letter? Are names capitalized?
U
Understanding
Did I answer what the question asked?
P
Punctuation
Does it end with a period, question mark, or exclamation point?
S
Sense
Say it out loud slowly — does it make sense?

Spelling tip: If a word doesn't look right, use your Say It Slow or Swap strategies from Card 3. Don't let one hard word stop you from submitting.

Here are real examples from your tests where the CUPS Check would have helped:

B "Dogs are not able to go to restaurants."

"The icewas covered" → "The ice was covered" (missing space)
"A airport" → "An airport"
"I like to do with my dog is sleep." → "My favorite thing to do with my dog is sleep."
CUPS CHECK IN ACTION

C — "Does it start with a capital? No! Change 'the' to 'The.' Wait, actually 'the' isn't even the right word..."

Every single time before you click Submit. On tests AND on AlphaWrite. It takes 10 seconds and can save you from losing points on mistakes you already know how to fix.

G3.2 — PARAGRAPH ABOUT THE CAMEL LIBRARY

You haven't been taught how to write a paragraph yet — and that's okay. This card will teach you.

Which one is a real paragraph?
A "Good for you."
B "Soil is very important to Earth. First, soil helps plants grow by giving them water and food. Without soil, we would not have trees, flowers, or food to eat. Second, soil is home to many animals like worms and bugs. These small animals help break down old leaves and make the soil richer. Soil is one of the most important things on our planet."

Every paragraph has 4 parts:

The 4 Parts of a Paragraph
Central Idea Tells the reader what your paragraph is about
Detail + Why Give a fact from the article and explain why it matters
Detail + Why Give another fact from the article and explain why it matters
Closing Wrap it up — restate the main idea in different words
Tap to see how to build the "Digging on Dirt" paragraph step by step
Review the 4 parts above first
BUILDING A PARAGRAPH — STEP BY STEP
"Okay, the question says: explain why soil is important. My central idea should say that soil is important."

[Central Idea] "Soil is very important to Earth."

"Now I need a detail from the article. The article said soil helps plants grow. Let me explain why that matters."

[Detail 1 + Why] "First, soil helps plants grow by giving them water and food. Without soil, we would not have trees, flowers, or food to eat."

"Good. I need one more detail. The article said soil is home to animals."

[Detail 2 + Why] "Second, soil is home to many animals like worms and bugs. These small animals help break down old leaves and make the soil even richer."

"Now I need a closing that wraps it up."

[Closing] "Soil is one of the most important things on our planet."
PLANNING TEMPLATE
Before you write, plan your paragraph with this template:

Central Idea: ________________________________
Detail 1: ________________________________
Why it matters: ________________________________
Detail 2: ________________________________
Why it matters: ________________________________
Closing: ________________________________

Fill in the blanks first, then write the full paragraph. This is the same structure you'll use on every single writing test paragraph.
DON'T LET SPELLING STOP YOU
Writing a paragraph means writing many sentences. You might hit words you can't spell. That's okay — use your strategies from Card 3:
1. Say it slow and write the sounds you hear
2. Swap for a simpler word
3. Keep writing — a paragraph with spelling mistakes is worth way more than "Good for you."

Remember: Question 11 is worth 20 out of 45 points. Even a paragraph with some misspelled words can earn most of those points. A one-sentence answer cannot.
