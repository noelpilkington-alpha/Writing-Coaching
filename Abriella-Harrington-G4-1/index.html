Polish #1: What a True Appositive Is

An appositive renames the noun — with a noun phrase

Abriella, on Q3 you were asked to add an appositive that gives more information about Peter Tabichi. You correctly set a phrase off with commas — that part shows you understand the move. The one detail to tighten: an appositive has to rename the noun with a noun phrase, not just describe it.

YOU WROTE (G4.1 Q3)
"Peter Tabichi, from Kenya, Africa, teaches high school science."
"From Kenya, Africa" is a describing phrase that tells where he is from. An appositive instead renames Peter Tabichi with a noun phrase.
RENAME WITH A NOUN PHRASE
"Peter Tabichi, a dedicated science instructor, teaches high school science."
Now the phrase between the commas is a noun phrase ("a dedicated science instructor") that renames Peter Tabichi — that is a true appositive.
THE RULE
An appositive is a noun phrase placed right next to a noun to rename it. Ask yourself: "Could this phrase stand in for the name?" A dedicated science instructor could rename Peter Tabichi — so it is an appositive. From Kenya, Africa tells where he is from but cannot rename him — so it is a describing phrase, not an appositive.
Which sentence uses a true appositive?
A Peter Tabichi, from Kenya, Africa, teaches high school science.
B Peter Tabichi, a science award winner, teaches high school science.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
You already nailed the comma setup — the only step left is making the phrase a noun phrase that renames. Get that, and the appositive questions become a guaranteed point.

Polish #2: Give Each Idea a Clean Boundary

Join smoothly with a connecting word, or use a clean period

Abriella, on Q5 you combined two sentences — a smart instinct. The one thing to watch: when two complete ideas land next to each other, each idea needs a clean boundary. Either join them smoothly with a connecting word (because, but, so, and), or end one with a period and start the next. On Q5 the two ideas ran together with only a comma between them.

YOU WROTE (G4.1 Q5)
"His dedication has transformed the school because of his efforts, enrollment has doubled, and more students are now attending college."
Two complete ideas join with only a comma — "his dedication has transformed the school" and "enrollment has doubled" need a cleaner connection.
JOIN SMOOTHLY WITH "SO"
"His dedication has transformed the school, so enrollment has doubled and more students are now attending college."
The connecting word "so" gives each idea a clean boundary and shows how they relate — cause and effect.
THE RULE
When you put two complete ideas together, give each one a clean boundary. Two clean ways:
1. Join them with a connecting word: because, but, so, and.
2. End the first idea with a period and start the next as its own sentence.
Both keep your ideas exactly — they just make the boundary clear.
Which version gives each idea a clean boundary?
A His dedication has transformed the school because of his efforts, enrollment has doubled, and more students are now attending college.
B His dedication has transformed the school, so enrollment has doubled and more students are now attending college.
ONE QUICK FORMAT NOTE
On Q7 the prompt asked for one sentence and you wrote two strong ones. Your content was great — just a light reminder to match the format the prompt asks for. When it says one sentence, give one complete sentence with your reason inside it.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Your instinct to combine ideas is exactly right — that is advanced writing. Adding a clean boundary every time turns a near-miss into full credit, with no change to your ideas.
