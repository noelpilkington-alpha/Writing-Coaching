Run-On Sentence — Q6

Too many ideas crammed into one sentence

Your answer tried to fit everything into a single sentence by chaining clauses with so, but, and because:

YOU WROTE
Once, I had to admit to my mother that I had stolen my phone at night, so after I told her, she thanked me for telling the truth, but grounded me because what I did was still wrong.

Three conjunctions in one sentence = run-on. The reader gets lost. The fix is simple: split it into two sentences.

Tap to see the fixed version
Read the explanation above first
FIXED
Once, I had to admit to my mother that I had stolen my phone at night. After I told her, she thanked me for telling the truth but still grounded me because what I did was wrong.
RULE: KEEP SENTENCES CLEAR
Aim for one or two ideas per sentence. If you use more than two conjunctions (so, but, because, and), it's time to split. A period is your best friend.

THAN has an A — just like comparAtive, grAde, and lArger. If you're comparing two things, use thAn. If you're talking about time ("first this, then that"), use thEn.

Sentence Clarity — Q9

Keep your sentence structure parallel

Your sentence starts with one structure and then shifts to another:

YOU WROTE
This story teaches honesty is important and to always be honest upfront instead of trying to avoid the situation.

The problem: "teaches honesty is important" and "to always be honest" don't match. The first is a that-clause; the second is an infinitive. When you list two things after "teaches," they should follow the same pattern.

Tap to see the fixed version
Read the explanation above first
FIXED
This story teaches that honesty is important and that being upfront is always better than trying to avoid the situation.
RULE: KEEP ITEMS IN A LIST PARALLEL
If one item starts with "that," the next should too.
Mismatched: teaches honesty is important and to always be honest
Parallel: teaches that honesty is important and that being upfront is better

You only wrote 3 sentences. A strong G5 paragraph needs 6–8 sentences. The ideas are there, but they need to be expanded with specific details and explanations of why they matter.

BETTER VERSION (6 SENTENCES)
New research shows that horses can communicate with humans in surprising ways. For example, studies found that horses can read human facial expressions and tell the difference between happy and angry faces. Don't just mention the evidence — give it its own sentence, then explain why it matters to your main point. One detail crammed into a list isn't as strong as one detail given room to breathe.

The G5 Paragraph Plan

TS → D1 → W → D2 → W → D3 → W → CS

Follow this map every time you write a paragraph on the test. Each letter is one sentence:

Paragraph Map — "Say What, Horse?"
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail from the passage  •  W = Why it matters  •  CS = Closing Sentence
TS New research shows that horses can communicate with humans in surprising ways.
D1 Horses can read human facial expressions and tell the difference between happy and angry faces.
W This shows they pay close attention to how we feel.
D2 Scientists trained horses to use symbols to say whether they wanted a blanket or not.
W This shows that horses can learn to share what they need.
D3 Horses have been living alongside humans for thousands of years.
W That long history may explain why they are so good at understanding us.
CS These findings reveal that horses are much better communicators than most people realize.
REMEMBER THE PATTERN
Every detail (D) gets a "Why it matters" (W) right after it. That's what turns a list of facts into an explanation. After each detail, ask yourself: "So what? Why does this matter to my main point?" That answer is your W sentence. Then wrap it all up with a strong closing sentence.
