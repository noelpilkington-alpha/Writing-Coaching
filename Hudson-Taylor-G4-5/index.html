Comma Splices: Two Sentences Need More

Q3 — 1 out of 2

A comma splice happens when you connect two complete sentences with only a comma. A comma alone is not strong enough to hold two complete thoughts together.

Q3 — YOU WROTE
"Sharyl is a jingle-dress dancer, she spends hours rolling metal cones by hand."

The problem: Both sides of that comma are complete sentences. "Sharyl is a jingle-dress dancer" can stand alone. "She spends hours rolling metal cones by hand" can also stand alone. You cannot just put a comma between them.

Three ways to fix a comma splice:

Comma only → Period + new sentence
Comma only → Comma + conjunction (and, so, but)
Comma only → Make one part depend on the other
Tap to see corrected versions
Three different ways to fix it
FIX 1: PERIOD
"Sharyl is a jingle-dress dancer. She spends hours rolling metal cones by hand."
FIX 2: CONJUNCTION
"Sharyl is a jingle-dress dancer, and she spends hours rolling metal cones by hand."
FIX 3: DEPENDENT CLAUSE
"Because Sharyl is a jingle-dress dancer, she spends hours rolling metal cones by hand."
THE COMMA SPLICE TEST
When you use a comma between two parts of a sentence, cover up everything after the comma. Can the first part stand alone as a sentence? Now cover the first part. Can the second part stand alone? If both can stand alone, you need more than just a comma. Add a conjunction (and, but, so) or use a period.
Try it yourself
"The powwow brings families together, everyone celebrates their heritage."

Test it: "The powwow brings families together." Complete? Yes.
"Everyone celebrates their heritage." Complete? Yes.
Both complete = comma splice.

Fixed: "The powwow brings families together, and everyone celebrates their heritage."

Spelling + Tense Errors

Q1 — 1.5 out of 2 | Q5 — 1 out of 2 | Q6 — 1 out of 3

Three questions lost points because of spelling mistakes or a tense error. These are quick fixes once you know what to look for.

Spelling errors:

"outifts" (Q1) → "outfits"
"woman" (Q1) → "women" (plural)
"celibreate" (Q6) → "celebrate"

Tense error:

Q5 — YOU WROTE
"Did shawl dances began when shawls replaced the blankets young women once wore?"

The rule: When a question starts with "Did," the main verb must be in its base form (no past tense). "Did" already carries the past tense for the whole sentence.

"Did... began" → "Did... begin"
THE "DID" RULE
Whenever your sentence starts with "Did," the next verb stays in base form:
Did she go (not "Did she went")
Did it begin (not "Did it began")
Did they run (not "Did they ran")

Think of it this way: "Did" is already doing the past-tense job. The main verb just stays normal.
Spelling tip: sound it out slowly
For words like "outfits" and "celebrate," try sounding them out one syllable at a time:

out-fits (not "out-ifts") — the "f" comes before the "i"
cel-e-brate (not "cel-i-breate") — three syllables, "e" in the middle

If a word looks funny, sound it out syllable by syllable and check if each chunk makes sense.

Answer What the Question Asks

Q6 — 1 out of 3

On Q6, you wrote something related to the passage but did not answer the specific thing the question was asking for.

Q6 asked: What lesson do readers learn from this passage?

Q6 — YOU WROTE
"People celibreate differently around the world."

Two problems:

  • Too generic. "People celebrate differently" could be about any passage. The question asks what lesson this specific passage teaches readers.
  • Does not name the lesson. A "lesson" means what the reader takes away. What do we learn about persistence, tradition, or cultural pride from the jingle dress story?

STRONGER ANSWER
"Readers learn that cultural traditions like the jingle dress can be kept alive when people like Sharyl put in the time and effort to make them by hand, passing the craft to the next generation."

Notice: the strong answer names a specific lesson (traditions stay alive through effort), connects it to a specific detail from the passage (Sharyl making cones by hand), and explains why it matters (passing the craft to the next generation).

THE "LESSON" FORMULA
When a question asks "What lesson do readers learn?" your answer should follow this pattern:

Readers learn that [specific idea] because [evidence from the passage].

If your answer could work for any passage, it is too generic. Make sure your answer mentions something only found in this passage.

One important lesson from "Celebrating Native Stories" is that cultural traditions survive when people dedicate themselves to keeping them alive.
