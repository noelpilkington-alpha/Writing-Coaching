Combine with an Appositive

Your #1 fix — fold two ideas into one sentence

Audrey, this is the single fastest way to pick up points. On Question 1, the task asked you to combine the two ideas into one sentence using an appositive. Here is what you wrote:

YOU WROTE (Q1 — the task was to combine into ONE sentence)
"My family ownes two dogs. They are named Lucy and Rocket."

Both ideas are good — but they are still two separate sentences. The task asked you to fold them into one sentence using an appositive: a little group of words that renames the noun and is set off with commas. Naming the dogs (Lucy and Rocket) renames "two dogs," so it can slide right into the first sentence.

COMBINED INTO ONE SENTENCE WITH AN APPOSITIVE
"My family owns two dogs, Lucy and Rocket."

See how the second idea folds in? "Lucy and Rocket" renames "two dogs," so a comma joins them into one smooth sentence — no second sentence needed.

The task asks: combine the two ideas into ONE sentence using an appositive. Which answer does that?
A My family owns two dogs. They are named Lucy and Rocket.
B My family owns two dogs, Lucy and Rocket.
THE RULE
An appositive is a group of words that renames a noun right next to it, and it is set off with commas.

✗ "I have a pet. It is a cat named Mochi." (two sentences)
✓ "I have a pet, a cat named Mochi." (one sentence with an appositive)

To combine: find the noun in the first sentence, then drop in the renaming words right after it, with a comma.

One Complete Sentence, with a Reason

Answer in a full sentence that adds new information

Audrey, on Questions 6–10 the goal is always one complete sentence — a who or what, plus an action — that answers the question and gives a reason that adds something new. A couple of your answers were close but slipped on this. Here is Question 9:

YOU WROTE (Q9)
"Pet a cute fluffy cat."

This is a fragment — it has an action ("pet") but no who or what is doing it, so it is not a complete sentence. Adding the subject "I" makes it whole.

ONE COMPLETE SENTENCE WITH A REASON
"I would pet a cute, fluffy cat, because its soft fur is calming to touch."

On Question 8, your reason went in a circle: "The dog park because it is for dogs." That repeats the question instead of giving a new reason. A strong reason adds new information, like: "The dog park, because my dog can run off the leash and make friends there."

Which option is one complete sentence that answers the question?
A Pet a cute fluffy cat.
B I would pet a cute, fluffy cat, because its soft fur is calming.
THE HABIT TO BUILD
Before you finish a Question 6–10 answer, check two things:

1. Is there a who or what (the subject) plus an action? If not, add one — usually "I."
2. Does my reason add new information, or does it just repeat the question? Make it tell something the reader did not already know.

CLEARER WORDING (Q10)

THE SLOW READ-BACK
When you finish an answer, read it back slowly in your head, one word at a time:

• owns — one short word, no extra "e" (owns, not ownes)
• bitten — two t's in the middle (bit-ten)
• Ask: "Does each it / I / they point to the right thing?" If you mean the pet, say it, not I.

If a word or a pronoun looks off when you read it back, that is your cue to fix it.

SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Your ideas already earn points — clean spelling and the right pronoun just make sure the reader sees exactly what you mean. A quick read-back at the end of each answer is one of the easiest ways to add points on your next test.
