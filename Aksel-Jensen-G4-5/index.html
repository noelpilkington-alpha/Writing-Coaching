THE FIX — 4 CLEAN WAYS TO COMBINE TWO SENTENCES
Take G4.4 Q1: "Calm music helps people relax. It can also help people feel less stressed." Pick one:

1. Add a FANBOYS word (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so):
"Calm music helps people relax, and it can also help people feel less stressed."

2. Use a period and start a new sentence:
"Calm music helps people relax; it can also help people feel less stressed."

3. Subordinate one clause (because, since, while, although):
"Because calm music helps people relax, it can also help people feel less stressed."

4. Turn it into one tight sentence with a list or appositive:
"Calm music — a natural stress reliever — helps people relax and feel less stressed."
Pick the version that correctly combines "Calm music helps people relax." and "It can also help people feel less stressed."
A Calm music helps people relax, it can also help people feel less stressed.
B Calm music helps people relax, and it can also help people feel less stressed.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
You've hit this on 4 out of 5 tests. Every Q1 comma splice is a 0.5-point partial. That adds up. The fix: on Q1, after you write your combined sentence, find the word in the middle that holds it together. If it's just a comma, add a FANBOYS word or restructure with a period. What an Appositive IS (and What It Isn't)

You reach for relative clauses. Here's how to spot the difference.

Aksel, on Q3–Q4 when the prompt asks you to use an appositive, you often use a relative clause or a compound sentence instead. Both sound good, both are grammatically correct — but they are not appositives. The grading feedback docks partial credit because the task wasn't met.

What Is an Appositive?
DEF An appositive is a noun phrase that renames another noun right next to it, usually set off by commas.
My dog, a golden retriever, loves the beach.
"A golden retriever" renames "my dog." That's the whole move.
NOT Not an appositive: a "which..." or "who..." clause. That's a relative clause.
My dog, who loves the beach, is a golden retriever. ← relative clause, NOT appositive.
NOT Not an appositive: two sentences joined with "and." That's a compound sentence.
My dog is a golden retriever, and she loves the beach. ← compound, NOT appositive.
YOUR G4.2 Q4 (Mar 3)
"…bluebonnet festivals, which have fried food..."
("Which have fried food" is a relative clause, not an appositive. An appositive would be: "bluebonnet festivals, spring celebrations filled with fried food and music," — a noun phrase that renames the festival.)
YOUR G4.3 Q3 (Mar 4)
"He asked his mom for some lettuce, and she was shocked because he never eats vegetables."
(This is a compound sentence with "and." An appositive version would rename one of the nouns: "He asked his mom, a gardener who grew her own lettuce, for some.")
YOUR G4.4 Q3 (Mar 5)
"Music can help you learn by keeping your attention."
(No appositive at all here. An appositive version: "Music, a natural focus booster, can help you learn by keeping your attention.")
The prompt says: "Combine these sentences using an appositive: The elephant is the largest land animal. The elephant lives in Africa." Which answer is an appositive?
A The elephant, which is the largest land animal, lives in Africa.
B The elephant, the largest land animal, lives in Africa.
THE SIMPLE TEST FOR AN APPOSITIVE
If you can remove the comma-chunk entirely and the sentence still makes sense with no verb missing — it's an appositive.

✓ "The elephant lives in Africa." (removed "the largest land animal" — still works) → appositive
✗ "The elephant lives in Africa." (from "which IS the largest land animal") → you lost the verb "is" — not an appositive

Quick rule: If it has a verb, it's a clause. No verb = appositive.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
You hit this on 3 of 5 tests. Each appositive miss is 0.5 points. Memorize the test: no verb in the comma-chunk = appositive. When a prompt says "use an appositive," write a noun phrase that renames the noun, set it off with commas, and check that the chunk has no verb. Done.
