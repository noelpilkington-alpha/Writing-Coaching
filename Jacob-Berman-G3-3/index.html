Your 6-Step Writing Process

Plan the paragraph before you write it

Jacob, the best writers don't just start typing. They follow a process. Here are the six steps to use on every writing test — the one in purple is the step that fixes your paragraph.

Read the question carefully so you know exactly what it is asking.
Read the article and notice the specific facts and details you could use.
Plan a quick paragraph outline in your head before you write: Topic Sentence → Detail 1 → Detail 2 → Closing. Make each line a different idea. Then turn the outline into your paragraph.
Draft the paragraph by turning each outline line into a full sentence.
Add details from the article so each idea is backed up by something specific.
Reread and fix spelling, names, and any sentence that repeats an idea you already wrote.
WHY STEP 3 MATTERS MOST FOR YOU
In Question 11 you wrote almost the same sentence four times. A quick outline with four different lines — Topic, Detail 1, Detail 2, Closing — would have stopped that before it started. When each outline line is a new idea, your paragraph is automatically full of new ideas.

Build a Paragraph with DIFFERENT Details

The one card that unlocks most of your points

Jacob, this is the heart of your review. Here is your actual Question 11 paragraph. Look at how many times the same idea comes back:

YOU WROTE (G3.3 Q11, paragraph scored 9 out of 20)
"Satoshi chilhood influenced the creation of pokemon. Satoshi chilhood influenced her to create something awsome that can be exciting. Her chilhood influenced her to create these cards that can be worth a lot of money. So now you have learned that Satoshi chilhood influenced her to create pokemon."

Every sentence says the same thing: "Satoshi's childhood influenced Pokemon." That is one idea repeated four times. A strong paragraph gives the reader different ideas that each add something new.

What a strong paragraph looks like
TOPIC State your main idea once: Satoshi's childhood inspired Pokemon.
DETAIL 1 A specific fact from the article (something he did as a kid).
DETAIL 2 A different fact — a new idea, not a repeat of detail 1.
CLOSING Wrap up the main idea in a fresh way.
You already wrote your topic sentence. Which sentence should come NEXT?
A His childhood influenced him to create Pokemon for everyone to enjoy.
B As a boy, Satoshi loved collecting bugs and insects near his home.
Tap to see a model paragraph

MODEL PARAGRAPH (using real article details)

Satoshi Tajiri's childhood inspired him to create Pokemon. As a boy, he loved collecting bugs and insects near his home. Catching and trading those creatures is exactly what players do in the Pokemon games. His curiosity about nature became the idea of exploring the world to find new creatures. Because of the things he loved as a child, Satoshi turned his hobby into a game enjoyed all over the world.

SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Your paragraph is worth 20 of the 45 points on the test. Replacing three repeated sentences with three different ideas is the single biggest jump you can make. Each new idea is points you are leaving on the table right now.

Use Details from the Article

Specific facts beat general statements every time

Jacob, here is the other thing that held your paragraph back: it had zero details from the article. The article told a real story about Satoshi Tajiri — how he loved collecting bugs and insects as a child, and how that inspired the idea of catching creatures in Pokemon. Your paragraph never mentioned any of it.

When you only write general statements like "something awsome that can be exciting," the reader cannot tell that you understood the article. One concrete detail proves you read it and makes the whole paragraph stronger.

ONE DETAIL CHANGES EVERYTHING
Vague: "His childhood influenced him to make something exciting."

With a detail: "As a child he collected bugs and insects, which is why players catch creatures in Pokemon."
Which sentence shows you read the article?
A Satoshi's childhood was important and helped him make a great game.
B Satoshi collected bugs as a child, which inspired catching creatures in Pokemon.
HOW TO FIND A DETAIL
After you read the article, ask yourself: "What is one specific thing the article told me?" A name, an action, a place, a fact. Then use it. In this article the strongest detail was that Satoshi loved collecting bugs and insects — that is the seed of the whole game.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Graders give the most points to paragraphs that use real details from the passage. A paragraph with one specific fact will almost always score higher than one with only general statements — even if both are the same length.

One Clear, Complete Sentence

Answer the question and give a reason — cleanly

Jacob, your short answers were strong overall, but Question 8 lost a point. The question asked whether "Sally" is a good name for the seal. Questions 6–10 ask for one clear sentence that answers the question and includes a reason or example. Here is what you wrote:

YOU WROTE (G3.3 Q8, scored 2 out of 3)
"I don't think Sally is a great name because I have different opinons for example I would call her lucky."

The idea is fine, but two thoughts run together without a stop, and "I have different opinons" is vague (and misspelled). A clean, complete sentence makes your point clear in one shot.

Which answer is one clear, complete sentence?
A I don't think Sally is a great name because I have a different opinion I would call her Lucky.
B I don't think Sally is a great name because I would call her Lucky after she survived being trapped.
YOUR ANSWER, CLEANED UP
"I don't think Sally is a great name because I would call her Lucky after she survived being trapped."
THE RULE FOR QUESTIONS 6–10
Write one complete sentence that does two things: it answers the question and it gives a reason or example. The word "because" is a great way to connect your answer to your reason in a single sentence. Read it back once to make sure it is one clean thought, not two squished together.
