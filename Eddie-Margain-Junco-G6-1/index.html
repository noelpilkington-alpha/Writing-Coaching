The simplest way to turn a 16/20 essay into an 18/20 essay

Graders notice this immediately — the spelling and grammar row of the rubric is worth 3 points, and getting the subject's name right is the cheapest win on the whole rubric.

🔍
The good news: You got the spelling correct on your very first mention ("Wilma Rudolph, a girl born in 1940..."). So you know how to spell it. The issue is not knowledge — it is the proofread pass you did not run.
Which is the best use of the last few minutes of a G6 writing test?
A Once you finish the last sentence, submit right away so the essay feels complete and fresh.
B Reread the full essay from the top. Check every proper noun. Fix any spelling, capitalization, or punctuation slips before you submit.
THE 2-MINUTE PROOFREAD PASS
G6 tests give you 90 minutes. There is almost always time for a proofread before you submit. Here is the checklist:

• Subject's name: scan every paragraph — does it match the spelling in the passage?
• Homophones and letter swaps: "womon" → "woman", "Wimlma" → "Wilma"
• Missing letters: "Wilm" → "Wilma", "Wlima" → "Wilma"
• Punctuation: every sentence ends with a period, question mark, or exclamation point
• Capitalization: proper nouns and sentence starts

Two minutes of rereading is worth up to 3 rubric points. That is the highest-value 2 minutes on the whole test.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Your essay scored 16/20. If the proofread pass had caught the 5 Wilma misspellings plus "womon", the mechanics row of the rubric (worth 3 points) moves up — worth about +1 to +2 points on essay, or +2 to +4 percentage points on the test. That alone gets you from 86.7% very close to 90%.

Your #2 Lift: Evidence Depth

One extra "this shows..." sentence per body paragraph

Eddie, your G6.1 essay already does something a lot of G6 writers don't: you name real facts from the passage in every body paragraph. The issue is not whether you use evidence — it is how much you do with each piece of evidence once you mention it.

The Q11 Essay Rubric — Where the 4 Points Went
5 pts Five-paragraph structure — you earned strong marks here
5 pts Evidence + explanation of what it shows — this is where a point or two is hiding
4 pts Organization and transitions — you are strong here
3 pts Varied sentence types and formal language — solid
3 pts Spelling, punctuation, grammar — the proofread card covered this

FROM YOUR ESSAY (body paragraph 4)
"In 1960, Wilma competed in the Olympic Games located in Rome. She had won three gold medals in track and field, where she had been training for a long time. This broke history because she was the first American to win so many medals in one Olympics."

NEXT-LEVEL VERSION (adds one analysis sentence)
"In 1960, Wilma competed in the Olympic Games in Rome. She won three gold medals in track and field, and she was the first American woman to win three gold medals in a single Olympics. This shows that her perseverance wasn't just about getting back on her feet — it was about turning years of therapy and training into a world-record performance no one had ever done before."
The bold sentence is the "this shows..." follow-up. It connects the fact back to the thesis (perseverance) and explains why the fact matters. That is the single sentence the rubric rewards.
Which follow-up sentence is a real "this shows..." analysis?
A This shows that Wilma won three gold medals and she was successful.
B This shows that the same determination she used to learn to walk again was what made her Olympic record possible — perseverance carried her from a metal leg brace to a world-record performance.
THE "SO WHAT?" TEST
After every evidence sentence in your essay, ask yourself: "So what? Why does this matter for my thesis?"

Your answer becomes the next sentence. Use one of these openers:
• "This shows that..."
• "This proves..."
• "In other words..."
• "This is significant because..."

Rule of thumb: Evidence = 1 rubric point. Explanation of that evidence = 1 more rubric point. Skip the explanation and you leave half the evidence row unclaimed.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
You have 3 body paragraphs per essay. Adding one "this shows..." sentence per body paragraph is three extra sentences total — maybe three minutes of writing. That is worth +1 to +2 points on essay and another 2–4 percentage points on the test. Combined with the proofread pass from the last card, that is your jump from 86.7% to 90%+.

THE CONCLUSION PARAGRAPH YOU COULD ADD
"Wilma Rudolph's story is proof that perseverance can turn the hardest starts into the greatest finishes. From a metal leg brace as a child to three Olympic gold medals in Rome, she refused to let illness decide what her life would be. Her example teaches us that effort, family support, and the refusal to quit can reshape what looks impossible into what actually happens — and that is a lesson worth carrying into our own challenges."
Three moves: (1) restate the thesis about perseverance in new words, (2) briefly tie back to the main evidence of the essay, (3) leave the reader with the larger takeaway.
THE G6 CONCLUSION CHECKLIST
Every G6 conclusion paragraph should do three things:
1. Restate the thesis — in new words, not the same words you used in the introduction.
2. Tie back to your main evidence — one quick sweep of the points you made.
3. Final takeaway — one sentence that leaves the reader with the larger lesson.

A conclusion is not a new idea or a new piece of evidence. It is the closing frame that makes the whole essay feel finished.
