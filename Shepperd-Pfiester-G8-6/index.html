Appositives — The WHOLE Noun Phrase

You missed 4 appositive questions across 6 tests — all the same mistake

Shepperd, here is the pattern. When a question asks "which is the appositive in this sentence?", you pick one piece of the noun phrase — but an appositive is the whole noun phrase between the commas. Let us walk through your actual picks.

⚑ The appositive rule: An appositive is a noun phrase that renames the noun right before it, set off by commas. When the phrase has extra describing words, the appositive is the ENTIRE phrase — not just the head word.
From G8.1 Q4
Identify the appositive: "Workshops, hands-on courses led by local experts, taught skills to residents."
Your pick: local experts
Correct: hands-on courses led by local experts
Why: "Local experts" is a phrase inside the appositive, not the whole thing. The appositive is everything between the two commas — the full phrase that renames "workshops." What ARE the workshops? Hands-on courses led by local experts. That is the appositive.
From G8.2 Q4
Identify the appositive in the sentence about market booths at the plaza.
Your pick: small wooden booths
Correct: small wooden booths selling postcards and sketches of the towers
Why: Same issue. You grabbed the first chunk of the noun phrase. The full noun phrase between the commas, including the describing words ("selling postcards and sketches of the towers"), is the appositive.
From G8.3 Q4
Which sentence contains an appositive?
Your pick: (a sentence with a descriptive clause but no comma-set-off noun phrase)
Correct: the sentence with a noun phrase between two commas, renaming the noun before it
Why: The giveaway is two commas around a noun phrase that renames the subject. A descriptive clause with "that" or "which" is NOT an appositive — appositives do not use those words.
3-STEP APPOSITIVE TEST
1. Find the two commas. The appositive sits BETWEEN them.

2. Select the entire phrase between those commas. Not just the first word. All of it.

3. Check: does it rename the noun right before the first comma? If yes, that is your appositive. If it starts with "that" or "which," it is a clause, not an appositive.
Identify the appositive in: "The festival, a week-long celebration of local music and food trucks, draws thousands every year."
A a celebration
B of local music and food trucks
C a week-long celebration of local music and food trucks
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q4 on every G8 test is an appositive question. You've missed 4 of them. Those are 4 points directly in your reach with a single rule: the WHOLE noun phrase between the commas is the appositive.

Logical Connectors — Match the Relationship

3 misses across Q6–Q10 — each one picked a connector that did not fit the meaning

Shepperd, three multiple-choice questions across your 6 tests asked you to pick the connector (words like and, so, when, unless, although) that best joined two clauses. Each time, you picked a connector that was grammatically legal but did not match the relationship the sentences were actually describing.

From G8.4 Q10
Fill the blank: "Astronauts follow a strict daily schedule, ______ their evenings on the ISS are calmer."
Your pick: and
Correct: so
Why: "And" would mean the two ideas are just sitting side-by-side. But there is a cause-and-effect link: the schedule CAUSES the calmer evenings. That is CAUSE → EFFECT, which calls for so.
From G8.5 Q6
Fill the blank: "______ the technology fails, difficult questions arise about safety."
Your pick: Unless
Correct: When (or If)
Why: "Unless" means "except if" — it flips the logic. The sentence is saying: AT THE MOMENT the tech fails, safety questions show up. That is a TIME / CONDITION relationship — "when" or "if."
From G8.6 Q6
Fill the blank: "______ a memory is consolidated by the hippocampus, it remains in short-term storage."
Your pick: Although
Correct: Until (or Before)
Why: "Although" would signal contrast, but there is no contrast here — the sentence says the memory stays in short-term UNTIL the next step happens. That is a TEMPORAL SEQUENCE (one thing happens, then another), which calls for "until" or "before."

Fill the blank: "______ a rocket reaches escape velocity, it can leave Earth's gravity." What is the relationship?
A Although a rocket reaches escape velocity, it can leave Earth's gravity.
B When a rocket reaches escape velocity, it can leave Earth's gravity.
C Unless a rocket reaches escape velocity, it can leave Earth's gravity.
YOUR 2-STEP RULE
Step 1 — Name the relationship: addition, cause → effect, effect → response, contrast, time/sequence, condition.

Step 2 — Pick the connector that matches from the table above.

All 3 of your misses would have been caught by doing step 1. The trick is to ASK the question before you pick.

Which Sentence Belongs? — Paragraph Logic

Two misses on G8.1 — both would have been caught by one strategy

Shepperd, questions 7–9 on the test often ask: "Which sentence best supports the paragraph's point?" or "Which sentence does NOT belong?" You missed two of these on G8.1 alone. Here they are.

From G8.1 Q7
Which sentence best supports the claim: "The library improved ACCESS for many residents"?
Your pick: "Workshops taught skills to residents."
Correct: A sentence about hours, locations, or services being available to people.
Why: The topic word is ACCESS (can people get to it?). Workshops teaching skills are about programs, not access. Those are different topics. The workshops option belongs in a paragraph about programs, not a paragraph about access.
From G8.1 Q9
Which sentence does NOT belong in a paragraph about the library's PROGRAMS?
Your pick: "Films were shown on the deck."
Correct: A sentence that is about something else entirely (building architecture, funding, etc.)
Why: Films shown on the deck ARE programs! The question is asking which sentence does NOT belong. You picked one that DOES belong. Double-check: does the question say "supports" or "does NOT belong"?
3-STEP PARAGRAPH-LOGIC STRATEGY
1. Underline the topic word in the question (access, programs, funding, etc.). That single word is the filter.

2. For each answer option, ask: "Is this sentence ABOUT that exact word?" Similar is not the same: "programs" and "access" are different. "Workshops" and "films" are both programs.

3. Double-check whether the question asks for what DOES belong or what does NOT belong. This is where Q9 got you.
Which sentence best supports: "Community gardens improved NUTRITION for neighborhood residents"?
A Neighbors got to know each other while tending plots together.
B Families reported eating more fresh vegetables each week after the gardens opened.
C The program was funded by a grant from the city council.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q7 and Q9 show up on every test as "which sentence" questions. Underlining the topic word — access, nutrition, programs — is a 3-second habit that would have caught both of your G8.1 misses.
