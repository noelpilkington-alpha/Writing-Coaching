Write Complete Sentences

Every sentence needs a subject + verb + finished thought

On several questions, you wrote fragments — pieces of sentences that don't stand on their own. Let's look at the pattern and fix it together:

THE COMPLETE SENTENCE CHECK
Before you move on, ask yourself three questions:

1. Does it start with a capital letter?
2. Does it end with a period, question mark, or exclamation point?
3. Does it make sense on its own — could someone understand it without reading anything else?

If any answer is "no," it's not a complete sentence yet. Keep going!

Fill in the Blank = ONE Word

Q5 — Read the directions carefully

Q5 gave you two sentences and asked you to fill in the blank with one connecting word:

The Question
Anna held the seal gently. ______, Jim untangled the net.
YOU WROTE
A whole new sentence instead of one word

The question only wanted one word to go in the blank — a word that connects the two sentences. You wrote a completely new sentence instead.

CORRECT ANSWER
Anna held the seal gently. Meanwhile, Jim untangled the net.

Words that work here: Meanwhile, Then, Next.

HOW TO HANDLE FILL-IN-THE-BLANK
Step 1: Read the sentences around the blank.
Step 2: Count how many words they want. (Usually one!)
Step 3: Try a connecting word: Meanwhile, Then, However, Also, Next, Finally.
Step 4: Read the whole thing out loud to see if it sounds right.

This is a quick win — once you know what the question is asking, you can get it right every time.

Q2 — Expand the sentence with details

Q3 — Add an appositive

YOU WROTE
Sally swam to the other seals to find his Mom.

Good start! Two things to fix: Sally is a girl, so it should be "her" not "his." And the question asked for an appositive — a short description tucked inside the sentence with commas:

WITH AN APPOSITIVE
Sally, the baby seal, swam to the other seals to find her mom.
THE APPOSITIVE PATTERN
Name, [short description], rest of sentence.

Think of it like a whisper to the reader: "Sally — oh, by the way, she's the baby seal — swam to the other seals."

More examples:
• Anna, the oldest child, spotted the seal first.
• The fishnet, a tangled mess, was wrapped around the pup.

Your Paragraph: Answer the Prompt

Write about what the question asks

Your paragraph scored 2/20. The main reason is that you wrote about something different from what the prompt asked.

The Prompt Asked
Explain how Satoshi Tajiri's childhood influenced the creation of Pokémon.
YOU WROTE (SHORTENED)
"I will reveal it. I'll give it one out of five poké balls this is my explanation what is very cringe..."

You clearly know a lot about Pokémon, and that is awesome! But the prompt asked you to explain how Tajiri's childhood led to Pokémon — using details from the article you read. Writing about your own opinion of Pokémon, or rating it, doesn't answer the question.

Here is what the paragraph needed to look like:

Paragraph Plan — "How Tajiri's childhood influenced Pokémon"
TS = Topic Sentence  •  D = Detail from the article  •  CS = Concluding Sentence
TS Satoshi Tajiri's love of catching bugs as a kid led him to create Pokémon.
D1 As a child, Tajiri spent hours catching bugs — friends called him "Dr. Bug."
D2 When he grew up, he remembered catching bugs and used that idea for a video game.
D3 Pokémon became huge — over 150 characters, TV shows, movies, and apps.
CS Tajiri's childhood hobby turned into one of the most popular games in the world.
THE #1 RULE FOR PARAGRAPHS
Read the prompt. Answer the prompt.

Before you start writing, ask yourself: "What is the question asking me to explain?" Then make sure every sentence in your paragraph helps answer that question. Your own opinions are great — but only include them if the prompt asks for them.

You know a lot about Pokémon. Next time, use that knowledge together with the article details to answer what the prompt asks.

Your Proofreading Checklist

Catch small mistakes before you turn in your test

Several of your answers lost points because of small mistakes that you can learn to catch. Here is your personal checklist:

Start every sentence with a capital letter.
On Q1 you wrote "why" instead of "Why." On Q9 you started with a lowercase letter. Always check your first letter.
End every sentence with punctuation.
Several of your answers are missing periods or question marks at the end. When you finish writing, go back and check that every sentence has an ending mark.
Check your spelling — especially names.
You wrote "Shally" instead of Sally and "thay" instead of they. The character names are in the passage — look back and double-check them.
Check verb forms: "did" + base verb.
On Q1 you wrote "did Anna named" — after "did," use the base form: "did Anna name." This is a common mistake and easy to fix once you know the pattern.
Don't smash two sentences together (run-ons).
On Q4 you joined ideas without a comma or connecting word. If you have two ideas, either use a period between them or connect them with ", and" or ", so."
