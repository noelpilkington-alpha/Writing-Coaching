#1 Focus: An Appositive Renames the Noun

Question 3 — combining two sentences with an appositive

Question 3 asked you to combine these two sentences using an appositive:

Sentence 1: Porcupines are rodents.
Sentence 2: They are the slow movers of the forest.
YOU WROTE (G3.6 Q3 — 1 out of 2)
"porcupines are rodents, beautiful creatures, but are the slow movers of they forest."
You put a phrase between commas — good instinct! But "beautiful creatures" came from your imagination, not from sentence 2. And "the slow movers of the forest" got tacked on the end with "but are" instead of becoming the appositive.
THE RULE
An appositive renames the noun right before it — using the other sentence's words — and sits between commas.

Step 1: Find the noun both sentences talk about: porcupines.
Step 2: Take the describing part of the other sentence: the slow movers of the forest.
Step 3: Tuck it right after the noun, between commas.

The test: if you delete the appositive, the sentence should still make sense on its own.
THE COMBINE THAT EARNS 2 OUT OF 2
"Porcupines, the slow movers of the forest, are rodents."
Delete the middle part and you still have "Porcupines are rodents." That is how you know the appositive is doing its job.
Combine with an appositive: "The porcupine is a rodent." + "It is one of the slowest animals in the forest." Which answer does it right?
A The porcupine, one of the slowest animals in the forest, is a rodent.
B The porcupine is a rodent, an amazing animal, but is one of the slowest animals in the forest.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
You already use commas around a middle phrase — the shape is there. The fix is what goes inside: the other sentence's noun phrase, not a new phrase you invent. Get that right and Q3 goes from 1 point to 2, every test.

#2 Focus: Finish the Sentence Right

Two small endings cost 2 points on G3.6

Fila, two answers on this test were almost perfect — the ideas and the wording were right. Each one lost a point at the very end of the sentence.

Ending #1: a question must end with a question mark. Q4 asked you to rewrite a statement as a question, and your question wording was spot on:

YOU WROTE (G3.6 Q4 — 1 out of 2)
"Do sharp quills protect them from danger."
A real question — ending in a period. That one tiny mark cost the point.
THE FIX
"Do these sharp quills protect them from danger?"

Ending #2: a comma cannot join two complete thoughts. On Q9, you wrote two strong complete thoughts — and glued them together with only a comma:

YOU WROTE (G3.6 Q9 — 2 out of 3)
"The porcupine is a special animal because of its quills, it's so amazing how it can easily poke the human skin."
Each half could stand alone as its own sentence — so a comma alone is not strong enough to hold them together.
TWO WAYS TO FIX IT
1. Period + new sentence: "The porcupine is a special animal because of its quills. It's so amazing how it can easily poke the human skin."
2. Add a connector: "The porcupine is a special animal because of its quills, and it's so amazing how it can easily poke the human skin."
Which one finishes the sentence right?
A Do porcupines climb trees to stay away from predators.
B Do porcupines climb trees to stay away from predators?
THE ENDING CHECK
On your read-back, look at how every sentence ends:

1. If it asks something, it ends with ? — no exceptions.
2. If a comma sits between two complete thoughts, replace it with a period and a new sentence, or add a connector like and, so, or because.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
These two endings cost you 2 points on G3.6 — and the writing in both answers was already there. These are the easiest points on the whole test to win back.
