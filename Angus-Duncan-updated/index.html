KEY SKILL: MATCH YOUR ANSWER TO THE QUESTION
Before you write, underline the key word in the question (safe, discovery, change). Then make sure your answer uses that exact idea. If the question says "safe," your answer should be about safety — not food, not fun, not care in general.
Why does this matter for paragraphs too?
The same skill applies to paragraph writing. When a question asks you to explain how something happened, your paragraph needs to focus on the how — not just state what happened. Matching your answer to the question is the foundation of strong writing at every level.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
When your answer does not match the question, you can lose points even if what you wrote is true. In paragraph writing, staying focused on the question is what separates a good paragraph from a great one.

Quick Fix — "has" vs. "as"

Q8 — Can you spot the small word that changes the meaning?

Your answer for Question 8 had a small but important mix-up. Read both versions carefully. Which one makes sense?

Tap the sentence where the meaning is correct:
A "A puppy starts off very small, but has it grows it becomes bigger."
B "A puppy starts off very small, but as it grows, its legs get longer and its bark gets louder."

Here is what you wrote:

YOUR ANSWER (Q8)
"A puppy starts off very small, but has it grows it becomes bigger."

"Has" means to possess something. "As" means "while" or "during." One letter changes the entire meaning of the sentence.

The sentence could also be more specific. Instead of just saying it "becomes bigger," describe how the change happens.

Tap to see the fixed version
Read the explanation above first
FIXED
"A puppy starts off very small, but as it grows, its legs get longer and its bark gets louder."
PROOFREADING TIP
After you finish writing, read your sentence slowly, word by word. Small words like "has," "as," "is," and "it" are easy to mix up when you write quickly. Reading slowly catches these before they cost points.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Small word mix-ups can completely change what your sentence means. In a paragraph, one confusing sentence can make the whole paragraph harder to follow. Proofreading is what separates good writers from great ones.

Punctuation Check — Two Complete Thoughts

Q9 — Can you see where the break should go?

Your answer for Question 9 joins two complete thoughts together. Which version uses the right punctuation?

Tap the version with correct punctuation:
A "One time I asked for a pet monkey my parents just laughed and said no."
B "One time I asked for a pet monkey, and my parents just laughed and said no."

Here is what you wrote:

YOUR ANSWER (Q9)
"One time I asked for a pet monkey my parents just laughed and said no."

This is actually two complete thoughts joined without any punctuation:

  • Thought 1: "One time I asked for a pet monkey"
  • Thought 2: "my parents just laughed and said no"

Each side can stand alone as a sentence. When that happens, you need a comma + joining word to connect them.

Tap to see the fixed version
Read the explanation above first
FIXED
"One time I asked for a pet monkey, and my parents just laughed and said no."
RULE: JOINING TWO COMPLETE THOUGHTS
When two complete thoughts are joined in one sentence, use a comma + joining word (and, but, so, or, yet). Cover the middle — if each side makes sense on its own, you need that comma + joining word.
See another example
Wrong: "I wanted to go outside it started raining."
Right: "I wanted to go outside, but it started raining."

Both sides can stand alone, so you need a comma + joining word in the middle.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
In paragraph writing, you often connect ideas across sentences. Getting the punctuation right between complete thoughts makes your writing clearer and easier to follow — and helps you earn full points.

Paragraph Plan — Topic Sentence, Details, Why It Matters, Closing Sentence
Topic Sentence Yacouba saved many villages with his idea.
