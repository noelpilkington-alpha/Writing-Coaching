Write a Thesis, Not a Roadmap

The single rewrite that moves the essay from 15 to 19

Aileya, here is the opening sentence of your G7.2 essay. It is the first thing the grader reads — and it is doing something specific that caps your score.

YOUR ACTUAL INTRO (G7.2)
"Have you wondered, how is adding a bright green lane useful? I will be discussing what caused it, why they were installed, the concerns, and the benefits!"

The hook question is great — graders love that. The problem is the second sentence. It is a roadmap: it tells the reader what you are going to talk about. A G7 essay needs a thesis: a claim you will then defend with evidence.

Roadmap (what you wrote)
"I will be discussing what caused it, why they were installed, the concerns, and the benefits!"
Lists topics. No position. Reader does not know what you think.
Thesis (what earns 19–20)
"Although residents worried about losing parking, the bike lane project made the streets safer, helped local shops, and gave families a calmer evening."
Makes a claim. Acknowledges the concern. Previews three reasons you will defend.
THE THESIS FORMULA
A G7 thesis does three things in one sentence:
1. Names the topic (the bike lane project)
2. Makes a claim about it (it was successful / it was worth the cost / etc.)
3. Previews 2–3 reasons the body paragraphs will prove

Test it: if your sentence starts with "I will be discussing..." or "In this essay...", rewrite it. Cut the "I will" and state the claim directly.
Which sentence is a thesis (not a roadmap)?
A In this essay I will explain the reasons for the bike lane, the problems people had with it, and the good things that happened afterward.
B Despite early worries about lost parking, the bike lane project succeeded because it reduced accidents, brought more customers to local shops, and made evening streets feel safer for families.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Your essay already has the three body paragraphs that match a real thesis — reasons, concerns/solutions, benefits. You are doing the hard work in the body. The intro just needs to promise the claim the body already proves. That rewrite alone is worth +2 to +3 points on the 20-point essay. 15/20 becomes 18–19/20.

Q7: Matching Evidence to the Exact Topic

The one multiple-choice question you missed — same skill family

Aileya, Q7 asked which sentence best supports a paragraph topic about drivers and riders sharing the road. Here is how you chose:

YOUR ANSWER (Q7)
"Parents said the streets felt calmer in the evening."
CORRECT ANSWER (Q7)
"Volunteers put up clear signs about the rules for bikes and cars."

Both sentences are positive, and both are from the same passage. The trap is that the one you picked sounds pleasant and related — calmer evenings, happy families. But the topic was not "neighborhood pleasantness." The topic was how drivers and riders share the road.

🧩
"Sharing the road" is a coordination topic, not a mood topic. It is about rules, signs, lanes — the stuff that lets drivers and bikers use the same space without crashing. A sentence about evening calm supports a different topic (family life, quality of life). Signs about bike-and-car rules is a direct hit.
A paragraph's topic sentence is: "Local businesses benefited from the new bike lane." Which sentence best supports that topic?
A After the lanes opened, many more children felt safe biking to school.
B Shop owners on Maple Street said their weekend sales grew by 20% once bikers began stopping in for coffee.
THE "EXACT TOPIC" CHECK
Before you pick a supporting detail, do this:

1. Underline the topic sentence's key phrase (for Q7: "sharing the road").
2. Ask: is this phrase about safety? Rules/coordination? Mood/feel? Money? Name the category.
3. Pick the answer that matches that category, not the one that is most pleasant to read.

Tonally nice sentences are bait. Category-matching sentences are points.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
This is the same skill as the thesis card. Both are about matching the right evidence to the right claim. Fix the thesis in the essay, sharpen the category-check on multiple choice, and the whole test gets tighter. One missed multiple choice = +3.3% on the overall score.

Your Q11 Essay: What Works, What to Polish

The path from 15/20 to 19 or 20

Here is the shape of your G7.2 essay with what graders reward and what they marked down:

Intro: "Have you wondered, how is adding a bright green lane useful? I will be discussing what caused it, why they were installed, the concerns, and the benefits!"

Body 1 (causes/reasons): Explained why the bike lanes were installed, using details from the passage.

Body 2 (concerns): Named the residents' concerns (parking, cost) and the solutions volunterrs made avaliable.

Body 3 (benefits): Explained the benefits. Included: "the streets felt much more calmer" and mentioned that the project lead to safer roads.

Conclusion: Tied the essay together and re-stated the bike lane's importance.

✓ Rhetorical question hook — "Have you wondered..." pulls the reader in. G7 graders give credit for voice.
✓ Full five-paragraph structure — intro, three bodies, conclusion. Every rubric row gets credit for this.
✓ Specific details from the passage in every body paragraph, not just general statements.
⚠ Intro is a roadmap, not a thesis — the second sentence lists topics instead of stating a claim. Single biggest score lift.
⚠ Double comparative — "streets felt much more calmer" is either "more calm" or "calmer", not both.
⚠ Spelling — "avaliable" → available, "volunterrs" → volunteers, "lead" → led (past tense).
Reveal the 19/20 polished version
Same essay, sharper intro, fixed polish slips

Intro (rewritten): "Have you wondered how a bright green stripe on the road could change a whole neighborhood? Although some residents worried about losing parking, the bike lane project made the streets safer, helped local shops, and gave families calmer evenings."

Body 1 (causes/reasons): Why the lanes were installed — unchanged, still strong.

Body 2 (concerns): Residents' concerns and the solutions volunteers made available. (spelling fixed)

Body 3 (benefits): The streets felt much calmer, and the project led to safer roads. (double comparative + "lead→led" fixed)

Conclusion: Unchanged — already strong.

THE JUMP FROM 15/20 TO 19 OR 20
Notice what did not change: three body paragraphs, specific evidence, transitions, conclusion, voice. What changed: the intro now claims something (the project succeeded for three reasons) instead of listing what the essay will cover. Four spelling/word-choice fixes. That is a 4–5 point lift, not a rewrite.
