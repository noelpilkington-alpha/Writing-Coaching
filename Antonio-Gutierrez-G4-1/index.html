Your 6-Step Writing Process

The same routine for every question on the test

Antonio, the best writers do not just start typing. They follow a routine on every question. Here is the one to use — pay special attention to Step 2, because that is the step that would have boosted your paragraph the most.

1
Read the passage and the question carefully, all the way through.
2
Pin down exactly what the prompt asks. Say it back in your own words. Is it asking you to retell what happened, or to explain how someone's opinion changed? Answer THAT.
3
Plan your answer. For the paragraph, type a quick rough outline in the box first, then build your real answer on top of it.
4
Draft your answer in complete sentences. One complete idea per sentence.
5
Revise — reread the question, then your answer. Did you answer the EXACT question? Add any missing piece.
6
Check spelling and sentence breaks. Make sure no two complete thoughts are joined with only a comma.
WHY STEP 2 MATTERS MOST FOR YOU

Answer the Exact Question

Your biggest opportunity on the paragraph (Question 11)

Antonio, the G4.1 paragraph asked about the story "Surprise Kick": how do the events change Cody's opinion? You wrote a clear, accurate retell of what happened — but the retell centered on Mud, not on Cody's change of opinion. Here is what you wrote:

YOU WROTE (G4.1 Q11)
"He thought that mud would lose control immediatley but he didn't. Mud ran past defenders then he shot and scored."

That is a good summary of the plot. But the question was not "what did Mud do?" It was "how does Cody's opinion change?" The paragraph needs to name Cody's view at three moments:

Answering a "How Does Their Opinion Change?" Prompt
BEFORE What did Cody think at the start? (He doubted Mud.)
TURN What event changed his mind? (Mud got the ball and scored.)
AFTER What does Cody think now? (He sees Mud as a real player.)
The prompt asks how the events change Cody's opinion. Which sentence answers THAT question?
A Mud ran past the defenders and then he shot the ball and scored a goal.
B At first Cody doubted that Mud could play, but after Mud scored, Cody saw him as a real player.

MODEL ANSWER (answers the exact prompt)

The events change Cody's opinion of Mud from doubt to respect. At first, Cody did not think Mud could handle the ball and expected him to lose control right away. The turning point came when Mud got the pass, ran past the defenders, and scored. After that play, Cody changed his mind and saw Mud as a real player who belonged on the field. By the end, Cody respects a player he once doubted.

THE HABIT
When a prompt asks how someone's opinion changes, your paragraph must name their view before, the event that changed it, and their view after. Retelling the plot is not enough — tie every detail back to the person whose opinion changed.

A Comma Cannot Join Two Sentences

The same small slip showed up on Q1 and Q6

Antonio, twice on G4.1 you joined two complete sentences with only a comma. Each half could stand on its own — so a comma is not strong enough to hold them together. Here are both spots:

FROM G4.1 Q1
"In this event, Science teacher Peter Tabichi received an award, he won the prestigious Global Teacher Prize."
Two complete sentences joined by just a comma.
FROM G4.1 Q6
"I can not pass the typing check, I have to type 35 words per minute."
Again, two complete thoughts held together with only a comma.
TWO WAYS TO FIX A COMMA SPLICE
1. Use a period. End the first sentence, then start a new one.
"...received an award. He won the prestigious Global Teacher Prize."

2. Add a joining word after the comma: and, but, so, because.
"I can not pass the typing check, because I have to type 35 words per minute."
Which version is written correctly?
A I can not pass the typing check, I have to type 35 words per minute.
B I can not pass the typing check because I have to type 35 words per minute.
THE QUICK TEST
Before a comma, read what comes after it. If that part could stand alone as its own sentence, a comma is not enough. Use a period to make two sentences, or add a joining word (and, but, so, because) right after the comma.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
These were close calls — you got 1 of 2 on Q1 and 2 of 3 on Q6. Catching comma splices on your final check is worth a point or two on the short-answer section every test, and it is one of the easiest points to win back.
