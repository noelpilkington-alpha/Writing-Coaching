Step 5 Error: Missing Comma (Q1)

Your appositive was good but missing its closing comma

Reed, on Q1 you wrote an appositive correctly — that is a strong skill. But you missed the closing comma that ends the appositive phrase, which cost you half a point.

Q1 — YOUR ANSWER (1 out of 2)
"A colony, a large group, has one leader the queen bee."

What is right: "a large group" is a perfect appositive. The first comma is there. The meaning is clear.

What is missing: A comma before "the queen bee" to set off this second renaming phrase, OR a clearer structure that separates the two ideas.

TWO WAYS TO FIX IT
Option 1: "A colony, a large group, has one leader: the queen bee."
Option 2: "A colony, a large group of bees, has one leader, the queen bee."
THE APPOSITIVE COMMA RULE
An appositive needs a comma before it starts AND a comma after it ends (unless it is at the very end of the sentence, where a period closes it).

• "The queen bee, the colony's leader, lays all the eggs." → Two commas around "the colony's leader"
• "The colony is led by the queen bee, the only one who lays eggs." → One comma, then the period closes it
Which sentence has the appositive punctuated correctly?
A The mighty oak, a tall and strong tree provides shade for the entire park.
B The mighty oak, a tall and strong tree, provides shade for the entire park.
YOUR STEP 5 CHECK
After writing any sentence with an appositive, ask: "Did I put a comma at the start AND end of the extra information?" If the appositive is not at the end of the sentence, it needs two commas. This single check earns back the half-point you lost on Q1.

Step 5 Error: Run-On Sentence (Q3)

"then" needs a conjunction or period before it

Reed, on Q3 you combined two ideas using "then" but forgot to add a proper connector before it. The word "then" is not a conjunction by itself. It needs "and" before it, or a period to start a new sentence.

Q3 — YOUR ANSWER (1.5 out of 2)
You wrote two actions connected with just "then" — which creates a run-on. It should be "and then" or split into two sentences.
THE RULE: "THEN" IS NOT A CONJUNCTION
"Then" tells you when something happened, but it does not join sentences. To join two complete actions:

• Use "and then": "The bees collected nectar and then flew back to the hive."
• Use a period: "The bees collected nectar. Then they flew back to the hive."

Without "and" or a period, you get a run-on: "The bees collected nectar then flew back" — two complete actions with no proper connector.
Which sentence correctly connects two actions?
A The worker bees gathered pollen from the flowers then brought it back to the hive.
B The worker bees gathered pollen from the flowers and then brought it back to the hive.
YOUR STEP 5 CHECK
When proofreading, look for "then" in your sentences. Ask: "Is there an 'and' before it, or did I start a new sentence?" If neither, add "and" before "then." This is a quick fix that costs you half a point when missed.

Your Paragraph Needs More D+E

16/20 → 18+ means explaining what your evidence means

Reed, your paragraph earned 16 out of 20 on Q11 about "The Mighty Oak Tree." Two things held it back: a repeated word ("by" used twice in a row) and not enough development and explanation after your examples.

REPEATED WORD ISSUE
You used "by" back-to-back in your paragraph, which made the sentence awkward. A quick proofread catches repetition like this.
THE D+E PATTERN
After you state a fact or give an example, add:

D (Development): Explain what the example means in your own words.
E (Explanation): Connect it back to your topic sentence. Why does this example matter?

Without D+E: "The oak tree provides shade for animals." (states a fact, stops)
With D+E: "The oak tree provides shade for animals during hot summer days. This means the tree acts like a natural shelter, keeping smaller creatures cool and safe. This shows how one tree can support an entire community of living things."
