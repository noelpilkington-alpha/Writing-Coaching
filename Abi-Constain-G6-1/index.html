The #1 Unlock: Build a 5-Paragraph Essay

The prompt asked for five paragraphs — you wrote one block

Abi, the essay rubric asked for five clear paragraphs: an introduction with a thesis, three body paragraphs each with a topic sentence, and a conclusion. Your essay had all the right facts, but they ran together as one long block with no breaks and no thesis up front. It opened with a bare fact ("Wilma Rudolph was born in 1940") instead of a claim about perseverance. Giving your facts this shape is the single biggest unlock.

The 5-paragraph essay skeleton
INTRO Introduction + thesis — open with a claim that answers the prompt and previews your three points.
BODY 1 Topic sentence + evidence + explanation — illness and recovery (polio, braces, daily therapy).
BODY 2 Topic sentence + evidence + explanation — training and the Olympics (basketball, bronze, three golds).
BODY 3 Topic sentence + evidence + explanation — what we learn and her legacy (becoming an educator and coach, helping kids).
CLOSE Conclusion — restate your thesis and end with the big takeaway.
YOUR THESIS, READY TO USE
A thesis is a claim that answers the prompt. For this essay it could be:

"Wilma Rudolph showed perseverance by overcoming illness, training relentlessly, and inspiring others — and her story teaches us that determination can beat long odds."

Notice it previews your three body paragraphs (illness, training, inspiring others). That is the whole intro doing its job.
Which sentence is a real thesis to open the essay, and which is just a bare fact?
A "Wilma Rudolph showed perseverance by working through sickness, training hard, and reaching big goals."
B "She got polio and had to wear leg braces."
YOUR ACTION STEP
Before you write, type a quick outline in the response box: thesis, then three body points, then a conclusion idea. Once the five-paragraph plan is there, you fill it in with the facts you already know. The structure does the heavy lifting.

From Retelling to Explaining

An expository essay needs evidence AND "what this shows"

Abi, your essay is full of accurate facts — that is a real strength. But most of it retells what happened in order, like a story. An expository essay needs each fact PLUS a sentence explaining what it shows about perseverance. You name the facts; you just rarely explain what each one proves. Watch how small the upgrade is:

YOU WROTE (FACT ONLY)
"She did therapy every day."
FACT + EXPLANATION
"She did therapy every day. This shows that perseverance means doing the hard work over and over, even when results come slowly."
YOU WROTE (FACT ONLY)
"She even raced when she was hurt."
FACT + EXPLANATION
"She even raced when she was hurt. This shows she refused to let pain stop her from reaching her goals."
THE PATTERN: FACT → EXPLANATION
After each fact from the passage, add one sentence that answers:

• "This shows that..." (what the fact reveals)
• "This matters because..." (why it counts)

You already have all the facts. The explanation sentence is what turns a retelling into a real expository essay.
Which choice would earn more essay points: a fact alone, or a fact plus an explanation?
A "At age twelve she took off her brace. This shows that years of effort finally paid off and that she never gave up on walking."
B "At age twelve she took off her brace."
YOUR ACTION STEP
In your next essay, after you write a fact from the passage, re-read it and ask: "What does this show about my point?" Then write that sentence. One "This shows..." sentence per fact is where most of your missing points live.

YOUR STORY, RESTRUCTURED (5 PARAGRAPHS)
Intro: Wilma Rudolph showed perseverance by overcoming serious illness, training relentlessly, and inspiring others — and her story teaches us that determination can beat long odds.

Body 1: Wilma's perseverance began with her health. Born in 1940, she got polio and had to wear leg braces, and doctors said she may never walk again. But she never quit and did therapy every day. This shows that perseverance means doing the hard work over and over, even when progress is slow. At age twelve, she finally took off her brace.

Body 2: She then turned that determination toward sports. She started playing basketball to get stronger, and a coach saw her speed and skills. She made the Olympics at sixteen and won a bronze medal in 1960, then won three gold medals in Rome — the first American woman to do that. This shows that years of relentless effort can lead to goals that once seemed impossible.

Body 3: Her story also teaches us how to live. We learn three things from her: work hard, take small steps, and help others. She became an educator and coach who helped many kids. This matters because it shows perseverance is not just about winning — it is about lifting up the people who come after you.

Conclusion: Wilma worked through sickness and fear, made big goals, and reached them, even racing when she was hurt. Her story shows that with perseverance, we can beat tough odds.
SO WHAT? WHERE YOUR POINTS LIVE
Notice the rebuilt essay uses your exact facts — nothing was added that you did not already know. It just added a thesis, paragraph breaks, and "this shows" sentences, and fixed "perseverance" and the run-on list. Structure and explanation are where most of your 8 lost points live.
