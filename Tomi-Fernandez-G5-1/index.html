THE 20-SECOND READ-BACK
Before you hit submit, do this on every written answer:

1. Read each sentence aloud in your head, slowly. Your ear catches dropped words.
2. Scan for capital letters in the middle of sentences. They should not be there unless it's a name.
3. Check the first word is capitalized and the sentence ends with punctuation.

This takes about 20 seconds per answer. On G5.1 it would have won back roughly 2 full points — that's the gap between 87% and a higher score.
WHY SLOW READ-BACK WORKS
When you write, your brain knows what you meant to write, so it auto-fills gaps. Reading out loud (even silently in your head) forces you to process the actual words on the page, not the version in your head. That is how "if she did do the" slips past — your brain filled in the missing word without telling you.

Your #1 Recurring Error: Comma Splices

G5.1 Q3 — joining two complete sentences with just a comma
🔍
What is a comma splice? When you take two complete sentences and glue them together with just a comma (no "and," "but," "so," or "because"). The fix is easy once you spot it — but you have to spot it first.

Tomi, on Q3 you were asked to combine "Crista stood up straight" and "Her backpack knocked Ms. Kessler's coffee mug onto the floor." You got the right idea — you put both ideas together — but you joined two complete sentences with just a comma. Each side of that comma is a full sentence with its own subject and verb. That's a comma splice.

G5.1 Q3 — COMBINE SENTENCES (SCORED 0.5/2)
You wrote: "I stood up straight, my backpack knocked Ms. Kessler's coffee mug onto the floor."
Left side: "I stood up straight" — subject ("I"), verb ("stood") — complete sentence. Right side: "my backpack knocked Ms. Kessler's coffee mug onto the floor" — subject ("my backpack"), verb ("knocked") — complete sentence. Two complete sentences joined by just a comma = comma splice.

There are two clean fixes. Both work, and both earn full credit:

FIX OPTION 1: ADD A CONJUNCTION
✓ "I stood up straight, and my backpack knocked Ms. Kessler's coffee mug onto the floor."
✓ "When I stood up straight, my backpack knocked Ms. Kessler's coffee mug onto the floor."
✓ "I stood up straight so fast that my backpack knocked Ms. Kessler's coffee mug onto the floor."

The conjunction (and / but / so / because / when) tells the reader how the two ideas connect.
FIX OPTION 2: MAKE THEM TWO SENTENCES
✓ "I stood up straight. My backpack knocked Ms. Kessler's coffee mug onto the floor."

A period + capital letter is always a safe move when you're not sure how to connect two complete thoughts.
G5.1 Q3 — which revision actually fixes the comma splice?
A I stood up very straight, my backpack knocked the coffee mug to the floor.
B I stood up straight, and my backpack knocked the coffee mug to the floor.
THE COMMA SPLICE TEST
Before you submit any combined sentence, do this two-step check:

1. Find the comma.
2. Ask: is what's on the left of the comma a complete sentence? Is what's on the right a complete sentence?
3. If both are complete sentences, you need either a conjunction (and/but/so/because/when) OR a period + new sentence.

✗ "I stood up straight, my backpack knocked..." ← both sides complete → splice
✓ "I stood up straight, and my backpack knocked..." ← conjunction added → fixed
✓ "I stood up straight. My backpack knocked..." ← two sentences → fixed
WHEN A COMMA ALONE IS FINE
Commas work fine when what follows isn't a full sentence — like a phrase, a list, or an appositive:

✓ "I stood up straight, knocking the coffee mug over." ← "knocking..." isn't a full sentence
✓ "Ms. Kessler, a kind teacher, was not at her desk." ← appositive phrase
✓ "I grabbed my pencil, eraser, and notebook." ← list

The rule only applies when both sides of the comma could stand alone as full sentences.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q3 was worth 2 points and you earned 0.5 — that's 1.5 points on the table from one structural slip. On a 25-point short-answer section, 1.5 points is 6 percentage points on the section. Lock this pattern in and it stops happening.

G5.1 Q10 — VAGUE WORDING (SCORED 2.01/3)
You wrote: "I would tell them that it was me and help clean it up Because I am honest about those things."
"Those things" is doing a lot of vague work. What things? Honest about mistakes I made? Honest about breaking something? Naming the specific thing makes the answer score higher.
CLEANER Q10 — SPECIFIC, NOT VAGUE
✓ "I would tell them that it was me and help clean it up, because I am honest about mistakes I make."
✓ "I would tell them it was me and help clean it up, because owning up to what I broke is the honest thing to do."

Which version answers directly and specifically?

THE "NAME THE THING" RULE
Any time you catch yourself writing "those things," "that stuff," "it," or "something like that," stop and ask: "What thing specifically?" Then replace the vague word with the specific word.

Vague: "I am honest about those things."
Specific: "I am honest about mistakes I make."

Vague: "He did it because he was scared."
Specific: "He ran out of the room because he was scared."
ANSWER FIRST, EVIDENCE SECOND
On "How would X change?" prompts, skip rephrasing the question. Lead with your answer.

Don't: "I think it would have changed if Crista had cleaned up the mess instead of running into the cafeteria because..."
Do: "Ms. Kessler would have just thanked Crista instead of teaching her the lesson about owning up, because cleaning up the mess is the honest thing to do."

Same length, same facts — but the answer lands in the first sentence instead of the last.
SO WHAT? WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
Q8 and Q10 each lost about 1 point. That is 2 points on a 25-point section — roughly 8 percentage points. Naming the specific thing and answering directly is a free point every test.
